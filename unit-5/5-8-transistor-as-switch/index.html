TOPIC 5.8

Transistor as a Switch

Cutoff is OFF, saturation is ON, and the active region is only crossed on the way

The idea: ON and OFF

A switch has two states: OFF, where no current flows, and ON, where current flows with almost no voltage across the switch. A BJT can do the same job electronically, with no moving parts, millions of times per second.

  • OFF = CUTOFF: with the base voltage below about 0.5 V, IB ≈ 0 and IC ≈ 0. The full supply voltage appears across the transistor (VCE ≈ VCC), like an open switch.
  • ON = SATURATION: with enough base current, the transistor saturates. IC is limited only by the load, and VCE(sat) is only about 0.2 V, like a closed switch.

A switch circuit avoids the active region except for the brief moment of changing state, because in the active region both the voltage and the current are significant and the transistor wastes power as heat.

Key points

  • OFF = cutoff: IB ≈ 0, IC ≈ 0, VCE ≈ VCC (open switch).
  • ON = saturation: IC set by the load, VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V (closed switch).
  • No moving parts; it can switch millions of times per second.
  • The active region is only crossed while changing state, because it wastes power.

Designing a saturated switch

To be sure the transistor is fully ON, we design it to saturate even for the lowest β it might have.

  • Step 1. Find the ON collector current set by the load: IC(sat) = (VCC − VLOAD − VCE(sat)) / RC.
  • Step 2. Find the minimum base current needed: IB(min) = IC(sat) / β(min).
  • Step 3. Choose a larger base current for safety, usually 2 to 5 times IB(min). This extra drive is called the OVERDRIVE factor.
  • Step 4. Choose the base resistor: RB = (VIN − VBE(sat)) / IB, with VBE(sat) ≈ 0.8 V.

Worked example: drive an LED (forward voltage 2 V, about 20 mA) from VCC = 5 V, with the input coming from a 3.3 V logic output, using a transistor with β(min) = 100.

  • RC = (5 − 2 − 0.2) / 20 mA = 140 Ω → choose the standard value 150 Ω, so IC(sat) ≈ 18.7 mA.
  • IB(min) = 18.7 mA / 100 ≈ 0.19 mA.
  • With an overdrive of 5: IB ≈ 0.93 mA.
  • RB = (3.3 − 0.8) / 0.93 mA ≈ 2.7 kΩ.

Key points

  • IC(sat) = (VCC − VLOAD − VCE(sat))/RC, then IB(min) = IC(sat)/β(min).
  • Give 2 to 5 times IB(min) (overdrive) so it saturates even at the lowest β.
  • RB = (VIN − VBE(sat))/IB, with VBE(sat) ≈ 0.8 V.
  • LED example: RC = 150 Ω, IC(sat) ≈ 18.7 mA, IB ≈ 0.93 mA, RB ≈ 2.7 kΩ.

Switching inductive loads (relays and motors)

Relays, solenoids and motors are coils (inductors). An inductor resists sudden changes in current. When the transistor switches OFF, the coil current tries to keep flowing, and the coil produces a large voltage spike (V = L·di/dt) at the collector. This spike can exceed the transistor's breakdown voltage and destroy it.

The standard protection is a FLYBACK DIODE connected across the coil, reverse biased during normal operation (cathode to the positive supply). When the transistor turns off, the diode gives the coil current a safe path to circulate and decay, clamping the collector voltage to about one diode drop above VCC.

Key points

  • A coil resists sudden changes of current: turning it off gives a spike V = L·di/dt.
  • The spike can exceed the transistor's breakdown voltage and destroy it.
  • A flyback diode across the coil (cathode to +VCC) lets the current circulate and decay.
  • The collector is clamped to about one diode drop above VCC (≈ 12.7 V here).

Power in a switch

The power dissipated in the transistor is P = VCE × IC.

  • OFF: IC ≈ 0, so P ≈ 0.
  • ON: VCE ≈ 0.2 V, so P is small (for 18.7 mA, about 3.7 mW).
  • DURING SWITCHING: for a short time both VCE and IC are large, and the power peaks. This SWITCHING LOSS grows with how often the switch changes state (frequency) and how slowly it changes.

This is why fast edges matter and why switching circuits run cool, while a transistor left in the active region with a large load current can get hot.

Key points

  • P = VCE × IC: about 0 when OFF, small when ON (≈ 3.7 mW for 18.7 mA).
  • During each transition both are large, so power spikes: switching loss.
  • Switching loss rises with frequency and with slower edges.
  • A transistor left in the active region with a large current gets hot.

Switching times and speed

A transistor cannot switch instantly. When a pulse is applied to the base, the collector current changes after some delays:

  • DELAY TIME td: from the input step to IC reaching 10% of its final value (time to charge the junction capacitances).
  • RISE TIME tr: IC rising from 10% to 90%.
  • STORAGE TIME ts: after the input is removed, the time until IC falls to 90%. During saturation, extra charge is stored in the base, and it must be removed before the transistor can leave saturation.
  • FALL TIME tf: IC falling from 90% to 10%.

Turn-on time ton = td + tr. Turn-off time toff = ts + tf. Storage time is usually the longest, and it gets longer with more overdrive. Ways to reduce it include a SCHOTTKY CLAMP (a Schottky diode from base to collector that prevents deep saturation) and a speed-up capacitor across RB.

Key points

  • ton = td + tr; toff = ts + tf, measured at the 10% and 90% levels of IC.
  • Storage time comes from extra charge stored in the base during saturation.
  • ts is usually the longest and grows with overdrive.
  • A Schottky clamp or a speed-up capacitor across RB reduces ts.

Applications and summary

Transistor switches are everywhere: driving LEDs and displays from microcontrollers, switching relays and motors, controlling power with pulse-width modulation (PWM), and in logic gates. The simplest logic gate is the transistor INVERTER (NOT gate): a high input saturates the transistor and pulls the output low; a low input cuts it off and the output is pulled high through RC.

Summary: OFF = cutoff (IC ≈ 0, VCE ≈ VCC); ON = saturation (VCE ≈ 0.2 V). Design for saturation at minimum β with 2-5 times overdrive. Protect inductive loads with a flyback diode. Switching speed is limited mainly by storage time.

Key points

  • Uses: LEDs and displays, relays and motors, PWM power control, logic gates.
  • Inverter (NOT gate): high in saturates the transistor, output low; low in cuts it off, output high.
  • Design for saturation at minimum β with 2-5 times overdrive; flyback diodes on coils.
  • Switching speed is limited mainly by storage time.
