TOPIC 5.11

Punch-Through and Breakdown

What happens when a transistor is pushed past its voltage and power limits

Voltage limits of a transistor

Every transistor has maximum voltages it can withstand. Beyond them, the current rises sharply and is no longer controlled by the base. Unless the current is limited by the circuit, the transistor overheats and is destroyed.

There are two main ways a BJT can fail at high voltage:

  • PUNCH-THROUGH: the collector depletion region grows right across the base.
  • AVALANCHE BREAKDOWN: the electric field in a reverse-biased junction becomes strong enough to multiply carriers.

Datasheets list the safe limits as maximum ratings, for example VCBO (collector-base, emitter open), VCEO (collector-emitter, base open) and VEBO (emitter-base, collector open).

Key points

  • Above its maximum voltages, the current rises sharply and the base loses control.
  • Unless the circuit limits the current, the transistor overheats and is destroyed.
  • Two high-voltage failures: punch-through and avalanche breakdown.
  • Datasheet limits: VCBO, VCEO, VEBO, each with the third terminal open.

Punch-through (reach-through)

From the Early effect (topic 5.10) we know that increasing the collector-base reverse voltage widens the collector depletion region and makes the neutral base thinner.

If the voltage keeps increasing, the collector depletion region can widen all the way across the base until it touches the emitter-base depletion region. The neutral base width is now ZERO. This is PUNCH-THROUGH (also called reach-through).

The base can no longer control the current. The emitter-base barrier is lowered by the collector voltage itself, and a large current flows directly from the emitter to the collector, limited only by the external circuit.

Punch-through is most likely in transistors with a very thin or very lightly doped base. It usually happens at a lower voltage than avalanche breakdown only in such devices, and it is avoided by design through proper base width and doping.

Key points

  • Higher VCB widens the collector depletion region and thins the neutral base.
  • When it reaches the emitter-base depletion region, W = 0: punch-through.
  • The base loses control; a large E-to-C current flows, limited only by the circuit.
  • Avoided by design: enough base width and base doping.

Avalanche breakdown of the collector junction

In a reverse-biased collector-base junction, a strong electric field exists across the depletion region. Carriers crossing it are accelerated. If the field is strong enough, a carrier gains enough energy between collisions to knock an electron out of a covalent bond when it hits a silicon atom, creating a new electron-hole pair. This is IMPACT IONISATION.

The new carriers are accelerated too and create more pairs, so the number of carriers multiplies like an avalanche. The MULTIPLICATION FACTOR M (how many carriers come out for each one that enters) is often modelled as:

M = 1 / (1 − (V / BV)n)

where V is the reverse voltage, BV is the breakdown voltage and n is a constant, typically between 2 and 6. As V approaches BV, M rises toward infinity and the current rises sharply.

With the emitter open, this breakdown of the collector-base junction occurs at BVCBO, the highest voltage rating of the transistor.

Key points

  • A strong field lets carriers gain enough energy to make new electron-hole pairs: impact ionisation.
  • The new carriers do the same, so the number multiplies like an avalanche.
  • M = 1/(1 − (V/BV)n), n typically 2 to 6; M → ∞ as V → BV.
  • Emitter open, this sets BVCBO, the highest voltage rating.

Why BVCEO is lower than BVCBO

In the common emitter connection with the base open, breakdown happens at a LOWER voltage, BVCEO.

The reason is the transistor's own gain. Holes created by avalanche multiplication in the collector junction are swept into the base. With the base open they cannot leave, so they act exactly like a base current. The transistor amplifies this "base current" by β, producing more collector current, which passes through the high-field region and creates even more avalanche holes. This POSITIVE FEEDBACK makes the current run away at a voltage well below BVCBO.

A useful approximation is:

BVCEO ≈ BVCBO / β1/n

Example: BVCBO = 60 V, β = 100, n = 4 gives BVCEO ≈ 60 / 3.16 ≈ 19 V.

So in common emitter circuits, the collector-emitter voltage must stay below VCEO, which is always lower than VCBO on the datasheet.

Key points

  • Base open: avalanche holes stay in the base and act as base current.
  • The transistor multiplies them by β, making more avalanche: positive feedback.
  • BVCEO ≈ BVCBO/β1/n: 60 V, β = 100, n = 4 gives about 19 V.
  • In CE circuits keep VCE below VCEO, always lower than VCBO.

Emitter-base breakdown

The emitter-base junction is never reverse biased in normal active operation, but it can be in switching circuits or when a large negative signal reaches the base of an NPN.

Because the emitter is very heavily doped, the emitter-base depletion region is narrow and the field in it is very strong. As a result, it breaks down at a low reverse voltage, typically about 5 to 7 V for small-signal silicon transistors (the datasheet value VEBO). Exceeding it repeatedly can permanently reduce the transistor's β.

Protection: limit the reverse base-emitter voltage, for example with a diode across the base-emitter junction.

Key points

  • The EB junction is reverse biased only in switching or with large negative base signals.
  • Heavy emitter doping: narrow depletion region, very strong field.
  • It breaks down at about 5 to 7 V (VEBO); repeated breakdown can lower β for good.
  • Protect with a diode across B-E to clamp the reverse voltage.

Second breakdown and the safe operating area

In power transistors, a different failure can happen when HIGH VOLTAGE and HIGH CURRENT occur together. The current may concentrate in a small spot. That spot heats up, its current increases further (because hotter silicon conducts more), and it heats up even more: a local thermal runaway. The voltage suddenly collapses and the transistor is usually destroyed. This is SECOND BREAKDOWN.

To keep a transistor safe, manufacturers give a SAFE OPERATING AREA (SOA) graph of IC against VCE on log-log axes. The safe region is bounded by:

  • the maximum collector current IC(max);
  • the maximum power dissipation line, VCE × IC = P(max);
  • the second-breakdown limit at higher voltages;
  • the maximum voltage VCEO.

The operating point must stay inside this area at all times, including during switching. At higher temperatures the allowed power must be reduced (derated).

Key points

  • High voltage and high current together can concentrate current in a hot spot: second breakdown.
  • The SOA is bounded by IC(max), the power line, the second-breakdown limit and VCEO.
  • The operating point must stay inside at all times, including during switching.
  • At higher temperatures the power limit is derated.

Summary

Punch-through: the collector depletion region reaches the emitter depletion region, the base width becomes zero and the base loses control. Avalanche breakdown: impact ionisation multiplies carriers; it sets BVCBO. With the base open, transistor gain lowers this to BVCEO ≈ BVCBO / β1/n. The emitter-base junction breaks down at only about 5-7 V. Power transistors must stay inside the safe operating area to avoid second breakdown. Always keep voltages below the datasheet maximum ratings with a safety margin.

Key points

  • Punch-through: W reaches zero and the base loses control.
  • Avalanche sets BVCBO; with the base open, gain lowers it to BVCEO.
  • The EB junction breaks down at only about 5 to 7 V.
  • Stay inside the SOA and below all maximum ratings, with a safety margin.
