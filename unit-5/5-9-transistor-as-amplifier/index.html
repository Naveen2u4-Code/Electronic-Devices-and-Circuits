TOPIC 5.9

Transistor as an Amplifier

A small base signal controls a large collector current drawn from the supply

Your device asks for reduced motion, so every animation starts paused. Press Play on any animation to run it. Sliders, buttons and dragging still update the picture.

What is amplification?

An AMPLIFIER takes a small input signal, for example a few millivolts from a microphone, and produces a larger copy of it at the output, strong enough to drive a speaker or be processed further.

The transistor does not create energy. The extra power in the output signal comes from the DC power supply. The transistor acts like a valve: the small input signal controls how much current flows from the supply through the load. A small change at the base causes a large change in collector current, and that large current change through a resistor creates a large voltage change.

For this to work, the transistor must operate in the ACTIVE REGION the whole time, where IC is controlled smoothly by the input.

Key points

  • An amplifier makes a larger copy of a small input signal.
  • The extra output power comes from the DC supply, not from the transistor.
  • The transistor is a valve: a small base change controls a large collector current.
  • It must stay in the active region the whole time.

Why the transistor must be biased

An AC signal swings both positive and negative. But a silicon transistor only conducts usefully once VBE reaches roughly 0.6–0.7 V. (The collector current actually rises exponentially with VBE, so there is no sharp threshold. 0.7 V is simply a typical value at milliamp currents.) If we applied a small signal directly to the base with no DC voltage, the transistor would stay OFF for most of the cycle and the output would be badly distorted.

The solution is BIASING: we set a steady DC operating condition first, with a DC base current, a DC collector current and a DC collector voltage, so that the transistor sits in the middle of the active region even when no signal is present. This resting point is called the Q-POINT (quiescent point). The small signal is then added on top of it, making the operating point swing a little above and below the Q-point.

For the largest undistorted output swing, the Q-point is usually placed near the middle of the load line, with VCE roughly half of VCC. How to set and stabilise the Q-point is covered in Unit 6.

Key points

  • The base only conducts above about 0.7 V, so an unbiased AC input is mostly lost.
  • Biasing sets a DC Q-point in the active region before any signal arrives.
  • The signal swings the operating point above and below Q along the load line.
  • Q near the middle (VCE ≈ VCC/2) gives the largest clean swing.

The common emitter amplifier circuit

The standard single-transistor voltage amplifier is the VOLTAGE-DIVIDER BIASED COMMON EMITTER (CE) AMPLIFIER. It is called common emitter because the emitter is the terminal shared by input and output: the signal enters between base and ground, the output is taken between collector and ground, and the emitter is held at signal groundA node with no AC voltage on it. It may still carry a DC voltage. Here the bypass capacitor CE holds the emitter at a steady 1.3 V, so for the signal it behaves like ground. by the bypass capacitor CE.

Why the CE configuration is used for voltage amplification

  • Large voltage gain. |Av| ≈ gm(RC ∥ RL), typically tens to a few hundred (−170 for this circuit).
  • Current gain as well. The base current is about β times smaller than the collector current, so the CE stage has the highest power gain of the three configurations. Common base has a current gain of about 1 and common collector a voltage gain of about 1 (topics 5.4–5.6).
  • Convenient impedances. The input resistance (≈ R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ, kilohms) and the output resistance (≈ RC) suit cascading stages.
  • One supply, stable bias. The divider and RE give a Q-point that tolerates transistor-to-transistor β spread and temperature.
  • The costs: the output is inverted (180°), and the bandwidth is limited by the Miller effectA capacitance connected between the input and an inverting output of gain −A looks (1 + A) times larger from the input. In a CE stage the collector–base capacitance Cμ is multiplied this way, which lowers the upper cutoff frequency. (see Frequency response).

How it works, step by step

  1. The input signal vin passes through C1 and adds to the DC base voltage VB ≈ 2.0 V.
  2. CE holds the emitter at a steady VE ≈ 1.3 V, so practically the whole base signal appears across the base–emitter junction: vBE = VBE + vbe.
  3. The collector current depends exponentially on vBE. For a small signal its change is ic ≈ gm·vbe, which is the same thing as ib = vbe/rπ and ic = β·ib.
  4. The collector voltage is VC = VCC − iCRC, so when iC rises, VC falls, and when iC falls, VC rises.
  5. C2 passes only the AC part of VC to the load RL.

Because the collector voltage falls when the input rises, the output is INVERTED, a 180° phase shift between input and output. You can watch every step of this happen in Activity D.

Key points

  • R1, R2 set the DC base voltage; RE stabilises the Q-point; CE puts the emitter at signal ground.
  • C1 and C2 pass the signal and block DC; VCC supplies the energy; RL is what the amplifier drives.
  • vin → small change in vBE → gm times larger change in iC → large change in VC.
  • VC falls when the input rises: the output is inverted (180°).

3.1

Transistor Q1: the active amplifying device

The only part that can deliver more signal power than it receives.

1 · What is it?

An NPN bipolar junction transistor (a general-purpose small-signal type such as the 2N3904 or BC547 family). It has three terminals: base B (the control input), collector C (carries the controlled output current) and emitter E (the common return).

2 · Where is it connected?

B goes to the R1–R2 junction and C1. C goes to RC (up to VCC) and C2. E goes to RE ∥ CE (down to ground).

3 · Electrical function

In the forward-active regionThe operating region for amplification: base–emitter junction forward biased, collector–base junction reverse biased. Here iC is controlled by vBE and is nearly independent of vCE. it is a voltage-controlled current source: iC ≈ ISevBE/VT, almost independent of VCE. This needs:

  • BE junction forward biased: VBE ≈ 0.6–0.7 V.
  • CB junction reverse biased: VC > VB. In the example, VBC = 2.00 − 4.80 = −2.80 V.

4 · Why is it required?

Resistors and capacitors are passive: they can only lose signal power. The transistor lets a tiny input power control a much larger current drawn from VCC. The DC supply provides the output energy. The transistor only steers how much of it flows.

5 · Influence on DC operation

It draws a quiescent current ICQ that is set by the bias network. Its own parameters are poorly controlled: β can range from about 100 to 300 or more for one part number and rises with temperature, and VBE falls about 2 mV/°C. That is exactly why the divider and RE exist.

6 · Influence on AC operation

It converts vbe into ic = gmvbe. At the base it presents rπ = β/gm, and at the collector its output resistance is ro = VA/IC. The input only supplies the small power ib·vbe needed to drive the base.

7 · If it is removed

No collector current flows, so VC rises to VCC and nothing reaches the output. With no base current loading it, the divider gives VB = VTh = 2.105 V.

8 · If its parameters change

Higher β: with this bias design IC rises only about 4 % (1.29 → 1.34 mA for β = 100 → 200), so the bypassed gain −gmR barely moves. However, rπ = β/gm roughly doubles, which raises Rin. Lower VA (a stronger Early effect) means a smaller ro and slightly less gain.

9 · Key equations

IC ≈ β·IB (forward-active only)
IE = IC + IB = (β + 1)·IB
IC = α·IE, α = β/(β + 1)
iC ≈ IS·evBE/VT·(1 + vCE/VA)

10 · Example

IB = 12.9 µA gives IC = 100 × 12.9 µA = 1.29 mA and IE = 1.30 mA. At this Q-point, a 1 mV change of vBE changes iC by gm × 1 mV ≈ 49.7 µA, nearly four times the entire DC base current.

Advanced: why a few millivolts at the base move the collector current so much

Because iC grows exponentially with vBE, it doubles for every VT·ln 2 ≈ 18 mV and rises ten-fold for every ≈ 60 mV. The slope of that exponential at the Q-point is the transconductance gm = dIC/dVBE = IC/VT.

The same exponential is why the signal must stay small (|vbe| ≪ VT). Larger signals see a curved characteristic and come out distorted (section Distortion). Note also that IC ≈ βIB fails in saturation, where IC < βIB.

3.2

Collector resistor RC: current-to-voltage conversion

Turns the transistor's current signal into the output voltage.

1 · What is it?

A fixed resistor, 5.6 kΩ here, often called the collector load.

2 · Where is it connected?

Between the VCC rail and the collector. The collector node also feeds the output capacitor C2.

3 · Electrical function

By Ohm's law it converts collector-current changes into voltage changes. KVL from the supply through RC to the collector gives VCC = ICRC + VC.

4 · Why is it required?

The transistor delivers a current signal. Without a resistance to develop it across, the collector would be tied to VCC and its voltage could not change: vc = 0.

5 · Influence on DC operation

It sets VC and VCE for a given IC. In the forward-active region IC is fixed by the divider and RE, not by RC. RC only decides where the collector voltage sits, and so how much room the signal has.

6 · Influence on AC operation · phase inversion

The output is vc = −ic(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro). When vin rises, iC rises, the drop across RC grows and VC falls. That minus sign is the 180° inversion. RC also sets the output resistance, Rout ≈ RC ∥ ro.

7 · If it is removed

Open: the collector has no DC path, so IC → 0. The CB junction becomes forward biased and Q1 acts like a base–emitter diode; VB collapses to ≈ 0.8 V. There is no amplification.
Shorted (collector tied to VCC): IC still flows (VCE ≈ 10.7 V), but VCC is AC ground, so vout = 0.

8 · If its value is increased / decreased

Larger RC: more gain, but a lower VCQ. Too large and VCE is squeezed: the output clips on the saturation side, or the transistor saturates outright once ICRC > VCC − VE − 0.2 V.
Smaller RC: VC moves toward VCC and the gain falls. The swing toward cutoff (ICQ·rac) also shrinks.

9 · Key equations

VC = VCC − ICRC
VCE = VC − VE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE)
Av ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro)
Unloaded, ro ignored: Av ≈ −ICRC/VT

10 · Example

VC = 12 − 1.29 mA × 5.6 kΩ = 4.80 V, and VCE = 4.80 − 1.30 = 3.50 V. With gm = 49.7 mA/V, the unloaded gain is Av = −49.7 mS × (5.6 kΩ ∥ 77.7 kΩ) = −259. With RL = 10 kΩ it is −49.7 mS × 3.43 kΩ = −170.

Assumptions behind Av ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro)

  • Q1 is in the forward-active region, and the signal is small: |vbe| ≪ VT.
  • MidbandThe frequency range where the coupling and bypass capacitors act as short circuits and the transistor's internal capacitances still act as open circuits, so the gain is flat.: C1, C2 and CE are short circuits, and the junction capacitances are open circuits.
  • RE is fully bypassed, and the base spreading resistance rx is neglected.
  • The gain is measured from the base. Including the source multiplies it by Rin/(Rin + RS).
Why increasing RC does not always improve practical gain

1 · The load is in parallel. RC ∥ RL can never exceed RL. With RL = 10 kΩ, going from RC = 5.6 kΩ to 56 kΩ raises RC ∥ RL only from 3.6 to 8.5 kΩ.

2 · The bias runs out of room. With IC fixed, VCE = VCC − IC(RC + RE) shrinks until the transistor saturates. Keeping it active then forces a smaller IC, which lowers gm. In fact −gmRC = −(ICRC)/VT, the DC voltage across RC divided by VT. That voltage cannot exceed about VCC − VE − 0.2 V ≈ 10.5 V, so the unloaded bypassed gain here is capped near 10.5/0.0259 ≈ 400.

3 · ro sets a ceiling. Even with an ideal current-source load, the gain cannot exceed −gmro = −VA/VT ≈ −3900.

3.3

Emitter resistor RE: thermal and bias stabilisation

Makes IC depend on resistor values instead of on the transistor.

1 · What is it?

A 1 kΩ resistor in the emitter lead.

2 · Where is it connected?

From the emitter to ground, with CE in parallel.

3 · Electrical function: negative feedback

If IC rises (from heat or a higher β), VE = IERE rises. VB is held by the divider, so VBE = VB − VE falls, IB falls, and IC is pulled back. The output current is sensed and opposes its own change.

4 · Why is it required?

Real transistors vary. RE turns the fixed base voltage into a nearly fixed emitter current, IE ≈ (VB − VBE)/RE, which depends only weakly on β and VBE.

5 · Influence on DC operation · thermal runaway

Thermal runawayA positive-feedback loop: more current → more power dissipated → hotter junction → lower VBE, higher ICBO and higher β → even more current, possibly until the transistor is destroyed. See topic 6.5. is a loop: IC↑ → PC = VCEIC↑ → the junction heats → VBE falls (≈ 2 mV/°C), ICBO doubles every ≈ 10 °C and β rises → IC↑ again. RE breaks the loop. A 50 mV drop in VBE (≈ 25 °C of heating) shifts IC by only ≈ ΔVBE/RE = 50 µA (≈ 4 %). If VBE itself were held fixed with no RE, the same 50 mV would multiply IC by e50/25.9 ≈ 7.

6 · Influence on AC operation · the trade-off

The same feedback also acts on the signal if RE is not bypassed. This is emitter degenerationNegative feedback from an unbypassed emitter resistance. It lowers and stabilises the gain, raises the input resistance and improves linearity.: Av ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE), which drops the gain from −170 to about −3.5. Good DC stability costs AC gain, and that cost is what CE removes.

7 · If it is removed

Shorted (emitter grounded): the divider now drives the base through RTh alone, so IB ≈ (VTh − VBE)/RTh ≈ 0.17 mA. That would call for IC ≈ 17 mA, far more than RC allows (≈ 2.1 mA). The result is saturation with VCE ≈ 0.1 V, and no amplification.
Open: CE blocks DC, so the emitter has no DC path. IE = 0, the transistor is in cutoff and VC = 12 V.

8 · If its value is increased / decreased

Larger RE: IC falls roughly as 1/RE, stability improves and VC rises. Because gm falls with IC, the bypassed gain falls too.
Smaller RE: IC and gm rise but stability gets worse. In this circuit the transistor saturates once RE drops below about 700 Ω (try it in Activity B).

9 · Key equations

VE = IERE
VB ≈ VE + VBE
IE ≈ (VB − VBE)/RE
IC = β(VTh − VBE)/(RTh + (β+1)RE)

10 · Example

VE = 1.30 mA × 1 kΩ = 1.30 V, so VB = 1.30 + 0.70 = 2.00 V. Changing β from 100 to 200 moves IC only from 1.29 to 1.34 mA (+4.4 %). In fixed bias, where IC ∝ β, the same change would double IC (+100 %).

Design guideline and further reading

A common starting point is VE ≈ VCC/10 (1.2 V here). That is large enough to swamp VBE variations, yet small enough to leave most of the supply for the signal. It is a heuristic, not a law. The formal measure of bias stability is the stability factor S = ∂IC/∂ICBO; see 6.6 Stability factors and 6.5 Thermal runaway.

3.4

Emitter bypass capacitor CE: increasing AC gain

Keeps RE for DC and removes it for the signal.

1 · What is it?

A large capacitor, 100 µF here, usually an electrolytic with its + lead on the emitter because VE ≈ +1.3 V.

2 · Where is it connected? Why in parallel?

Directly across RE, from emitter to ground. A capacitor's impedance depends on frequency. In parallel it leaves RE's DC path untouched, because DC cannot flow through a capacitor, but gives the signal current a low-impedance route around RE.

3 · Under DC: an open circuit

Under constant voltage, i = C·dv/dt = 0. Once charged to VE ≈ 1.3 V it carries no DC, so all of IE flows through RE and the full DC stabilisation is retained.

4 · Under AC: a near short

XCE = 1/(2πfCE) = 1.6 Ω at 1 kHz. The emitter's AC voltage is then ≈ 0, so the whole input appears as vbe and Av ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro).

The comparison that really sets CE

XCE must be small compared with the resistance looking into the emitter, re + (RS ∥ R1 ∥ R2)/(β + 1) ≈ 20 Ω, not merely compared with RE = 1 kΩ. That is why CE (100 µF) is ten times larger than C1 or C2, and why CE usually sets the lower cutoff frequency.

5 · Emitter degeneration and gain

With an emitter impedance Ze = RE ∥ ZCE, the input divides as vin = vbe + ieZe, so only part of it drives the junction: Av ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + Ze). Bypassing makes Ze ≈ 0, which gives the full gain.

6 · If it is removed

The DC Q-point is unchanged, but the gain drops from −170 to ≈ −3.5 and Rin rises from 1.62 to 7.63 kΩ. In exchange the gain is far more linear and nearly independent of IC, β and temperature.
Shorted (a failed capacitor): the emitter is grounded at DC too, which is the same as shorting RE, so the transistor saturates.

7 · If it is too small

The bypass only works at higher frequencies, so fCE moves up and low frequencies lose gain. With CE = 10 µF, fCE ≈ 800 Hz, which would cut into the audio band.

8 · Frequency dependence

At very low frequencies the gain is the unbypassed −3.5. It rises past a zero at 1/(2πRECE) = 1.6 Hz and a pole near fCE = 1/(2πRe,eqCE) ≈ 80 Hz, and reaches −170 in midband.

9 · Key equations

XCE = 1/(2πfCE)
Re,eq = RE ∥ [re + (RS ∥ R1 ∥ R2)/(β+1)]
fCE ≈ 1/(2πRe,eqCE)
Unbypassed: Av ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE)

10 · Example

At 1 kHz, XCE = 1.59 Ω ≪ 20 Ω, so the bypass is effective. At 50 Hz, XCE = 31.8 Ω, which is larger than 20 Ω, so the bypass is only partial and the gain is already well below midband. Re,eq = 1 kΩ ∥ 20.4 Ω = 20.0 Ω, giving fCE = 1/(2π × 20 Ω × 100 µF) ≈ 80 Hz.

Partial bypass: trading gain for stability and linearity

Splitting RE into RE1 (unbypassed) in series with RE2 (bypassed) keeps the full RE1 + RE2 for DC while setting a defined midband gain Av ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE1). For a gain of −10 here, RE1 ≈ 3.59 kΩ/10 − 20 Ω ≈ 340 Ω. That gain depends far less on β, IC and temperature, distortion falls, and the bandwidth increases.

3.5

Base bias resistors R1 and R2: voltage-divider bias

Fix the base voltage, and with RE, the Q-point.

1 · What are they?

Two resistors forming a voltage divider: R1 = 47 kΩ (upper) and R2 = 10 kΩ (lower).

2 · Where are they connected?

R1 runs from VCC to the base and R2 from the base to ground. C1 also joins at the base node.

3 · Electrical function

They establish a base voltage VB ≈ 2.0 V that hardly depends on the transistor. RE then converts it into the emitter current IE ≈ (VB − VBE)/RE, which sets the Q-pointQuiescent (resting) operating point: the DC values ICQ and VCEQ with no signal applied. The signal swings the transistor around this point..

4 · Why not a fixed base supply?

Fixed bias (a single RB from VCC) sets IB, so IC = βIB follows β exactly. Changing β from 100 to 300 triples IC and can drive the transistor into saturation. A divider sets a voltage, which with RE gives a current that is almost independent of β.

5 · Influence on DC operation

The simple result VB ≈ VCCR2/(R1 + R2) = 2.105 V holds only if the divider current ≫ IB. For accurate work, replace the divider with its Thevenin equivalentAny linear two-terminal network can be replaced by one voltage source VTh (its open-circuit voltage) in series with one resistance RTh (its resistance with sources zeroed).: VTh = 2.105 V in series with RTh = R1 ∥ R2 = 8.25 kΩ. The base current then drops 12.9 µA × 8.25 kΩ = 0.106 V across RTh, so VB is really 2.00 V. The unloaded approximation overestimates IE by 8 %.

6 · Choosing the divider current

Here the current through R2 is 0.20 mA ≈ 16 × IB. A usual target for a "stiff" divider is (β+1)RE ≥ 10RTh, or roughly R2 ≤ βRE/10. In this circuit 101 kΩ is compared with 82.5 kΩ, so the rule is only just met. The shortcut's error is roughly RTh/((β+1)RE) ≈ 8 %. Smaller resistors give a stiffer bias but draw more supply current and lower Rin, because R1 ∥ R2 shunts the signal.

7 · Influence on AC operation

VCC is AC groundAn ideal DC supply holds a constant voltage, so it has no AC voltage on it. In the small-signal circuit it is therefore connected to ground., so for the signal both resistors run from the base to ground and appear in parallel: RB = R1 ∥ R2 = 8.25 kΩ. This is part of Rin = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ = 1.62 kΩ.

8 · If one is removed or changed

  • R1 open: there is no base drive, so VB → 0 and the transistor is in cutoff (VC = 12 V).
  • R2 open: the base is fed through R1 alone, so IB is large and the transistor saturates.
  • R1 shorted: the base is tied to VCC, and a huge base current (limited only by RE) saturates the transistor and may damage it.
  • R2 shorted: the base is grounded, so the transistor is in cutoff.
  • R2↑ or R1↓: VB↑, so IC↑ (ΔIC ≈ ΔVB/RE) and VCE↓, moving toward saturation. The reverse moves toward cutoff.

9 · Key equations

VB ≈ VCC·R2/(R1 + R2) (unloaded)
VTh = VCCR2/(R1+R2), RTh = R1 ∥ R2
IB = (VTh − VBE)/(RTh + (β+1)RE)
VE ≈ VB − VBE, IE ≈ VE/RE

10 · Example

VTh = 12 × 10/57 = 2.105 V and RTh = 470/57 = 8.25 kΩ. Then IB = (2.105 − 0.7)/(8.25 k + 101 k) = 12.9 µA. The unloaded shortcut would give IE = 1.405 mA, against 1.30 mA from the Thevenin analysis.

What happens to the Q-point when the base voltage changes?

With a stiff divider, ΔIC ≈ ΔVB/RE and ΔVCE ≈ −ΔIC(RC + RE). The Q-point slides along the DC load line: up and to the left (toward saturation) when VB rises, down and to the right (toward cutoff) when it falls. A +0.1 V change in VB here moves IC by about +0.1 mA and VCE by about −0.66 V. Topic 6.4 Voltage divider bias covers the design in depth.

3.6

Input coupling capacitor C1

Lets the signal in and keeps the source's DC out.

1 · What is it?

A 10 µF coupling capacitor, usually an electrolytic with its + lead toward the base, which sits at +2.0 V DC.

2 · Where is it connected?

In series between the signal source (vin, RS) and the base node.

3 · Electrical function

AC coupling, DC isolation. It passes the signal and blocks DC, so the source and the bias network can sit at different DC levels.

4 · Why is it required?

The base sits at 2.0 V while a typical source sits at 0 V DC with a low resistance. Connected directly, the source would appear in parallel with R2 and drag VB down, destroying the bias. C1 protects the Q-point from whatever is connected at the input.

5 · Influence on DC operation

None in normal use: it acts as an open circuit and simply charges to about 2.0 V.

6 · Influence on AC operation

In midband it acts as a short (XC1 = 15.9 Ω at 1 kHz, against RS + Rin ≈ 1.67 kΩ). At low frequencies it forms a high-pass filter with the resistance it sees, Req = RS + Rin, so low frequencies are attenuated below fC1.

7 · If it is removed

Open: no signal reaches the base, so the output is zero. The DC is unaffected.
Shorted (direct coupling): the result depends on the source. A 50 Ω generator pulls the base to ≈ 13 mV, so the transistor is in cutoff. A high-resistance source would merely shift the Q-point.

8 · If its value is increased / decreased

Larger: fC1 moves down, extending the bass response (the part gets bigger and costs more). Smaller: fC1 moves up. For example, 0.47 µF gives fC1 ≈ 200 Hz.

9 · Key equations

XC1 = 1/(2πfC1)
fC1 = 1/(2πReqC1)
Req = RS + Rin, Rin = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ (bypassed)

10 · Example

Req = 50 Ω + 1618 Ω = 1668 Ω, so fC1 = 1/(2π × 1668 Ω × 10 µF) ≈ 9.5 Hz. Req is the resistance seen by the capacitor with the source zeroed. If CE were removed, Rin would rise to 7.6 kΩ and fC1 would fall to ≈ 2.1 Hz.

3.7

Output coupling capacitor C2

Delivers the signal to the load and keeps the collector DC away from it.

1 · What is it?

A 10 µF coupling capacitor with its + lead toward the collector (VC ≈ +4.8 V).

2 · Where is it connected?

In series between the collector node and the load RL (the output terminal vout).

3 · Electrical function

It passes the AC collector signal and blocks the 4.8 V DC collector voltage, so vout swings around 0 V.

4 · Why is it required?

The load (the next stage, a speaker or an ADC input) should not receive DC: it would bias the next stage wrongly, waste power, or damage a load. A DC-coupled RL would also change this stage's own Q-point.

5 · Influence on DC operation

None in normal use: it acts as an open circuit, charged to ≈ 4.8 V, and RL carries no DC.

6 · Influence on AC operation

In midband it acts as a short (15.9 Ω at 1 kHz ≪ 10 kΩ), so RL appears in parallel with RC and Av = −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro). At low frequencies it forms a high-pass filter with Rout + RL.

7 · If it is removed

Open: RL receives nothing (vout = 0), although the collector still swings, with an unloaded gain of −259 measured at the collector.
Shorted: RL is DC-coupled. The collector sees a Thevenin source of 7.69 V behind RC ∥ RL = 3.59 kΩ, so VC drops to ≈ 2.9 V and VCE to ≈ 1.6 V. Q1 stays active, but the clean swing shrinks from ≈ 3.3 V to ≈ 1.4 V, and ≈ 0.3 mA of DC flows in the load.

8 · If its value is increased / decreased

Larger: fC2 moves down. Smaller: fC2 moves up, and with a low-resistance load (small RL) C2 must be larger for the same cutoff.

9 · Key equations

fC2 = 1/(2π(Rout + RL)C2)
Rout ≈ RC ∥ ro

10 · Example

Rout = 5.6 kΩ ∥ 77.7 kΩ = 5.22 kΩ, so fC2 = 1/(2π × 15.2 kΩ × 10 µF) ≈ 1.0 Hz. Here C2 is not the limiting capacitor, because it sees a large resistance.

3.8

DC power supply VCC

The source of all output energy and of the bias.

1 · What is it?

A 12 V DC source (a regulated supply or a battery).

2 · Where is it connected?

Its + terminal is the top rail feeding R1 and RC. Its − terminal is the circuit ground.

3 · Electrical function · energy conversion

It supplies the bias currents and the power that ends up in the output signal. The transistor converts DC power from VCC into signal power, under the control of the input.

4 · Why is it required?

Energy is conserved. Delivering 1 V peak into RL = 10 kΩ takes 50 µW, while the input supplies only about 11 nW. The difference must come from somewhere, and VCC is that source.

5 · Influence on DC operation

It sets VTh (∝ VCC), and therefore IC. It also fixes the ends of the DC load line: VCE = VCC at IC = 0, and IC = VCC/(RC + RE) at VCE = 0.

6 · Influence on AC operation

An ideal supply has zero AC impedance, so it is AC ground: RC and R1 return signal current to ground through it. Real supplies need a decoupling capacitor (e.g. 100 nF ceramic plus 10–100 µF) close to the stage, or signals couple between stages through the supply.

7 · If it is removed

There is no bias and no energy, so the transistor is off and the output is zero.

8 · If its value is increased / decreased

Higher VCC: IC rises roughly in proportion, giving more headroom for swing, more gain and more dissipation. Lower VCC: the swing shrinks and the margins to saturation and cutoff disappear. The available output swing is limited by VCEQ − VCE(sat) on one side and by ICQ·rac on the other.

9 · Key equations

PDC = VCC·ICC, ICC = IC + IR1
Pout = Vp²/(2RL)
Ap = Pout/Pin = Av²·Rin/RL

DC operation and the DC equivalent circuit

DC analysis always comes first. The small-signal parameters gm = IC/VT, rπ = β/gm and ro = VA/IC all depend on the Q-point, and the AC model is only valid if the transistor is actually forward-active. To get the DC equivalent circuit:

  • C1, C2 and CE become open circuits: at DC, i = C·dv/dt = 0.
  • The signal source and RL are therefore disconnected, and RE remains fully in the emitter circuit.
  • R1 and R2 set the base bias, and VCC is the only source.

DC current paths

  • Divider current: I1 flows from VCC through R1. At the base node it splits: I2 continues through R2 to ground and IB enters the base, so I1 = I2 + IB (KCL).
  • Collector current: IC flows from VCC down through RC into the collector.
  • Emitter current: IE = IC + IB leaves the emitter and flows through RE to ground.

Replacing the divider by its Thevenin equivalent

Step-by-step derivation

  1. Thevenin voltage and resistance of the divider (looking from the base, with Q1 disconnected):VTh = VCC·R2/(R1 + R2), RTh = R1R2/(R1 + R2)
  2. KVL around the base loop (VTh → RTh → B–E junction → RE → ground):VTh = IBRTh + VBE + IERE
  3. In the forward-active region, IE = (β + 1)IB. Substitute and solve:IB = (VTh − VBE) / (RTh + (β + 1)RE)The term (β + 1)RE is RE "reflected" into the base circuit. When it dominates RTh, IB·β, and therefore IC, hardly depends on β.
  4. Collector and emitter currents:IC = βIB, IE = (β + 1)IB
  5. Node voltages:VE = IERE, VB = VE + VBE = VTh − IBRTh, VC = VCC − ICRC
  6. KVL around the collector loop (VCC → RC → C–E → RE → ground) gives the DC load line:VCE = VCC − ICRC − IERE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE)
  7. Q-point = (VCEQ, ICQ). Check the assumption made in step 3: forward-active requires VCE > VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V, which is the same as VBC < ≈ 0.5 V. If the check fails, redo the analysis with the saturation model. Never report a "Q-point" that violates its own assumption.

The numbers for this circuit are worked out in full in the worked example: Q = (3.50 V, 1.29 mA).

Key points

  • For DC: capacitors are open, so the source and load drop out and RE stays in.
  • The Thevenin form VTh, RTh gives an exact base-loop equation; the unloaded divider formula is an approximation.
  • IB = (VTh − VBE)/(RTh + (β+1)RE); VCE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE).
  • Always confirm the forward-active assumption before using small-signal formulas.

AC operation: the small-signal model and gain

With the Q-point known, we study only the changes around it, the small-signalSignal amplitudes small enough that the transistor's curved characteristics look straight over the swing (|vbe| ≪ VT ≈ 26 mV). The circuit can then be analysed as linear. response. This section is a midband analysis. Low-frequency and high-frequency behaviour are covered under Frequency response. To get the midband AC equivalent circuit:

  • C1 and C2 become short circuits: their reactance is tiny compared with the resistances around them.
  • CE becomes a short, provided its reactance is small compared with the ≈ 20 Ω looking into the emitter. RE is then bypassed and the emitter is at AC ground.
  • VCC becomes AC ground: a DC source has no AC voltage, so it is replaced by a short. R1, RC now return to ground, which puts R1 ∥ R2 at the base and RC ∥ RL at the collector.
  • Q1 is replaced by its small-signal (hybrid-π) model.

Small-signal parameters (all set by the Q-point)

The key quantity is the TRANSCONDUCTANCEgm = ∂iC/∂vBE at the Q-point. It says how many amps of collector current change per volt of base–emitter signal, and equals IC/VT for a BJT. gm, the slope of the exponential iC–vBE curve at the Q-point:

gm = IC / VT, where VT = kT/q ≈ 25.9 mV at 300 K

rπ = β / gm · re = VT/IE ≈ 1/gm · ro = VA / IC

For IC = 1.29 mA: gm = 49.7 mA/V, rπ = 2.01 kΩ, re = 19.9 Ω and ro = 77.7 kΩ. A 1 mV change at the base changes the collector current by about 49.7 µA.

Derivation of the midband results

  1. With the emitter at AC ground, the whole base signal appears across rπ:vπ = vb
  2. The controlled source gmvπ flows from collector to emitter. It flows through RC, RL and ro in parallel, all returning to AC ground, and the output node goes negative when this current increases:vout = −gmvπ(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro)
  3. Voltage gain (base to output). The minus sign is the 180° phase inversion:Av = vout/vb ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro) = −49.7 mS × 3.43 kΩ ≈ −170
  4. Input resistance, seen by the source after C1:Rin = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ = 8.25 kΩ ∥ 2.01 kΩ ≈ 1.62 kΩ
  5. Output resistance, looking back into the collector with vs = 0 (so gmvπ = 0) and RL removed:Rout = RC ∥ ro ≈ 5.22 kΩ
  6. Effect of RL. The output divides between Rout and RL. Unloaded, the gain is −259; with RL = 10 kΩ it is −170; with RL = 1 kΩ it would be only about −41.Av = Av,unloaded × RL/(RL + Rout)
  7. Including the source resistance RS:Avs = vout/vs = Av × Rin/(Rin + RS) = −170 × 0.970 ≈ −165
Unbypassed emitter resistor: emitter degeneration derivation

If CE is absent, the emitter carries ie = (β+1)ib through RE, so vb = ibrπ + (β+1)ibRE. The resistance looking into the base becomes rπ + (β+1)RE, and, neglecting ro:

Av = −β(RC ∥ RL) / (rπ + (β+1)RE) ≈ −(RC ∥ RL) / (re + RE)

For this circuit that is −100 × 3.59 kΩ / (2.01 k + 101 k) ≈ −3.5, and Rin = 8.25 kΩ ∥ 103 kΩ ≈ 7.6 kΩ. When RE ≫ re, the gain ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/RE is set by a resistor ratio: low, but stable and linear.

Current gain and power gain: what is (and isn't) true

The transistor's small-signal current gain ic/ib is β. The whole stage's current gain is lower, because the input current splits into R1 ∥ R2 and the output current splits between RC and RL. So |Av|·β is only an upper bound on power gain. The correct stage power gain is Ap = Av²·Rin/RL = 170² × 1.62 k/10 k ≈ 4700 (36.7 dB), compared with |Av|·β = 17 000.

Midband ≠ all frequencies. Everything above assumes the coupling and bypass capacitors are shorts and the transistor's internal capacitances are open. Below ≈ 100 Hz CE, C1 and C2 stop being shorts; above ≈ 2 MHz Cπ, Cμ and stray capacitance stop being open. Both reduce the gain. See Frequency response.

Key points

  • Midband AC circuit: C1, C2, CE shorted, VCC → AC ground, Q1 → hybrid-π (rπ, gmvπ, ro).
  • gm = IC/VT = 49.7 mA/V at 1.29 mA; Av ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro) ≈ −170 (−259 unloaded).
  • Rin = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ ≈ 1.62 kΩ; Rout = RC ∥ ro ≈ 5.22 kΩ.
  • Unbypassed RE: Av ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE) ≈ −3.5. Stage power gain = Av²Rin/RL.

Distortion and clipping

The relation between VBE and IC is exponential, not straight. For very small inputs this curve is nearly straight over the tiny range used, so the output is a faithful copy. This is called SMALL-SIGNAL operation, and it requires the base signal to be much smaller than VT (a few millivolts).

For larger inputs:

  • the exponential curve makes the positive and negative halves of the output unequal (the output is DISTORTED);
  • if the swing is large enough, the transistor is driven into SATURATION on one peak, where VCE flattens near 0.2 V (VC ≈ VE + 0.2 V), or into CUTOFF on the other, where iC → 0. With no load VC then rises to VCC; with an AC-coupled RL it stops lower, at about VCQ + ICQ(RC ∥ RL). This is CLIPPING.

Keeping the input small and the Q-point centred avoids distortion.

Key points

  • IC grows exponentially with VBE, so only tiny inputs are copied faithfully.
  • Small-signal operation needs a base signal much smaller than VT (a few mV).
  • Larger inputs give unequal half-cycles (distortion) and harmonics.
  • Very large inputs clip: saturation on one peak, cutoff on the other.

DC and AC load lines

The DC LOAD LINE describes the circuit with no signal: VCE = VCC − IC·(RC + RE), because both resistors carry the DC current. The Q-point lies on this line.

For the signal, the capacitors act as short circuits: CE removes RE, and the output coupling capacitor connects RL in parallel with RC. So the signal sees a smaller resistance, rac = RC ∥ RL. The signal swings along a steeper line through the Q-point, called the AC LOAD LINE, with slope −1/rac.

The maximum undistorted output swing is limited by how far the Q-point is from saturation and from cutoff along the AC load line.

Key points

  • DC load line: VCE = VCC − IC(RC + RE); Q lies on it.
  • For the signal, CE removes RE and RL joins RC in parallel: rac = RC ∥ RL.
  • The AC load line passes through Q with slope −1/rac, steeper as RL falls.
  • The clean swing is limited by Q's distance to saturation and cutoff along the AC line.

Frequency response

The gain is not constant over all frequencies, because the circuit contains capacitors whose impedance 1/(2πfC) changes with frequency. There are three regions:

  • Low frequencies (below fL): the reactances of C1, C2 and CE are no longer small. C1 and C2 act as series high-pass filters, and CE fails to bypass RE, so degeneration returns. CE usually dominates because it sees only ≈ 20 Ω.
  • Midband (fL to fH): the coupling and bypass capacitors act as shorts and the transistor's capacitances act as open circuits. The gain is flat and equal to the value derived in AC analysis.
  • High frequencies (above fH): the junction capacitances Cπ (base–emitter) and Cμ (collector–base), plus wiring, probe and next-stage capacitance CL, start to short the signal. Cμ is especially harmful because of the Miller effect: seen from the input it looks like Cμ(1 + gmR′L) ≈ 4 pF × 171 ≈ 690 pF.

fC1 = 1/(2π(RS + Rin)C1) · fC2 = 1/(2π(Rout + RL)C2) · fCE = 1/(2πRe,eqCE)

Adding the three estimates (the short-circuit time-constant method) gives an upper estimate of fL: 9.5 + 1.0 + 79.5 ≈ 90 Hz with the hand-analysis Q-point, against ≈ 83 Hz from an exact solution of the same circuit. The graph below uses the page's transistor model, whose slightly higher IC (1.32 mA) moves both values up by a few hertz. Bandwidth BW = fH − fL ≈ fH. The gain–bandwidth product |Am|·BW stays roughly constant when the gain is traded for bandwidth (for example by partial bypassing), as long as one pole dominates the high-frequency response. That is a useful rule of thumb, not an exact law.

Key points

  • Low-frequency roll-off comes from C1, C2 and especially CE; high-frequency roll-off comes from Cπ, the Miller-multiplied Cμ and stray CL.
  • Midband is the flat region where the simple gain formula holds.
  • A larger source resistance lowers fH, because it must charge the Miller capacitance.
  • BW ≈ fH − fL; gain × bandwidth is roughly constant when one pole dominates.

Summary

A transistor amplifies by letting a small base signal control a large collector current drawn from the supply. It must be biased in the active region at a suitable Q-point. In the voltage-divider biased common emitter amplifier, R1 and R2 with RE set a stable Q-point, coupling capacitors pass the signal, the bypass capacitor keeps the gain high, and the output is inverted. DC analysis comes first: capacitors open, Thevenin base circuit, then a forward-active check. Midband AC analysis follows: capacitors shorted, VCC at AC ground, hybrid-π model, giving a gain of about −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro) with gm = IC/VT. The gain falls at low frequencies (C1, C2, CE) and at high frequencies (junction and stray capacitance). Small signals are amplified faithfully; large signals are distorted or clipped.

Key points

  • A small base signal controls a large collector current from the supply.
  • Bias in the active region at a suitable Q-point.
  • CE amplifier: coupling capacitors pass the signal, CE keeps the gain high, output inverted.
  • Av ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL ∥ ro) in midband; small signals clean, large signals distorted or clipped.

Check your understanding

Unless a question says otherwise, use VBE = 0.7 V and VT = 26 mV, and treat capacitors as shorts in midband.

A · Conceptual multiple-choice questions

1. For the CE stage to amplify linearly, the transistor's junctions must be:

Forward-active means the BE junction is forward biased (so it injects carriers) and the CB junction is reverse biased (so it collects them). Both forward is saturation; both reverse is cutoff.

2. Why is RE bypassed with CE?

CE is open at DC, so RE still stabilises the Q-point, and nearly a short at signal frequencies, so emitter degeneration disappears and the gain rises from about −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE) to −gm(RC ∥ RL). Bypassing actually lowers Rin.

3. The output of a CE amplifier is inverted because:

The inversion comes from VC = VCC − iCRC: input up → iC up → VC down. In midband the coupling capacitors add (almost) no phase shift.

4. At DC (steady state), the bypass capacitor CE behaves as:

For constant voltage, i = C·dv/dt = 0. Once charged to VE, CE carries no current, so it is an open circuit.

5. Why does removing CE reduce the voltage gain?

The Q-point (and so gm) does not change, because CE carries no DC. With RE in the signal path, vin = vbe + ieRE, so most of the input is "used up" across RE and the gain becomes ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE).

6. Why is DC analysis performed before AC analysis?

Every small-signal parameter is evaluated at the Q-point (gm = IC/VT, rπ = β/gm, ro = VA/IC). If the transistor were saturated or cut off, the hybrid-π model would not apply at all.

7. A BJT is biased at IC = 2 mA. Its transconductance is about:

gm = IC/VT = 2 mA / 26 mV ≈ 76.9 mA/V. Note that it depends only on IC, not on β.

8. In a stiff voltage-divider bias circuit, R2 is increased slightly. The Q-point:

A larger R2 raises VB, so ΔIC ≈ ΔVB/RE > 0 and VCE = VCC − IC(RC + RE) falls. The Q-point always stays on the DC load line.

9. In a typical CE amplifier, which capacitor usually sets the lower cutoff frequency?

Each capacitor's corner frequency is 1/(2πRC) with R the resistance it sees. CE sees RE ∥ [re + (RS ∥ RB)/(β+1)], only about 20 Ω here, so even 100 µF gives the highest corner (≈ 80 Hz). Cμ affects the upper cutoff.

10. The upper cutoff frequency fH of a CE stage is mainly caused by:

At high frequencies Cπ, Cμ and stray capacitance shunt the signal. Cμ appears (1 + gmR′L) times larger at the input (Miller effect), so the source resistance and the gain strongly influence fH. Coupling capacitors only matter at low frequencies.

  1. A divider-biased CE stage has VCC = 15 V, R1 = 68 kΩ, R2 = 12 kΩ, RC = 4.7 kΩ, RE = 1.2 kΩ and β = 150. Find VTh, RTh, IC and VCE, and verify the operating region.
    Solution

    VTh = 15 × 12/80 = 2.25 V; RTh = 68 ∥ 12 = 10.2 kΩ. IB = (2.25 − 0.7)/(10.2 k + 151 × 1.2 k) = 1.55/191.4 k = 8.10 µA, so IC = 1.21 mA and IE = 1.22 mA. VCE = 15 − 1.215 × 4.7 − 1.223 × 1.2 ≈ 7.82 V. Since VC = 9.29 V > VB = 2.17 V, the transistor is forward-active ✓.

  2. A bypassed CE stage runs at IC = 0.5 mA with β = 120, RC = 10 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ, and ro can be neglected. Find gm, rπ and Av.
    Solution

    gm = 0.5/26 = 19.2 mA/V; rπ = 120/19.2 mS = 6.24 kΩ; Av = −19.2 mS × (10 k ∥ 10 k) = −19.2 mS × 5 kΩ ≈ −96.

  3. The same kind of stage has its emitter resistor RE = 470 Ω left unbypassed, with IC = 2 mA, RC = 4.7 kΩ and no load. Estimate the voltage gain.
    Solution

    re = 26 mV/2 mA = 13 Ω. Av ≈ −RC/(re + RE) = −4700/483 ≈ −9.7. Bypassed, the gain would be −gmRC ≈ −362; degeneration trades about 31 dB of gain for stability and linearity.

  4. The resistance seen by the bypass capacitor is Re,eq = 25 Ω. What CE gives fCE = 20 Hz?
    Solution

    CE = 1/(2π × 20 × 25) = 318 µF, so use the next standard value, 330 µF. Notice how large CE must be, because the resistance it sees is small.

  5. With RS = 600 Ω, Rin = 2.4 kΩ, C1 = 4.7 µF, Rout = 4.7 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ and C2 = 1 µF, find fC1 and fC2.
    Solution

    fC1 = 1/(2π × 3.0 kΩ × 4.7 µF) ≈ 11.3 Hz; fC2 = 1/(2π × 14.7 kΩ × 1 µF) ≈ 10.8 Hz. Each R is the resistance the capacitor "sees", with the signal source zeroed.

C · Reasoning questions

  1. Why is RE bypassed using CE, and why must CE be much larger than C1?
    Answer

    RE is needed for DC stability but kills AC gain through degeneration. CE separates the two roles: it is open at DC and a short for the signal. It must be large because it has to be a short compared with the ≈ re (tens of ohms) looking into the emitter, whereas C1 only needs to be small compared with kilohms (RS + Rin).

  2. Why does a CE amplifier produce phase inversion? Would a common-collector stage invert?
    Answer

    The output is taken from the collector, where VC = VCC − iCRC: an increase in input increases iC and lowers VC. A common-collector stage takes its output from the emitter, where VE = iERE rises with the input, so it does not invert.

  3. Why are coupling capacitors needed? Give one reason at each end.
    Answer

    At the input, C1 stops the source's DC level and resistance from altering VB (a 50 Ω generator would pull the base to almost 0 V). At the output, C2 keeps the ≈ 4.8 V collector DC out of the load and stops RL from shifting the Q-point. Both pass the signal because their reactance is small in midband.

  4. What happens to the Q-point when the base voltage changes, and why does RE make this predictable?
    Answer

    With VBE nearly constant, VE follows VB, so ΔIE ≈ ΔVB/RE. The Q-point slides along the DC load line: a higher VB means more IC and less VCE, toward saturation. Because the change is set by RE rather than by β or the exponential junction, it is predictable and modest.

  5. Why does increasing RC not always improve practical voltage gain?
    Answer

    (1) The load and ro are in parallel, so RC ∥ RL ∥ ro saturates at about RL. (2) For a fixed IC, a larger RC lowers VCE until the transistor saturates. Keeping it active forces IC down, which lowers gm. The unloaded gain equals −(ICRC)/VT, so it is capped by the supply voltage. (3) The output resistance rises, so the next stage loads it more heavily.

D · Application questions

  1. You are adapting this stage as a microphone pre-amplifier that must pass 20 Hz. Which capacitor do you change first, and roughly to what value?
    Answer

    CE, because it sets fCE ≈ 80 Hz. For fCE ≈ 10 Hz (leaving margin so the combined fL stays below 20 Hz): CE ≈ 1/(2π × 10 × 20 Ω) ≈ 800 µF, so use 1000 µF. A microphone's higher source resistance also changes Re,eq and fC1, so re-check C1; or use partial bypassing, which raises Re,eq and so reduces the CE needed.

  2. Can this stage drive an 8 Ω loudspeaker directly through C2? Explain using the gain formula.
    Answer

    No. Av ≈ −gm(RC ∥ RL) ≈ −49.7 mS × 8 Ω ≈ −0.4, a loss rather than a gain, and 1.3 mA could not deliver useful power anyway. A low-output-resistance buffer (an emitter follower or power stage) is placed between the CE stage and the speaker.

  3. A design needs a gain of about −10 that stays constant across transistors and temperature. How would you modify the emitter circuit?
    Answer

    Use partial bypassing: split RE into RE1 (unbypassed) plus RE2 (bypassed by CE), keeping RE1 + RE2 = 1 kΩ for the same Q-point. Choose RE1 ≈ (RC ∥ RL)/10 − re ≈ 340 Ω (330 Ω standard). The gain is then set mainly by a resistor ratio and depends little on β, IC or temperature.

E · Troubleshooting

  1. On a built circuit you measure VB ≈ 0.68 V, VE ≈ 0 V and VC ≈ 0.1 V, and there is no output signal. What is the likely fault?
    Answer

    VE ≈ 0 with VBE ≈ 0.7 V and VCE ≈ 0.1 V means the emitter is grounded and the transistor is saturated. The likely cause is a shorted CE (a failed electrolytic, or one fitted with reversed polarity) or a shorted RE. With the emitter grounded, the divider overdrives the base. Compare Activity A → CE, Shorted.

  2. The DC voltages are correct (VB ≈ 2.0 V, VE ≈ 1.3 V, VC ≈ 4.8 V), but the gain is only about 3–4 instead of about 170. What is wrong?
    Answer

    A correct Q-point rules out the resistors and the transistor's DC behaviour. A gain of ≈ −(RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE) ≈ −3.5 is exactly the unbypassed value, so CE is open: dried out, a bad joint, or the wrong part fitted. Bridging a known-good capacitor across RE confirms it.
