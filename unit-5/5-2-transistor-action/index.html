How a Transistor Works: Carrier Flow Inside

Follow the carriers: injected by the emitter, across the base, collected by the collector

Biasing the transistor for amplification

To use a BJT as an amplifier, its two junctions must be biased in a particular way. This is called the active mode:

  • the emitter-base junction is forward biased;
  • the collector-base junction is reverse biased.

For a silicon NPN transistor this means the base is made about 0.7 V more positive than the emitter (VBE ≈ 0.7 V), and the collector is made more positive than the base (VCB > 0, often several volts).

We will follow the carriers in an NPN transistor step by step: first they are injected from the emitter, then they cross the base, and finally they are collected by the collector.

Key points

  • Active mode (for amplification): EBJ forward biased, CBJ reverse biased.
  • Silicon NPN: base about 0.7 V above the emitter (VBE ≈ 0.7 V).
  • Collector above the base (VCB > 0, often several volts).
  • Three steps follow: inject, cross the base, collect.

Step 1: Emitter injection

Forward biasing the emitter-base junction lowers its potential barrier. Carriers can now cross it easily:

  • electrons from the N-type emitter cross into the P-type base;
  • holes from the P-type base cross into the emitter.

Because the emitter is very heavily doped and the base is lightly doped, the electrons crossing from emitter to base far outnumber the holes crossing from base to emitter. Nearly all of the emitter current is therefore carried by electrons moving into the base. The fraction of emitter current carried by these electrons is called the emitter injection efficiency (γ), and in a good transistor it is very close to 1, typically above 0.99.

The holes that cross from base to emitter are wasted current: they must be supplied by the base terminal and do not reach the collector.

Key points

  • Forward bias lowers the EBJ barrier; electrons go E → B, holes go B → E.
  • Heavy emitter, light base: electrons far outnumber holes.
  • Emitter injection efficiency γ = electron share of IE, typically above 0.99.
  • Holes injected into the emitter are wasted current, supplied by the base.

Step 2: Crossing the base

Once inside the P-type base, the injected electrons are minority carriers surrounded by holes. Their concentration is high at the emitter edge of the base and almost zero at the collector edge, so they diffuse across the base toward the collector.

On the way, a few electrons meet holes and recombine. Each recombination removes a hole from the base, and that hole must be replaced by the base terminal. This is the main part of the base current.

Two design choices keep recombination very small:

  • the base is very thin, so electrons cross it quickly;
  • the base is lightly doped, so there are few holes to recombine with.

As a result, typically more than 99% of the injected electrons reach the collector side. The fraction that survives the trip is called the base transport factor (αT), very close to 1. This is exactly why two separate diodes cannot act as a transistor: their “base” would be far too wide and almost all electrons would recombine.

Key points

  • In the base, injected electrons are minority carriers and diffuse toward the collector.
  • A few recombine with holes; replacing those holes is the main part of IB.
  • A thin, lightly doped base keeps recombination small.
  • Base transport factor αT = fraction reaching the collector, very close to 1.

Step 3: Collection

The collector-base junction is reverse biased, so its depletion region contains a strong electric field pointing from collector to base. This field opposes the majority carriers, but it helps minority carriers.

When an electron that has crossed the base reaches the edge of this depletion region, the field sweeps it rapidly across into the collector. It then flows out of the collector terminal as the collector current.

Important result: the collector current is set by how many electrons the emitter injects (controlled by VBE), not by the collector voltage. As long as the collector junction stays reverse biased, raising VCB changes IC only very slightly. The collector behaves like a current source.

Key points

  • The reverse-biased CBJ has a strong field pointing from collector to base.
  • Electrons reaching it are swept into the collector and leave as IC.
  • IC is set by VBE (injection), not by VCB.
  • The collector behaves like a current source.

The complete picture and the currents

Putting the three steps together for an NPN transistor:

  • Electrons injected from the emitter make up most of the emitter current IE.
  • Most of these electrons cross the base and are collected, forming the collector current IC.
  • The base current IB supplies the holes that are lost to recombination in the base and the holes injected from the base into the emitter.

By Kirchhoff’s current law: IE = IB + IC.

Because only a tiny fraction of the carriers is lost, IB is very small compared with IC. A typical small-signal transistor might have IC about 100 times IB. If the base current is increased a little, the emitter injects more electrons and the collector current increases about 100 times as much. This control of a large current by a small one is transistor action, and it is the basis of amplification. Topic 5.3 defines the gains α and β precisely.

Key points

  • Injected electrons form most of IE; those collected form IC.
  • IB replaces holes lost to recombination and holes injected into the emitter.
  • Kirchhoff’s current law: IE = IB + IC.
  • A small change in IB gives about 100 times the change in IC: transistor action.

The band diagram view

The same process can be seen on an energy band diagram of the NPN transistor in active mode.

The conduction band edge has a hill at the emitter-base junction. Forward bias lowers this hill, so electrons from the emitter can climb over it into the base. In the base the band is flat, and the electrons diffuse across. At the collector-base junction the reverse bias creates a steep downhill slope, and electrons that reach it roll down into the collector, gaining energy that is given up as heat in the collector.

The height of the emitter hill is controlled by VBE. A small change in VBE changes the number of electrons that can get over the hill exponentially, which is why IC depends so strongly on VBE.

Key points

  • Ec has a hill at the EBJ; forward bias (VBE) lowers it.
  • Electrons climb the hill, diffuse across the flat base, then roll down the CBJ slope.
  • The energy gained on the slope is given up as heat in the collector.
  • IC rises exponentially with VBE, about 10 times per 60 mV.

The PNP transistor, and summary

A PNP transistor works in exactly the same way, with the roles of electrons and holes exchanged. The emitter injects holes into the N-type base; they diffuse across the thin base and are swept into the P-type collector. For active mode the emitter-base junction is still forward biased and the collector-base junction reverse biased, but all the voltages are reversed: the base is about 0.7 V more negative than the emitter, and the collector is more negative than the base. All current directions are reversed too.

Summary: forward-biased EBJ injects carriers, the thin lightly doped base passes almost all of them through, the reverse-biased CBJ collects them. A small base current controls a large collector current.

Key points

  • PNP: same process with holes as the injected carriers.
  • PNP active mode: EBJ still forward, CBJ still reverse, but all voltages and currents are reversed.
  • Inject (forward EBJ) → cross (thin, lightly doped base) → collect (reverse CBJ).
  • A small base current controls a large collector current.
