TOPIC 5.10

Early Effect (Base-Width Modulation)

The collector voltage squeezes the base, so the output curves tilt upward

Ideal vs real output curves

In an ideal transistor working in the active region, the collector current depends only on the base-emitter voltage (or base current). Changing the collector voltage would have no effect, so the output characteristics would be perfectly flat horizontal lines.

Real transistors are not like this. In the active region the output curves slope gently UPWARD: for a fixed base current or fixed VBE, IC increases slowly as VCE increases. This effect was explained by James Early in 1952, and it is called the EARLY EFFECT or BASE-WIDTH MODULATION.

Key points

  • Ideal: in the active region IC depends only on VBE (or IB), so the curves are flat.
  • Real: the curves slope gently upward; IC grows slowly with VCE.
  • Explained by James Early in 1952: the Early effect, or base-width modulation.

The cause: the base gets thinner

In the active region the collector-base junction is reverse biased. A reverse-biased junction has a depletion region whose width increases as the reverse voltage increases.

The collector-base depletion region extends into both the collector and the base. The part that extends into the base eats into the neutral (undepleted) base. So as VCB (or VCE) increases, the EFFECTIVE BASE WIDTH, the width of the neutral base region that electrons must diffuse across, becomes SMALLER.

In other words, the collector voltage changes (modulates) the width of the base. Since the base is already very thin, even a small reduction in its width is a significant fraction of it.

Key points

  • The reverse-biased CB depletion region widens as VCB rises.
  • It grows into both the collector and the base.
  • The part in the base shrinks the neutral (effective) base width W.
  • The base is already thin, so even a small change is a large fraction of W.

The consequences

A narrower effective base has three effects:

  1. STEEPER CARRIER GRADIENT: the concentration of injected electrons is set at the emitter edge of the base by VBE, and is nearly zero at the collector edge. If the base is narrower, the same concentration difference occurs over a shorter distance, so the gradient is steeper. Diffusion current is proportional to the gradient, so the collector current INCREASES, even though VBE has not changed.
  2. LESS RECOMBINATION: with a narrower base, electrons spend less time crossing it, so fewer recombine. The base transport factor rises, so α increases slightly and β increases noticeably.
  3. CHANGE IN INPUT CURRENT: for a fixed VBE, the emitter current also increases slightly. This is why the input characteristics shift a little when the output voltage changes (seen in topics 5.4 and 5.5).

If the collector voltage is raised far enough, the effective base width can shrink to zero. This is called PUNCH-THROUGH (topic 5.11).

Key points

  • Same concentration drop over a shorter W: steeper gradient, larger IC at the same VBE.
  • Shorter transit, less recombination: α rises slightly, β rises noticeably.
  • The emitter current also rises a little, shifting the input characteristics.
  • If W shrinks to zero the device punches through (topic 5.11).

The Early voltage

If the straight, sloping parts of the output curves in the active region are extended backward (to the left), they all meet the VCE axis at nearly the same point, at a NEGATIVE voltage. The magnitude of this voltage is called the EARLY VOLTAGE, VA. Typical values are tens to a few hundred volts.

The Early effect is included in the collector current equation as:

IC = IS·exp(VBE/VT)·(1 + VCE/VA)

The slope of an output curve gives the transistor's small-signal OUTPUT RESISTANCE:

ro = VA / IC

For example, with VA = 100 V and IC = 1 mA, ro = 100 kΩ. A larger Early voltage means flatter curves and a higher output resistance, which is better.

Key points

  • Extended back, the active-region lines meet the VCE axis near −VA.
  • VA is typically tens to a few hundred volts.
  • IC = IS·exp(VBE/VT)·(1 + VCE/VA); ro = VA/IC.
  • Larger VA: flatter curves, higher ro (100 kΩ at 100 V and 1 mA).

Why the Early effect matters in circuits

  • GAIN LIMIT: in an amplifier, ro appears in parallel with the collector load. Even with an ideal current-source load, the maximum voltage gain of a single transistor is limited to gm·ro = (IC/VT)·(VA/IC) = VA/VT. This is called the INTRINSIC GAIN. For VA = 100 V it is about 100/0.0259 ≈ 3860.
  • CURRENT SOURCES: a transistor used as a constant current source is not perfectly constant; its current changes slightly with voltage because of ro.
  • DESIGN TRADE-OFF: a wider or more heavily doped base reduces the Early effect (larger VA), but it also increases recombination and lowers β. Circuit techniques such as the CASCODE connection are used to reduce the effect instead.

Key points

  • ro sits in parallel with the load and caps the gain at gm·ro = VA/VT.
  • The intrinsic gain does not depend on IC: about 3860 for VA = 100 V.
  • A transistor current source changes slightly with voltage, slope 1/ro.
  • A wider or heavier-doped base raises VA but lowers β; the cascode is the usual circuit fix.

Summary

Increasing the reverse voltage across the collector-base junction widens its depletion region into the base and reduces the effective base width. This base-width modulation makes IC rise with VCE, slightly increases α and β, and gives the transistor a finite output resistance ro = VA/IC. The Early voltage VA measures how strong the effect is. It limits the maximum gain of a single transistor to VA/VT. Taken to the extreme, the base width reaches zero: punch-through.

Key points

  • Higher VCB widens the CB depletion region and narrows the effective base.
  • IC rises with VCE; α and β rise slightly; ro = VA/IC is finite.
  • VA measures the effect and caps single-transistor gain at VA/VT.
  • Taken to the extreme, W reaches zero: punch-through.
