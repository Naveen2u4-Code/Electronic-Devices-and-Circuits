TOPIC 5.1

BJT Structure: NPN vs PNP

Three doped regions, two junctions, and the two ways to stack them

What is a bipolar junction transistor?

A transistor is a three-terminal semiconductor device that can amplify a signal or act as an electronic switch. The bipolar junction transistor (BJT) was the first practical transistor, developed at Bell Labs in the late 1940s, and it made modern electronics possible.

A BJT is made of three doped semiconductor regions placed one after another, forming two PN junctions. The three regions are called the emitter (E), the base (B) and the collector (C), and each has its own terminal.

It is called bipolar because current inside it is carried by both kinds of carriers: electrons and holes. It is called a junction transistor because its action depends on its two PN junctions.

The key idea of a BJT is control: a small current into the base controls a much larger current flowing between the collector and the emitter.

Note: two separate diodes connected back to back do not make a transistor. Transistor action needs both junctions to share one extremely thin base region inside a single crystal. Topic 5.2 explains why.

Key points

  • A BJT has three doped regions, emitter, base and collector, forming two PN junctions.
  • Bipolar: both electrons and holes carry the current.
  • A small base current controls a much larger collector–emitter current.
  • Two back-to-back diodes are not a transistor: the base must be one extremely thin, shared region.

The three regions and two junctions

The three regions are not alike. Each is built for a different job:

  • Emitter: heavily doped. Its job is to emit (inject) a large number of charge carriers into the base.
  • Base: very thin and lightly doped. Its job is to let almost all injected carriers pass straight through to the collector, while giving a small control current.
  • Collector: moderately doped and physically the largest region. Its job is to collect the carriers coming from the base. Because most of the power is dissipated here, it is made large so it can get rid of heat.

The two PN junctions are:

  • the emitter-base junction (EBJ, also written JE), between emitter and base;
  • the collector-base junction (CBJ, also written JC), between collector and base.

Because the emitter and collector differ in doping and size, a BJT is not symmetrical. If you swap the emitter and collector connections, the device still works a little, but its current gain becomes very small. The base width is very small compared with the emitter and collector, and this thinness is essential for transistor action.

Key points

  • Emitter: heavily doped, injects carriers into the base.
  • Base: very thin and lightly doped, passes almost all carriers on to the collector.
  • Collector: moderately doped and largest, collects carriers and gets rid of the heat.
  • Two junctions, EBJ (JE) and CBJ (JC); the BJT is not symmetrical, so swapping E and C makes the gain very small.

NPN and PNP transistors

There are two types of BJT, depending on the order of the doped regions:

  • NPN: an N-type emitter, a P-type base and an N-type collector. The main current carriers through the base are electrons.
  • PNP: a P-type emitter, an N-type base and a P-type collector. The main current carriers through the base are holes.

Both work on the same principle. In a PNP transistor every voltage polarity and every current direction is simply the reverse of an NPN.

Circuit symbols: both have a base line in the middle with the emitter and collector drawn as slanted lines. The emitter carries an arrow that shows the direction of conventional current through the emitter. In an NPN, the arrow points out of the transistor (memory aid: “Not Pointing iN”). In a PNP, the arrow points in.

NPN transistors are used more often, because electrons move more easily than holes (higher mobility). This makes NPN transistors faster and gives them better performance for the same size.

Key points

  • NPN: N–P–N, electrons carry the current through the base.
  • PNP: P–N–P, holes carry it; every polarity and current direction is reversed.
  • The emitter arrow shows conventional current: out for NPN (“Not Pointing iN”), in for PNP.
  • NPN is more common: electrons have higher mobility, so NPN is faster.

Summary

The BJT is a three-region, two-junction device. The heavily doped emitter injects carriers, the thin lightly doped base controls them, and the large moderately doped collector collects them. NPN and PNP transistors work the same way with opposite polarities. The arrow in the symbol marks the emitter and shows the direction of conventional current.

Key points

  • Three regions, two junctions: the emitter injects, the base controls, the collector collects.
  • NPN and PNP work the same way with opposite polarities and current directions.
  • The symbol’s arrow marks the emitter and shows conventional current: out for NPN, in for PNP.
