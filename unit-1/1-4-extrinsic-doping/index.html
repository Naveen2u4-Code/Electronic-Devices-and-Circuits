TOPIC 1.4

Extrinsic Semiconductors: N-type and P-type Doping

How a tiny amount of impurity turns silicon into a useful conductor of electrons or holes

Why do we dope a semiconductor?

Pure (intrinsic) silicon has very few free carriers. At room temperature it has only about 1 × 10¹⁰ free electrons per cm³, among about 5 × 10²² silicon atoms per cm³. That is roughly one free electron for every 5 trillion atoms. Its conductivity is therefore very low, and it changes strongly with temperature. This makes pure silicon unsuitable for most electronic devices.

Doping is the process of adding a small, carefully controlled amount of a selected impurity element to the silicon crystal. The impurity atoms take the place of some silicon atoms in the crystal. Even one impurity atom for every million silicon atoms (about 5 × 10¹⁶ atoms per cm³) increases the number of free carriers by millions of times.

A semiconductor whose carriers come mainly from added impurities is called an extrinsic semiconductor. Depending on the impurity used, we get two types: N-type, which has extra free electrons, and P-type, which has extra holes.

Key points

  • Pure Si has only about 10¹⁰ free electrons per cm³ at room temperature.
  • Doping adds a small, controlled amount of a chosen impurity.
  • 1 impurity per million Si atoms raises the carrier count millions of times.
  • Extrinsic silicon is N-type (extra electrons) or P-type (extra holes).

N-type semiconductor: donor atoms

To make N-type silicon, we add atoms from group V of the periodic table, such as phosphorus (P), arsenic (As) or antimony (Sb). These are called pentavalent atoms because each has five valence electrons.

A phosphorus atom sits in the place of a silicon atom. Four of its five valence electrons form covalent bonds with the four neighbouring silicon atoms, exactly as a silicon atom would. The fifth electron has no bond to join. It is held to the phosphorus atom only by a weak attraction, so very little energy is needed to set it free: about 0.045 eV for phosphorus, 0.054 eV for arsenic and 0.039 eV for antimony. Compare this with the 1.12 eV needed to break a silicon bond. At room temperature, the available thermal energy is enough to free almost every one of these extra electrons.

Because the impurity atom gives away (donates) an electron, it is called a donor. After giving away its electron, the phosphorus atom has one more proton than electrons, so it becomes a positive ion. This ion is fixed in the crystal and cannot move, so it does not carry current.

Freeing a donor electron does not create a hole, because no bond is broken. A few electron-hole pairs are still created by heat, just as in pure silicon. As a result, free electrons far outnumber holes. In N-type material, electrons are the majority carriers and holes are the minority carriers. The letter N stands for negative, the charge of the majority carriers. The material as a whole remains electrically neutral.

Key points

  • Donors are group V atoms (P, As, Sb) with five valence electrons.
  • The 5th electron is freed by only about 0.045 eV (P), against 1.12 eV to break a Si bond.
  • The donor becomes a fixed positive ion; no hole is created.
  • N-type: electrons are the majority, holes the minority; the crystal stays neutral.

N-type on the energy band diagram

On the energy band diagram, each donor atom adds an allowed energy level, called the donor level (ED), inside the forbidden gap. It lies very close to the bottom of the conduction band: for phosphorus, Ec − ED is about 0.045 eV.

At absolute zero, the extra electrons sit in these donor levels. As the temperature rises, they easily jump the small distance into the conduction band, where they are free to move. The donor levels they leave behind represent the fixed positive donor ions.

At room temperature almost all donors are ionised, so the conduction band contains about as many electrons as there are donor atoms, plus the much smaller number created by breaking bonds. Because the conduction band is now much more populated, the Fermi level moves up, closer to the conduction band. This shift is explained in topic 1.5.

Key points

  • The donor level ED lies just below Ec (Ec − ED ≈ 0.045 eV for P).
  • At 0 K the extra electrons sit in ED; heat lifts them into the conduction band.
  • At 300 K nearly all donors are ionised, so n ≈ number of donors.
  • EF lies closer to Ec than in pure silicon.

P-type semiconductor: acceptor atoms

To make P-type silicon, we add atoms from group III, such as boron (B), aluminium (Al), gallium (Ga) or indium (In). These are trivalent atoms: each has only three valence electrons.

A boron atom in place of a silicon atom can complete only three of the four covalent bonds with its neighbours. One bond is left with a missing electron. An electron from a nearby silicon bond can move into this vacancy with very little energy, about 0.045 eV for boron. When it does, the boron atom has gained an extra electron and becomes a fixed negative ion, while the vacancy has moved to the neighbouring bond. That vacancy is a hole, and it is free to move through the crystal as explained in topic 1.3.

Because the impurity atom accepts an electron, it is called an acceptor. Each acceptor produces one hole without producing a free electron. Holes therefore far outnumber free electrons. In P-type material, holes are the majority carriers and electrons are the minority carriers. The letter P stands for positive, the effective charge of the majority carriers. Again, the material as a whole remains electrically neutral.

Key points

  • Acceptors are group III atoms (B, Al, Ga, In) with three valence electrons.
  • A neighbouring electron fills the incomplete bond with only about 0.045 eV (B).
  • The acceptor becomes a fixed negative ion and leaves a mobile hole behind.
  • P-type: holes are the majority, electrons the minority; the crystal stays neutral.

P-type on the energy band diagram

Each acceptor adds an allowed energy level, the acceptor level (EA), inside the gap just above the top of the valence band. For boron, EA − Ev is about 0.045 eV.

At absolute zero these acceptor levels are empty. As temperature rises, electrons from the valence band easily jump up into them. Each electron that does so leaves behind a hole in the valence band, and the filled acceptor level represents a fixed negative acceptor ion.

At room temperature almost all acceptors are ionised, so the valence band contains about as many holes as there are acceptor atoms. The Fermi level moves down, closer to the valence band.

Key points

  • The acceptor level EA lies just above Ev (EA − Ev ≈ 0.045 eV for B).
  • Valence electrons jump up into EA, leaving holes in the valence band.
  • At 300 K nearly all acceptors are ionised, so p ≈ number of acceptors.
  • EF lies closer to Ev than in pure silicon.

Majority and minority carrier concentrations

At room temperature we can assume every dopant atom is ionised. For N-type silicon with donor concentration ND, the electron concentration is therefore n ≈ ND.

The hole concentration does not stay at the intrinsic value ni. Because there are now so many electrons, holes meet electrons and recombine much more often, and the hole concentration falls. In equilibrium, n × p = ni² (the mass action law, topic 1.8), so p = ni² / ND.

Example, silicon at 300 K with ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ and ND = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³: n ≈ 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and p = (10¹⁰)² / 10¹⁶ = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³. Electrons outnumber holes by a factor of 10¹².

For P-type silicon with acceptor concentration NA: p ≈ NA and n = ni² / NA.

Since majority carriers dominate, conductivity is approximately σ ≈ q·ND·μn for N-type and σ ≈ q·NA·μp for P-type. For the example above (μn ≈ 1350 cm²/V·s), σ ≈ 2.2 S/cm and the resistivity ρ ≈ 0.46 Ω·cm, roughly 500,000 times lower than pure silicon.

Key points

  • N-type: n ≈ ND, p = ni²/ND. P-type: p ≈ NA, n = ni²/NA.
  • n × p = ni² = 10²⁰ cm⁻⁶ for Si at 300 K, whatever the doping.
  • More majority carriers means fewer minority carriers.
  • σ ≈ q·ND·μn or q·NA·μp; ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ gives ρ ≈ 0.46 Ω·cm.

Charge neutrality and compensation

Doping does not give the crystal a net charge. Every donor atom is neutral before it gives up its electron; afterwards, the free electron and the positive ion together still add up to zero. The same is true for each acceptor and its hole. This gives the charge neutrality equation (all dopants ionised):

n + NA = p + ND

If a crystal contains both donors and acceptors, the electrons released by donors fill the vacancies at acceptor atoms first, and the two partly cancel. This is called compensation. Only the difference produces free carriers:

  • If ND > NA, the material is N-type with n ≈ ND − NA.
  • If NA > ND, the material is P-type with p ≈ NA − ND.

Compensation is used in chip fabrication: a P-type region can be formed inside an N-type wafer by adding more acceptors than the donors already present.

Key points

  • Doping adds carriers, not net charge: n + NA = p + ND.
  • Donors and acceptors cancel each other first (compensation).
  • ND > NA: N-type, n ≈ ND − NA. NA > ND: P-type, p ≈ NA − ND.
  • Chip makers use it to form P regions inside N-type wafers.

Effect of temperature, and summary

The relation n ≈ ND holds only over a certain temperature range. Consider silicon with ND = 1 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³:

  • Freeze-out region (very low temperature, below about 100 K): there is not enough thermal energy to free all the donor electrons, so n is less than ND.
  • Extrinsic region (about 100 K to about 500 K): practically all donors are ionised, and electrons created by breaking bonds are still far fewer than ND. Here n ≈ ND and stays almost constant. Devices are designed to operate in this range.
  • Intrinsic region (high temperature): the number of electron-hole pairs created by heat (ni) becomes comparable to, and then larger than, ND. The material starts behaving like pure silicon again, and the doping no longer controls the carrier concentration. Heavier doping pushes this region to higher temperatures.

Key points

  • Freeze-out (below about 100 K): n < ND.
  • Extrinsic (about 100–500 K): n ≈ ND, almost constant; devices work here.
  • Intrinsic (high T): ni reaches and passes ND; doping loses control.
  • Heavier doping pushes the intrinsic region to higher temperatures.
