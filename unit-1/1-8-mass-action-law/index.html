TOPIC 1.8

Mass Action Law and Carrier Concentration

Why adding donors makes holes disappear, and how to calculate both carrier concentrations in any sample

Your device asks for reduced motion, so every animation starts paused. Press Play on any animation to run it. Sliders and buttons still update the picture.

The question

In topic 1.4 we doped silicon with donors at ND = 1016 cm⁻³. Almost every donor gives up an electron, so the electron concentration becomes n ≈ 1016 cm⁻³. We also said something surprising: the hole concentration becomes only about p ≈ 104 cm⁻³.

Pure (intrinsic) silicon at 300 K has n = p = ni ≈ 1010 cm⁻³. So adding donors did not just add electrons. It also removed holes: about a million times fewer than before.

That is strange, because a donor atom has nothing to do with holes. It gives away an electron; it does not capture holes. So why do the holes decrease when we add donors?

The answer is a simple and powerful rule called the mass action law. It lets us calculate the minority carrier concentration (holes in N-type, electrons in P-type) in any doped semiconductor. Minority carriers are tiny in number, but they control how diodes and transistors work, so we need to know them accurately.

Key points

  • Intrinsic Si at 300 K: n = p = ni ≈ 1010 cm⁻³.
  • N-type with ND = 1016: n ≈ 1016 but p ≈ 104 cm⁻³.
  • Donors do not remove holes directly, yet holes fall a million times.
  • The mass action law explains this and gives the minority carrier concentration.

Generation and recombination balance

Inside every semiconductor two opposite processes are always running.

1. Generation. Heat makes the lattice vibrate, and now and then a vibration breaks a covalent bond. This frees an electron and leaves a hole behind: an electron–hole pair is created. The generation rate G (pairs per cm³ per second) depends only on the material and its temperature. It does not depend on how many carriers are already there: a bond breaks whether or not other free carriers are nearby.

2. Recombination. A free electron meets a hole and falls back into the bond. Both carriers disappear. For a meeting you need an electron and a hole, so the recombination rate is proportional to both concentrations:

R = r n p

where r is a constant for the material. Double the electrons and each hole meets an electron twice as often; double both and recombination is four times faster.

Equilibrium. Start with no free carriers. Generation adds pairs, recombination is slow at first, so the numbers grow. As they grow, recombination speeds up, until it removes pairs exactly as fast as they are created. This steady state is thermal equilibrium:

G = r n p ⇒ n p = G/r

Both G and r depend only on the material and the temperature, so the product np is fixed by temperature alone. Doping does not change it.

We can find its value from intrinsic material, where n = p = ni. Then np = ni2. Since the product is the same for every sample of that material at that temperature,

n p = ni2

in any equilibrium sample, intrinsic or doped.

Key points

  • Generation rate G depends only on material and temperature.
  • Recombination rate R = r n p needs both an electron and a hole.
  • Equilibrium: G = R, so np = G/r, fixed by temperature.
  • Intrinsic material shows the constant is ni2: np = ni2 in any equilibrium sample.

The mass action law

Statement. In a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium, the product of the electron and hole concentrations is a constant at a given temperature:

n0 p0 = ni2

The subscript 0 means "in equilibrium". It does not matter whether the sample is intrinsic, N-type or P-type: if one concentration goes up, the other must go down by the same factor.

A second way to see it. In topic 1.5 we wrote the carrier concentrations using the Fermi level EF:

n = Nc e-(Ec − EF)/kT p = Nv e-(EF − Ev)/kT

Multiply them. The exponents add, and EF cancels out:

n p = Nc Nv e-(Ec − Ev)/kT = Nc Nv e-Eg/kT = ni2

The result depends only on the material (Nc, Nv, Eg) and the temperature. Moving EF up by kT multiplies n by e and divides p by e: the product never changes.

For Si at 300 K, ni ≈ 1010 cm⁻³, so np = 1020 cm⁻⁶.

Conditions. The law holds only

  • in thermal equilibrium: no light shining on the sample and no current being injected into it, and
  • for non-degenerate doping, where EF stays a few kT away from the band edges: below roughly 1018 to 1019 cm⁻³ in Si.

Key points

  • Mass action law: n0 p0 = ni2 in thermal equilibrium.
  • From the Fermi-level formulas, EF cancels: np = Nc Nv e-Eg/kT.
  • Si at 300 K: np = 1020 cm⁻⁶.
  • Valid only in equilibrium and for non-degenerate doping.

Why adding donors reduces holes

Now we can answer the question from the start.

Adding donors adds a large number of electrons. The temperature has not changed, so generation carries on at exactly the same rate as before: the same number of new pairs per second.

But each hole now finds itself surrounded by far more electrons. Recombination needs an electron and a hole, and its rate is r n p. With n suddenly much larger, holes are caught by electrons much more often. Recombination now outpaces generation, and the hole population falls.

As p falls, recombination slows down again. The fall stops when recombination once more equals generation. At the new balance there are many electrons and very few holes, and the product is back where it started: np = ni2.

Some electrons are lost too (one for every hole that recombines), but the donors supplied so many that n hardly notices. For holes the loss is everything.

The same happens in reverse with acceptors: they add many holes, electrons are caught by holes more often, and n drops until np = ni2 again.

Key points

  • Donors add electrons; generation continues at the same rate.
  • More electrons → holes recombine faster → p falls.
  • New balance: many electrons, few holes, np = ni2 again.
  • Acceptors do the reverse: n falls.

Charge neutrality and the exact solution

The mass action law gives one equation for two unknowns, n and p. We need a second equation, and it comes from charge neutrality: a piece of semiconductor in equilibrium has no net charge. The positive charges are holes and ionised donors (ND^+); the negative charges are electrons and ionised acceptors (NA^-). With all dopants ionised:

p + ND = n + NA

Put p = ni2/n into this and multiply by n. We get a quadratic equation, n2 − (ND − NA) n − ni2 = 0. Its positive root, for a net N-type sample (ND > NA), is

n = ND − NA/2 + sqrtleft(ND − NA/2right)2 + ni2, p = ni2/n

For a net P-type sample (NA > ND), the same steps give

p = NA − ND/2 + sqrtleft(NA − ND/2right)2 + ni2, n = ni2/p

The usual approximation. In most real samples the net doping is far larger than ni (for example 1016 against 1010). Then the square root is almost exactly (ND − NA)/2, and

n ≈ ND − NA (N-type), p ≈ NA − ND (P-type)

with the minority carrier from ni2 divided by the majority. Note that donors and acceptors cancel each other: a sample containing both is called compensated, and only the difference counts.

Check the intrinsic case: with ND = NA = 0, the exact formulas give n = p = ni, as they should. The approximation fails when the net doping is close to ni or smaller, for example at high temperature, where ni is large. Try it below.

Key points

  • Charge neutrality: p + ND = n + NA.
  • With np = ni2 this gives a quadratic; take the positive root.
  • If | ND − NA| gg ni: n ≈ ND − NA (N) or p ≈ NA − ND (P).
  • The minority carrier is always ni2 divided by the majority.
  • Compensated samples: only the difference ND − NA matters.

Worked examples

All three examples are for Si at 300 K, with ni = 1010 cm⁻³, so ni2 = 1020 cm⁻⁶. In each case the net doping is millions of times ni, so the simple approximation is excellent.

Example 1: N-type, ND = 1016 cm⁻³

Majority carriers: every donor gives one electron, so

n ≈ ND = 1016 cm-3

Minority carriers, from the mass action law:

p = ni2/n = frac10201016 = 104 cm-3

Example 2: P-type, NA = 1017 cm⁻³

p ≈ NA = 1017 cm-3 n = ni2/p = frac10201017 = 103 cm-3

Example 3: compensated, ND = 5×1016, NA = 2×1016 cm⁻³

There are more donors than acceptors, so the sample is N-type. Each acceptor cancels one donor:

n ≈ ND − NA = 5×1016 − 2×1016 = 3×1016 cm-3 p = ni2/n = frac10203×1016 ≈ 3.3×103 cm-3

Key points

  • Step 1: majority ≈ net doping (ND − NA or NA − ND).
  • Step 2: minority = ni2 / majority.
  • ND = 1016 → p = 104; NA = 1017 → n = 103 cm⁻³.
  • The further the Fermi level is from Ei, the more lopsided n and p become.

Temperature effect and summary

The intrinsic concentration ni rises very quickly with temperature, roughly as e-Eg/2kT. In Si near room temperature it grows by about 10 times for every 30 K. The product ni2 rises even faster: about 100 times for the same step.

Now take a sample with fixed donor doping ND. Over a wide range of temperature the majority carrier stays almost constant at n ≈ ND. But the minority carrier

p = ni2/ND

follows ni2 and rises very rapidly. This is why the reverse leakage current of a diode, which is carried by minority carriers, grows so strongly with temperature (topic 2.6).

At high enough temperature ni approaches ND. The thermally generated pairs now outnumber the electrons from the donors, the minority carriers catch up with the majority, and the sample behaves like intrinsic material. The more heavily a sample is doped, the higher the temperature it can reach before this happens. Devices stop working properly in this range, which is one reason why silicon devices have a maximum operating temperature.

Key points

  • ni rises steeply with temperature; ni2 even faster.
  • Majority stays ≈ ND; minority p = ni2/ND rises rapidly → diode leakage grows with heat.
  • When ni approaches ND the sample becomes intrinsic.
