TOPIC 1.1

What is a Semiconductor?

Insulators vs metals vs semiconductors

What decides if a material conducts?

Every atom has three parts that matter here: a positive nucleus, inner electrons held tightly close to it, and outer valence electrons. The inner electrons are locked in place and never take part in conduction.

An electric current is simply moving charge. So a material can conduct only if it has charge carriers that are free to move when a voltage is applied.

That makes the whole story one question: how tightly does the material hold on to its valence electrons? There are three answers. Insulators hold them very tightly, so nothing flows. Metals let them go completely, so current flows easily. Semiconductors are in between, and, most importantly, we can control them.

Insulators

An insulator is a material that does not allow electric current to flow under normal conditions. Common examples are glass (mostly SiO2), rubber, plastic, mica, porcelain, dry wood and diamond.

Why? In an insulator every valence electron is locked in a strong chemical bond, either covalent (shared between two atoms) or ionic (handed from one atom to another). There are practically no free electrons, so there is nothing to carry a current.

The energy view. Freeing an electron from its bond costs a minimum energy called the band gap, Eg. In an insulator this gap is large, more than about 4–5 eV: SiO2 ≈ 9 eV and diamond ≈ 5.5 eV (300 K). The typical thermal energy at room temperature is only kT ≈ 0.026 eV, hundreds of times too small, so heat cannot free the electrons. The resistivity is enormous: about 1010 to 1016 Ω·m.

Under a voltage the bound electrons are pulled slightly towards the positive side, so each atom becomes a little stretched. This is called polarisation. The charges shift a tiny distance but they do not flow. If the voltage is made extremely high, the electric field can rip electrons out of their bonds all at once. The insulator then fails suddenly, which is called dielectric breakdown, and it is usually permanently damaged.

Uses: insulators are as important as conductors. They cover wires, form printed circuit boards, and a very thin gate oxide (traditionally SiO2) sits inside every MOSFET in a chip.

Key points

  • All valence electrons are locked in strong bonds, so there are practically no free carriers.
  • Large band gap (> ~4–5 eV). SiO2 ≈ 9 eV is far beyond kT ≈ 0.026 eV at 300 K.
  • Resistivity ≈ 1010–1016 Ω·m.
  • A voltage only polarises the atoms. Too high a voltage causes dielectric breakdown, which is destructive.
  • Used for wire insulation, PCBs and MOSFET gate oxides.

Metals (conductors)

A metal (conductor) is a material that allows current to flow very easily. Silver, copper, gold and aluminium are the best-known examples.

Why? Each metal atom gives up its valence electrons (copper gives one) into a shared “sea” of free electrons that belongs to the whole crystal, not to any single atom. What remains are positive ion cores fixed in a regular lattice. The number of free electrons is huge: about 8.5 × 1028 per m3 in copper, roughly one per atom.

The energy view. In a metal the energy bands overlap or are only partly filled, so there is no gap. An electron can move into a slightly higher empty state with almost no extra energy.

Random motion vs drift. Even without a voltage the free electrons move very fast (about 106 m/s in copper), but in random directions. Just as many cross any plane one way as the other, so there is no net current. A voltage adds a slow drift towards the positive terminal on top of this random motion, typically only a fraction of a millimetre per second. This tiny drift, shared by an enormous number of electrons, is the current.

Resistivity is very low: about 10−8 Ω·m (copper ≈ 1.7 × 10−8 Ω·m at 300 K). When heated, the ion cores vibrate more, electrons collide with them more often, and the resistance increases. This is a positive temperature coefficient.

Uses: wires, cables, and the copper interconnects that wire up the transistors inside a chip.

Key points

  • Valence electrons form a shared electron sea (≈ 8.5 × 1028 m−3 in Cu). Ion cores stay fixed.
  • Bands overlap: no band gap.
  • Random motion gives no net current. A voltage adds a slow drift, and that is the current.
  • Resistivity ≈ 10−8 Ω·m (Cu 1.7 × 10−8 Ω·m, 300 K).
  • Hotter means more collisions and more resistance (positive temperature coefficient).

Semiconductors

A semiconductor is a material whose conductivity lies between that of metals and insulators and, more importantly, can be controlled by temperature, by light and by adding tiny amounts of impurities (doping).

Examples: silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge) are in group IV of the periodic table, so each atom has 4 valence electrons. Compound semiconductors such as gallium arsenide (GaAs) are also widely used.

Structure. In a silicon crystal each atom shares its 4 valence electrons with 4 neighbours, forming 4 covalent bonds. At 0 K every bond is complete, no electron is free, and pure silicon behaves like an insulator.

The energy view. The band gap is small: 1.12 eV for Si and 0.66 eV for Ge (300 K). At room temperature, heat breaks a few bonds. Each broken bond releases a free electron and leaves behind an empty place in the bond called a hole. A neighbouring bound electron can jump into the hole, so the hole moves, and it behaves like a positive charge carrier. Both electrons and holes carry current. Pure (intrinsic) silicon has a resistivity of about 2.3 × 103 Ω·m and germanium about 0.46 Ω·m: between metals and insulators.

Temperature and light. Heating breaks more bonds, so the number of carriers rises sharply and the resistance decreases. This is a negative temperature coefficient, the opposite of a metal. Light whose photon energy is larger than the band gap also breaks bonds, which is how solar cells and photodiodes work.

Why it matters. Because conduction can be controlled, semiconductors are used to make diodes, transistors and every integrated circuit. Doping, the most powerful way to control them, is covered in topic 1.4.

Key points

  • Si and Ge have 4 valence electrons in 4 covalent bonds. At 0 K they behave as insulators.
  • Small band gap: Si 1.12 eV, Ge 0.66 eV (300 K).
  • Each broken bond makes one free electron and one hole. Both carry current, and in pure material n = p.
  • Intrinsic resistivity: Si ≈ 2.3 × 103 Ω·m, Ge ≈ 0.46 Ω·m (300 K).
  • Hotter means more carriers and less resistance (negative temperature coefficient). Light can also create carriers.
