TOPIC 1.5

The Fermi Level and How Doping Shifts It

One energy level that tells you which states are filled, and how many electrons and holes a semiconductor has

Why we need the Fermi level

A silicon crystal contains an enormous number of allowed energy states, grouped into the valence band and the conduction band. Each state can hold one electron. To understand a semiconductor we must answer a simple question: which of these states are actually filled with electrons, and which are empty?

The answer depends on temperature and on doping. Instead of listing every state, we describe the whole picture with one reference energy called the Fermi level, EF.

If we know where EF sits on the band diagram, we can tell at a glance whether the material is intrinsic, N-type or P-type, and we can calculate how many free electrons and holes it has.

A useful picture is a stadium whose seats are filled from the bottom row upwards. EF is roughly the "water level" of the electrons: below it, the seats are almost all taken; above it, they are almost all empty. When the crowd warms up, a few people near the top jump to higher rows and leave gaps behind them.

Key points

  • A crystal has a huge number of allowed states; the question is which are filled.
  • The filling depends on temperature and doping.
  • The Fermi level EF is one reference energy that describes the filling.
  • Where EF sits tells us the type of material and its electron and hole numbers.

The Fermi-Dirac distribution

The Fermi-Dirac function gives the probability that an allowed state at energy E is occupied by an electron:

f(E) = 1 / (1 + exp((E − EF)/kT))

Here k is Boltzmann's constant and T is the absolute temperature. The product kT is the typical thermal energy: at 300 K, kT = 0.0259 eV.

At 0 K, every state below EF is filled (f = 1) and every state above it is empty (f = 0). The graph is a sharp step.

Above 0 K, the step softens over a range of a few kT around EF. Some electrons in states just below EF gain thermal energy and move to states just above it. Far from EF nothing changes: deep states stay full and high states stay empty.

At E = EF exactly, the exponential equals 1, so f = 1/2 at any temperature. This gives the definition: the Fermi level is the energy at which the probability of occupation is 50%. EF does not need to be an allowed state; in a semiconductor it usually lies inside the band gap.

The probability that a state is empty is 1 − f(E). In the valence band an empty state is a hole, so 1 − f(E) is the probability of finding a hole.

When E − EF is more than about 3kT, the 1 in the denominator is small next to the exponential, and

f(E) ≈ exp(−(E − EF)/kT)

This is the Boltzmann approximation; at 3kT its error is about 5%, and it shrinks quickly further out. It is used in most semiconductor calculations, because the conduction band is usually many kT above EF.

Key points

  • f(E) = 1/(1 + exp((E − EF)/kT)) is the probability a state is filled.
  • At 0 K it is a sharp step; above 0 K it softens over a few kT.
  • f(EF) = 1/2 at any temperature: this defines EF.
  • 1 − f(E) is the probability a state is empty (a hole in the valence band).
  • For E − EF > 3kT, f(E) ≈ exp(−(E − EF)/kT) (Boltzmann).

From probability to carrier concentration

The number of electrons at a given energy depends on two things: how many states are available at that energy, and the probability that each one is filled.

electrons at E = (states available at E) × (probability filled)

The number of available states per unit energy per unit volume is called the density of states, g(E). It is zero inside the band gap, because there are no allowed states there. Above Ec it grows with energy (roughly as √(E − Ec)), and below Ev it grows as we go deeper into the valence band.

In the conduction band, electrons are given by gc(E) × f(E). In the valence band, holes are given by gv(E) × (1 − f(E)). Because f(E) falls off exponentially above EF, and 1 − f(E) falls off exponentially below it, almost all electrons sit within a few kT of Ec, and almost all holes within a few kT of Ev.

Adding up (integrating) these distributions gives the two results used throughout the course:

n = Nc·exp(−(Ec − EF)/kT)
p = Nv·exp(−(EF − Ev)/kT)

Nc and Nv are the effective densities of states of the conduction and valence bands. Values for Si at 300 K: Nc ≈ 2.8 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and Nv ≈ 1.04 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. The formulas hold while EF stays more than about 3kT inside the gap.

The key idea: the closer EF is to Ec, the more electrons; the closer EF is to Ev, the more holes. Moving EF by just kT changes n or p by a factor of e ≈ 2.7.

Key points

  • Carriers at E = density of states g(E) × probability of occupation.
  • g(E) = 0 in the gap; electrons crowd near Ec, holes near Ev.
  • n = Nc·exp(−(Ec − EF)/kT), p = Nv·exp(−(EF − Ev)/kT).
  • EF near Ec → many electrons; EF near Ev → many holes.

Fermi level in an intrinsic semiconductor

In an intrinsic (pure) semiconductor, every free electron comes from a broken bond that also leaves a hole, so n = p. Setting the two formulas equal and solving for EF gives:

Ei = (Ec + Ev)/2 + (kT/2)·ln(Nv/Nc)

This position of the Fermi level is called the intrinsic level, Ei. The first term is exactly mid-gap; the second is a small correction. For Si, Nc is larger than Nv, so ln(Nv/Nc) is negative and Ei sits slightly below mid-gap: by only about 0.013 eV at 300 K, about 1% of the 1.12 eV gap. For most purposes, Ei ≈ mid-gap.

Using Ei as the reference, the carrier formulas take a neat form:

n = ni·exp((EF − Ei)/kT)
p = ni·exp((Ei − EF)/kT)

When EF = Ei, both give n = p = ni ≈ 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ for Si at 300 K.

Key points

  • Intrinsic: n = p, so EF sits at the intrinsic level Ei.
  • Ei = (Ec + Ev)/2 + (kT/2)·ln(Nv/Nc) ≈ mid-gap.
  • For Si, Ei is only about 0.013 eV below mid-gap at 300 K.
  • n = ni·exp((EF − Ei)/kT), p = ni·exp((Ei − EF)/kT).

N-type: EF moves up

Adding donor atoms (topic 1.4) puts extra electrons into the conduction band. At room temperature almost every donor is ionised, so n ≈ ND. From n = ni·exp((EF − Ei)/kT), a larger n needs a larger EF − Ei, so the Fermi level must rise toward Ec. Taking the logarithm gives its position:

EF − Ei = kT·ln(ND/ni)
Ec − EF = kT·ln(Nc/ND)

Values for Si at 300 K (ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³):

  • ND = 10¹⁴ cm⁻³ → EF − Ei ≈ 0.24 eV
  • ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ → EF − Ei ≈ 0.36 eV
  • ND = 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ → EF − Ei ≈ 0.48 eV (Ec − EF ≈ 0.086 eV)

Notice how slowly EF moves. Each 10× increase in doping raises EF by only kT·ln 10 ≈ 0.06 eV. The shift is logarithmic: a ten-thousand-fold increase in doping, from 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, moves EF by only about 0.24 eV.

Even at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, EF is still a few kT below Ec, so these formulas remain usable. At much heavier doping EF reaches the conduction band; the material is then called degenerate, and the simple formulas no longer apply.

Key points

  • Donors raise n, so EF moves up toward Ec.
  • EF − Ei = kT·ln(ND/ni); Ec − EF = kT·ln(Nc/ND).
  • Si at 300 K: 0.24, 0.36, 0.48 eV above Ei for ND = 10¹⁴, 10¹⁶, 10¹⁸ cm⁻³.
  • About 0.06 eV per decade of doping: the shift is logarithmic.

P-type: EF moves down

Adding acceptor atoms gives p ≈ NA. From p = ni·exp((Ei − EF)/kT), a larger p needs EF to fall below Ei, so the Fermi level moves down toward Ev:

Ei − EF = kT·ln(NA/ni)
EF − Ev = kT·ln(Nv/NA)

The idea is the mirror image of N-type: about 0.06 eV per decade of doping. For example, NA = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ puts EF about 0.36 eV below Ei.

Whatever the doping, the product n·p = ni² = 10²⁰ cm⁻⁶ (Si, 300 K) stays the same, so raising one carrier type lowers the other. The position of EF alone tells you the majority carrier: above Ei means N-type, below Ei means P-type, at Ei means intrinsic.

Key points

  • Acceptors raise p, so EF moves down toward Ev.
  • Ei − EF = kT·ln(NA/ni); EF − Ev = kT·ln(Nv/NA).
  • About 0.06 eV per decade, the mirror image of N-type.
  • n·p = ni² at every doping; EF above, at or below Ei means N-type, intrinsic or P-type.

Effect of temperature, and EF in equilibrium

Temperature. As temperature rises, ni grows very rapidly (topic 1.3), while the doping stays fixed. For an N-type sample, EF − Ei = kT·ln(ND/ni), and the ratio ND/ni keeps falling, so EF moves back toward Ei. Once ni becomes comparable to ND, heat-generated carriers outnumber the donor electrons: the sample behaves as intrinsic and EF ≈ Ei. For Si with ND = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, ni reaches ND at about 550 K.

At very low temperature the opposite happens: the donors "freeze out" and hold on to their electrons, and EF rises to lie between ED and Ec.

Equilibrium rule. In thermal equilibrium (no applied voltage, no light, steady temperature), the Fermi level is flat: it has the same value everywhere in a connected system. If EF were higher in one region, electrons there would flow into empty states at the same energy in the other region until the levels matched.

So when N-type and P-type material are joined, EF must line up into a single flat line, and the bands bend near the junction to make this possible. This is the starting point of the PN junction (topic 2.1).

Key points

  • Higher temperature → larger ni → EF moves back toward Ei.
  • When ni ≈ ND the sample behaves as intrinsic (≈ 550 K for Si with 10¹⁵ cm⁻³).
  • At very low temperature donors freeze out and EF lies between ED and Ec.
  • In equilibrium EF is flat through a connected system; joining N and P bends the bands.
