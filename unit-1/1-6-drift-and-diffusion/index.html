TOPIC 1.6

Drift and Diffusion Currents

The two ways electrons and holes carry current: pushed by a field, or spreading out on their own

How do carriers move?

Electric current is the net flow of charge. In a semiconductor the moving charges are free electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band.

Carriers can produce a net flow for two quite different reasons:

  • Drift: an electric field pushes them.
  • Diffusion: they spread out from where there are many of them to where there are few.

Both can happen at the same time, in the same piece of material. Every semiconductor device (diodes, transistors, solar cells) works through some balance of these two mechanisms, so it pays to understand each one clearly.

Before we push the carriers, we first need to see how they move when nothing is pushing them.

Key points

  • Current is the net flow of charge carried by electrons and holes.
  • Drift: carriers are pushed by an electric field.
  • Diffusion: carriers spread from high to low concentration.
  • Real devices work through a balance of the two.

Random thermal motion and scattering

Even with no voltage applied, free carriers are never still. Their thermal energy makes them move very fast: about 10⁷ cm/s (100 km/s) at room temperature. Each carrier moves in a random direction.

A carrier does not travel far in a straight line. It constantly collides (we say it scatters) with vibrating lattice atoms and with charged impurity ions, and every collision sends it off in a new direction. The average time between collisions is very short, about 0.1 picosecond (10⁻¹³ s). The average distance between collisions, called the mean free path, is only tens of nanometres.

The result is a random zig-zag path. Because every direction is equally likely, as many carriers cross any imaginary line from left to right as from right to left. The net movement averages to zero, so no current flows, even though every carrier is moving very fast.

This random motion is the background for everything that follows. Drift and diffusion are both small, orderly effects added on top of it.

Key points

  • Thermal speed of carriers ≈ 10⁷ cm/s at room temperature.
  • Carriers scatter off lattice vibrations and impurity ions about every 0.1 ps.
  • Mean free path: tens of nanometres.
  • Random directions → zero net movement → no current.

Drift current

Now apply an electric field E. Each carrier feels a force F = qE. Between collisions it speeds up slightly in the direction of that force; each collision then randomises its direction again. On top of the fast random motion, a slow, steady average motion appears. Its average velocity is called the drift velocity vd.

For low and moderate fields the drift velocity is proportional to the field:

vd = μ E

The constant μ is the mobility, measured in cm²/V·s. It tells us how easily a carrier moves through the crystal. Values for Si at 300 K: μn ≈ 1350 and μp ≈ 480 cm²/V·s. Mobility is studied in detail in topic 1.7.

Electrons drift opposite to E because their charge is negative; holes drift along E. They move in opposite directions, but they also carry opposite charges, so both drift currents point in the direction of E and add together.

The drift current density is

Jdrift = q (nμn + pμp) E = σ E

where σ is the conductivity. This is simply Ohm's law written at the microscopic level.

At very high fields, above about 10⁴ V/cm in Si, the carriers lose energy to the lattice as fast as they gain it. The drift velocity stops increasing and levels off near 10⁷ cm/s. This is called velocity saturation, and it limits the speed of very small transistors.

Key points

  • A field adds a slow average drift velocity on top of the random motion.
  • vd = μ E at low and moderate fields; Si: μn ≈ 1350, μp ≈ 480 cm²/V·s.
  • Electrons drift against E, holes along it; both currents point along E.
  • Jdrift = q(nμn + pμp)E = σ E (Ohm's law).
  • Above ≈ 10⁴ V/cm, vd saturates near 10⁷ cm/s.

Diffusion current

Diffusion needs no force at all. Suppose carriers are crowded on one side of a sample and sparse on the other; we say there is a concentration gradient. Every carrier still moves at random, but there are simply more carriers on the crowded side to wander across, so more cross from crowded to sparse than the other way. The net flow is from high concentration to low concentration, just as a drop of ink spreads out in still water.

The flow of carriers is proportional to the gradient: the steeper the change in concentration, the faster they spread. The constant of proportionality is the diffusion coefficient D, measured in cm²/s. Turning carrier flow into current gives

Jn,diff = + q Dn dn/dx
Jp,diff = − q Dp dp/dx

Watch the signs. Electrons diffuse from high to low concentration, but because they are negative, the electron diffusion current points the opposite way: toward the high concentration. Holes are positive, so they diffuse and carry current in the same direction, from high to low.

Diffusion carries on as long as there is a gradient. It stops only when the concentration has become uniform.

Key points

  • Diffusion needs no field: random motion plus a concentration gradient.
  • Carriers flow from high to low concentration; the flow ∝ gradient, constant D (cm²/s).
  • Jn,diff = qDn dn/dx, Jp,diff = −qDp dp/dx.
  • Electron current points toward high concentration; hole current points away from it.
  • Diffusion stops when the concentration is uniform.

The Einstein relation

Drift and diffusion look like different processes, but both come from the same random scattering motion. A carrier that scatters rarely both drifts easily in a field and spreads quickly in a gradient. So mobility and diffusion coefficient must be linked. The link is the Einstein relation:

D/μ = kT/q

The quantity kT/q is called the thermal voltage. At 300 K it is 25.9 mV. Values for Si at 300 K:

Dn = μn kT/q ≈ 1350 × 0.0259 ≈ 35 cm2/s
Dp = μp kT/q ≈ 480 × 0.0259 ≈ 12.4 cm2/s

Once you know a mobility, you know the diffusion coefficient too. Electrons in Si spread almost three times faster than holes, for the same reason that they drift almost three times faster.

Key points

  • D/μ = kT/q: one random motion causes both drift and diffusion.
  • Thermal voltage kT/q = 25.9 mV at 300 K.
  • Si at 300 K: Dn ≈ 35 cm²/s, Dp ≈ 12.4 cm²/s.

Total current, and drift balancing diffusion

In general both mechanisms act at once. The electron and hole current densities are each the sum of a drift part and a diffusion part, and the total current is their sum:

Jn = q n μn E + q Dn dn/dx
Jp = q p μp E − q Dp dp/dx
J = Jn + Jp

An important case is a non-uniformly doped bar, with more donors on the left than on the right, and no external voltage. At first the electron concentration follows the donors, so there are more electrons on the left. Electrons therefore diffuse from left to right.

As they leave, the fixed positive donor ions they leave behind on the left are no longer balanced, and the extra electrons on the right make that side negative. This separated charge creates an internal (built-in) electric field pointing from left to right. The field pushes electrons back to the left by drift.

The process stops when the two effects balance. In equilibrium the drift current exactly cancels the diffusion current, so the total current is zero, even though both currents are still flowing. The same balance creates the built-in potential of a PN junction (topic 2.2). Applying an external voltage upsets the balance, and a net current flows.

Key points

  • Jn = qnμnE + qDn dn/dx, Jp = qpμpE − qDp dp/dx, J = Jn + Jp.
  • Non-uniform doping: diffusion uncovers fixed ions and creates a built-in field.
  • In equilibrium, drift exactly cancels diffusion: total current = 0.
  • This balance is the origin of the PN junction built-in potential.
