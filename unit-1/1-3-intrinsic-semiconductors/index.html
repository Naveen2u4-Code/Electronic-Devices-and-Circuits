TOPIC 1.3

Intrinsic Semiconductors: Electrons and Holes

Where the free carriers in pure silicon come from, what a hole is, and how many carriers there are

What is an intrinsic semiconductor?

The word intrinsic means "belonging to the thing itself". An intrinsic semiconductor is a semiconductor crystal with no impurities added on purpose, so all of its electrical behaviour comes from the material alone. Pure silicon (Si) and pure germanium (Ge) are the standard examples.

"Pure" here is extreme. Electronic-grade silicon is about 99.9999999% pure, often called "nine nines". That is roughly one foreign atom for every billion silicon atoms. Chips are made on thin, polished discs of this crystal called wafers.

In topic 1.2 we saw that silicon has a band gap of 1.12 eV. Now we look inside the crystal and answer three questions:

  • Where do the free carriers in pure silicon come from?
  • What is a hole, and how can an empty space move?
  • How many carriers are there, and how does that number change with temperature?

The animation zooms from a wafer down to its atoms. Every atom you see is silicon.

Key points

  • Intrinsic = pure: no impurities added on purpose.
  • Examples: pure Si and pure Ge.
  • Electronic-grade Si is about 99.9999999% pure ("nine nines").

Crystal structure and covalent bonds

Silicon sits in group IV of the periodic table, so each atom has 4 valence electrons in its outer shell. In a silicon crystal every atom sits at the centre of a tetrahedron (a triangular pyramid), with 4 nearest neighbours at its corners. This 3D pattern is called the diamond lattice.

Each atom shares one valence electron with each neighbour, and the neighbour shares one back. A shared pair of electrons holding two atoms together is a covalent bond. Every atom therefore has 4 bonds. Counting both electrons in each bond, it has 8 electrons around it. Eight outer electrons is a very stable arrangement, which is why the crystal holds together so well.

Drawing the real 3D lattice is hard, so we use a flat 2D grid: each atom has 4 bonds (left, right, up, down), and each bond is drawn as two electrons. Keep in mind that this is a simplified picture of the 3D structure.

At 0 K (absolute zero) there is no thermal energy at all. Every bond is complete and every valence electron is locked in its bond. No electron is free to move, so if we connect a battery, no current flows. Pure silicon at 0 K behaves like an insulator.

Tap any atom to see its 4 bonds and the 8 electrons it shares.

Key points

  • Si has 4 valence electrons and bonds to 4 neighbours (diamond lattice, tetrahedral in 3D).
  • A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, one from each atom; each atom then has 8 around it.
  • At 0 K every electron is locked in a bond: no free carriers, no current. Pure Si is an insulator at 0 K.

Thermal generation: electron-hole pairs

Above 0 K the atoms in the crystal vibrate, and the vibrations get stronger as temperature rises. This vibration energy is shared out at random. Usually a bonded electron gets only a little of it. Once in a while, though, one gets enough, at least Eg = 1.12 eV in silicon, to break out of its bond.

When that happens, two carriers appear at once:

  • a free electron, which can now wander through the whole crystal, and
  • a hole, the empty place left behind in the broken bond.

This event is called electron-hole pair (EHP) generation. Because heat causes it, we call it thermal generation.

The band diagram from topic 1.2 tells the same story in terms of energy. A bonded electron is an electron in the valence band. Breaking the bond means the electron jumps up across the gap into the conduction band. The empty state it leaves in the valence band is the hole. The lattice and the band diagram are two views of one event, which is why they flash together in the animation.

Every generation event makes exactly one electron and one hole, so the counts always match. In an intrinsic semiconductor n = p, where n is the free electron concentration and p the hole concentration (both per cm³).

Temperature matters a lot. The number of pairs present follows exp(−Eg/2kT), where k is Boltzmann's constant. Because this is an exponential, a small rise in temperature breaks many more bonds. Slide to 0 K and generation stops completely.

Key points

  • Heat breaks a bond if an electron gains at least Eg (1.12 eV for Si).
  • Each break makes a pair: one free electron + one hole. So n = p.
  • Band view: electron jumps from VB to CB, leaving an empty VB state (the hole).
  • Higher T → many more pairs (exponential, exp(−Eg/2kT)).

What is a hole and how does it move?

A hole is not a particle that exists on its own. It is simply a missing electron: a bond that should hold two electrons but holds only one.

That gap does not stay put. An electron in a neighbouring bond is already bound in the crystal, so it needs very little energy to slide sideways into the empty place. It does not have to escape into the conduction band to do this. When it jumps, its old bond is now the one missing an electron. The hole has moved one bond, opposite to the electron's jump.

Repeat this and the hole travels through the crystal. Many different bonded electrons each take one short step one way, while the hole moves steadily the other way. It is far easier to follow one hole than all those electrons, so we treat the hole as a carrier in its own right.

What charge does it carry? The crystal is neutral when every bond is full. Removing one electron (charge −q) leaves that region with a net charge of +q (q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C). So a hole behaves like a positive carrier. Like any positive charge, it is pushed in the direction of an electric field. Try "Apply field E" below: the jumps become biased and the hole drifts along E.

Silicon therefore has two kinds of carriers: free electrons moving in the conduction band, and holes moving in the valence band. Because holes move by this hand-over process, they are slower. In Si the hole mobility is only about one third of the electron mobility.

Key points

  • A hole is a missing electron in a bond, not a separate particle.
  • A neighbouring bonded electron fills it, so the hole moves opposite to the electron's jump.
  • A hole acts as a +q carrier and drifts along E.
  • Two carriers: electrons (CB) and holes (VB); holes are slower (μp ≈ μn/3 in Si).

Recombination and equilibrium

Generation has an opposite. When a free electron meets a hole, it can drop back into the empty bond. The bond is whole again and both carriers vanish. This is recombination. The energy released is about Eg. In silicon it comes out mostly as heat (lattice vibrations), not as light.

The two processes depend on different things:

  • Generation depends only on temperature. Heat breaks bonds at a steady rate, whether there are many carriers or none.
  • Recombination needs an electron to find a hole, so it happens more often when both are plentiful. Its rate grows with n × p.

Start from zero carriers. At first there is nothing to recombine, so n rises. As n and p grow, recombination speeds up until it matches generation. From then on, as many pairs are destroyed each second as are created, and n stays constant on average. This balance is thermal equilibrium, and the value n settles at is ni.

Raise the temperature and generation increases, so the balance settles at a higher n. Lower it and n settles lower.

Each carrier survives only a short time between being generated and recombining. This average time is its lifetime, τ. In the animation τ is a few seconds; in real silicon it ranges from nanoseconds to milliseconds, depending on purity.

Key points

  • Recombination: an electron fills a hole and both disappear; energy ≈ Eg is released (mostly heat in Si).
  • Generation depends on T only; recombination grows with n·p.
  • Thermal equilibrium: generation rate = recombination rate, so n = p = ni stays constant.
  • Average time a carrier survives = lifetime τ.

Conduction in an intrinsic semiconductor

Connect a battery across a bar of pure silicon. The voltage sets up an electric field E inside the bar, pointing from the + contact to the − contact.

  • Free electrons (charge −q) are pushed against the field, so they drift towards the + contact.
  • Holes (charge +q) are pushed along the field, so they drift towards the − contact.

They move in opposite directions, but their charges are also opposite. Negative charge moving left is the same current as positive charge moving right. So both currents point the same way, along E, and they add:

total current = electron current + hole current

How well the bar conducts is measured by its conductivity:

σi = q · ni · (μn + μp)

μn and μp are the mobilities: how fast each carrier drifts per unit field. In Si at 300 K, μn ≈ 1350 and μp ≈ 480 cm²/V·s, so electrons carry about 74% of the current. Mobility is covered in topic 1.7.

Putting in numbers, the resistivity ρi = 1/σi of intrinsic silicon at 300 K is about 3.4 × 10³ Ω·m with ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Many textbooks give 2.3 × 10³ Ω·m because they use ni = 1.5 × 10¹⁰. Either way it is about 10¹¹ times higher than copper (1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m), simply because there are so few carriers.

Key points

  • Electrons drift against E (towards +); holes drift along E (towards −).
  • Both currents point along E and add: I = In + Ip.
  • σi = q·ni·(μn + μp); Si at 300 K: ρi ≈ 3.4 × 10³ Ω·m (2.3 × 10³ with ni = 1.5 × 10¹⁰).

Intrinsic carrier concentration and temperature

The intrinsic carrier concentration ni is the number of free electrons per cm³ in a pure semiconductor at equilibrium. Since n = p, it is also the number of holes per cm³. It is given by:

ni² = Nc · Nv · exp(−Eg/kT)  ⇔  ni = √(NcNv) · exp(−Eg/2kT)
  • Nc and Nv are the effective densities of states of the conduction and valence bands: roughly, how many places per cm³ are available for electrons and holes near the band edges. Both grow as T3/2.
  • k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K is Boltzmann's constant; kT = 0.0259 eV at 300 K.

Values at 300 K: Si ni ≈ 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ (many older textbooks use 1.5 × 10¹⁰), Ge ≈ 2.4 × 10¹³ cm⁻³, GaAs ≈ 2 × 10⁶ cm⁻³.

Compare that with the number of atoms. Silicon has about 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³, so at room temperature only about 1 atom in 5 × 10¹² gives up an electron.

Two things control ni, both through the exponential. Band gap: Ge's smaller gap (0.66 eV) gives over 1000 times more carriers than Si, and GaAs's larger gap (1.42 eV) gives far fewer. Temperature: in Si, ni roughly doubles for every 9 K rise near room temperature, and grows about 100 000 times between 300 K and 600 K.

This is the problem with intrinsic silicon. It has too few carriers to make useful devices, and the number swings wildly with temperature. The solution is to add a tiny, controlled amount of impurity: doping (topic 1.4).

Drag across the plot (or use the slider) to read ni and the intrinsic resistivity at any temperature.

Key points

  • ni² = NcNv·exp(−Eg/kT), with Nc, Nv ∝ T3/2.
  • At 300 K: Si ≈ 1 × 10¹⁰, Ge ≈ 2.4 × 10¹³, GaAs ≈ 2 × 10⁶ cm⁻³.
  • Only 1 Si atom in about 5 × 10¹² gives a free electron at 300 K.
  • Smaller Eg or higher T → far more carriers. Intrinsic Si is impractical, so we dope it.
