Mobility, Conductivity and the Hall Effect

How easily carriers move, how that sets the resistance of a sample, and how a magnet lets us measure it all

Introduction

In topic 1.6 we saw that an electric field makes carriers drift with a velocity vd = μ E. There the mobility μ was simply given as a number. This topic looks inside that number and answers three questions:

  • 1. Mobility. What decides μ, and why is it different for electrons and holes?
  • 2. Conductivity. How do the number of carriers and their mobility together decide how well a sample conducts?
  • 3. The Hall effect. How can we measure the carrier type, the carrier concentration and the mobility of a real sample?

Key points

  • Drift velocity vd = μ E; this topic explains what sets μ.
  • Conductivity depends on both carrier number and mobility.
  • The Hall effect measures carrier type, concentration and mobility.

Mobility

Mobility μ tells us how easily a carrier moves through the crystal when an electric field pushes it. It is the drift velocity per unit field:

μ = vd/E unit: cm2/V·s

Where does it come from? Between two collisions a carrier is pushed by the force qE, so it accelerates at a = qE/m*. On average it has a time τ (the mean free time) to pick up speed before a collision resets it. Its average extra velocity is therefore vd = (qτ/m*) E, which gives

μ = q τ/m*

Here m* is the effective mass: inside the crystal a carrier does not respond to a force like a free electron, and m* describes how "heavy" it behaves. So mobility is high when the carrier is light and when it travels a long time between collisions.

In silicon, electrons behave as lighter particles than holes (roughly 0.26 m0 against about 0.4 m0, where m0 is the free-electron mass), and they also go a little longer between collisions. Both effects favour electrons, so electrons are more mobile.

Values at 300 K for lightly doped material, in cm²/V·s:

  • Si: μn ≈ 1350, μp ≈ 480
  • Ge: μn ≈ 3900, μp ≈ 1900
  • GaAs: μn ≈ 8500, μp ≈ 400

Higher mobility means carriers respond faster, so devices can switch faster. That is why GaAs is used in high-frequency circuits, and why an NMOS transistor (electrons) is faster than a PMOS transistor (holes) of the same size. In CMOS logic the PMOS is usually made two to three times wider to make up for this.

Key points

  • μ = vd/E, unit cm²/V·s.
  • μ = qτ/m*: light carriers with long times between collisions are most mobile.
  • Electrons beat holes in Si, Ge and GaAs (Si: 1350 vs 480).
  • High mobility → fast devices: GaAs for high frequency, NMOS faster than PMOS.

What limits mobility: scattering

Every collision interrupts the carrier's acceleration and throws away the velocity it gained from the field. More collisions mean a shorter τ, and so a lower mobility. In a semiconductor there are two main things to collide with.

1. Lattice (phonon) scattering. The atoms of the crystal are always vibrating, and a carrier is scattered by these vibrations (a quantum of lattice vibration is called a phonon). A hotter crystal vibrates more strongly, so the carrier scatters more often. Lattice-limited mobility therefore falls as temperature rises, roughly as

μL ∝ T-3/2

2. Impurity (ionised dopant) scattering. Every donor or acceptor that has given up its carrier is a fixed charged ion. A passing carrier is pulled or pushed by its charge and deflected. More doping means more ions and more scattering. A fast (hot) carrier rushes past an ion and is deflected less, so this mobility rises with temperature, roughly as

μI ∝ fracT3/2NI

where NI is the concentration of ionised impurities. The two effects add up like resistors in parallel. This is Matthiessen's rule:

1/μ = 1/μL + 1/μI

The smaller of the two mobilities wins. At low temperature, impurity scattering dominates; in lightly and moderately doped silicon at room temperature and above, lattice scattering dominates. So the total mobility first rises and then falls with temperature. Heavy doping lowers the mobility a lot: near 1018 cm⁻³ the electron mobility in Si falls to a few hundred cm²/V·s.

Key points

  • More collisions → shorter τ → lower mobility.
  • Lattice scattering grows with temperature: μL ∝ T-3/2.
  • Impurity scattering grows with doping and weakens when hot: μI ∝ T3/2/NI.
  • Matthiessen's rule: 1/μ = 1/μL + 1/μI; the smaller one dominates.
  • Heavily doped Si (~ 1018 cm⁻³) has μn of only a few hundred cm²/V·s.

Conductivity and resistivity

Conductivity σ combines how many carriers there are with how easily they move. Both electrons and holes contribute:

σ = q (nμn + pμp) ρ = 1/σ R = ρ L/A

Here ρ is the resistivity (in Ω·cm) and R is the resistance of a bar of length L and cross-section area A.

Intrinsic Si at 300 K has n = p = ni ≈ 1010 cm⁻³. Then σ ≈ 2.9×10-6 S/cm and ρ ≈ 3.4×105 Ω·cm (about 3.4×103 Ω·m). Pure silicon is a poor conductor.

N-type Si with ND = 1016 cm⁻³: now n ≈ ND and the hole term is negligible, so σ ≈ qNDμn ≈ 2.2 S/cm and ρ ≈ 0.46 Ω·cm. A tiny amount of doping (one atom in about five million) lowers the resistivity by nearly a million times.

In doped material the majority carriers dominate: σ ≈ qNDμn for N-type, σ ≈ qNAμp for P-type. Because doping can change ρ over many orders of magnitude, it is the main way resistors are made inside integrated circuits (diffused and well resistors).

Temperature. In the normal (extrinsic) range the number of carriers is fixed by the doping, but mobility falls as lattice scattering grows. So the resistance rises slightly with temperature, like a metal. At very high temperature, intrinsic carriers (ni grows very fast) outnumber the dopants and the resistance falls sharply.

Key points

  • σ = q(nμn + pμp), ρ = 1/σ, R = ρ L/A.
  • Intrinsic Si: ρ ≈ 3.4×105 Ω·cm. N-type 1016: ρ ≈ 0.46 Ω·cm.
  • Doped material: the majority term dominates, σ ≈ qNDμn or qNAμp.
  • Extrinsic range: R rises slightly with T. Intrinsic range: R falls sharply.

The Hall effect: how it works

The set-up. Take a rectangular slab of semiconductor. A current I flows along its length (the x direction). A magnetic field B is applied at right angles to its large face (the z direction). The slab has width w (along y) and thickness t (along B).

The sideways push. A charge moving through a magnetic field feels the Lorentz force

F = q ( v × B)

which is at right angles to both its velocity and B. So the moving carriers are pushed sideways, toward one of the side faces (the y direction).

Charge piles up. Carriers collect on that face, and the opposite face is left with the opposite charge (the fixed dopant ions that the carriers came from). These two charged faces create a sideways electric field across the slab, called the Hall field EH.

Balance. The Hall field pushes the carriers back. The pile-up keeps growing until the electric force exactly cancels the magnetic force:

qEH = q vd B ⇒ EH = vd B

After that, new carriers flow straight along the slab again. This takes well under a nanosecond in a real sample.

The Hall voltage. Using vd = I/(q n w t) for N-type material, the voltage across the width is VH = EH w:

VH = I B/q n t

For P-type, replace n with p. Fewer carriers means each must drift faster to carry the same current, so the Hall voltage is larger in lightly doped material.

The key point: the sign. For the same current direction and the same B, electrons and holes are pushed to the same face. Electrons move backwards but carry negative charge, and the two minus signs cancel in q( v × B). In our picture (I along +x, B along +z) both go to the front face. But electrons make that face negative and holes make it positive. So the Hall voltage has opposite sign for N-type and P-type, and its sign tells you the carrier type. Reversing B or I also reverses the sign.

Key points

  • Lorentz force q( v× B) pushes moving carriers to one side face.
  • Piled-up charge creates the Hall field EH; at balance EH = vd B.
  • VH = IB/(qnt) (use p for P-type): fewer carriers → bigger VH.
  • Electrons and holes go to the same face, so VH has opposite sign for N and P.
  • Reversing B or I also flips the sign.

Hall measurements and applications

The Hall coefficient RH collects the result into one number that depends only on the material:

RH = EH/J B = −1/q n (N-type), RH = +1/q p (P-type)

Measuring VH with known I, B and t gives RH = VH t/(IB). From it we read off:

  • Carrier type from the sign of RH: negative means electrons (N-type), positive means holes (P-type).
  • Carrier concentration: n = 1/(q| RH|) (or p).
  • Mobility, by also measuring the resistivity: μH = σ| RH| = | RH|/ρ. This is called the Hall mobility. It is close to the drift mobility; strictly they differ by a factor of order one (the Hall factor, between about 1 and 2) which this page takes as 1.

This combined Hall and resistivity measurement is the standard laboratory method for characterising semiconductor wafers.

Applications. Because VH is proportional to B, a small slab with a known current is a Hall-effect sensor. Such sensors measure magnetic fields and, from the field around a wire, electric current without touching it. They detect position and speed: the rotor position in brushless DC motors, wheel speed in car anti-lock brakes. They also act as contactless switches, for example sensing a magnet when a phone cover or laptop lid is closed.

Key points

  • RH = −1/(qn) for N-type, +1/(qp) for P-type.
  • Sign of RH → carrier type; 1/(q| RH|) → concentration.
  • With resistivity: Hall mobility μH = σ| RH|.
  • Hall sensors: magnetic field, contactless current sensing, position and speed, lid and cover switches.
