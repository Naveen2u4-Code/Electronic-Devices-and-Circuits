TOPIC 1.2

Energy Band Diagrams

From one silicon atom to a full crystal, and how electrons become free

Why we need band diagrams

In topic 1.1 we saw that a material conducts only if it has electrons that are free to move. A band diagram is the tool engineers use to show this, using energy.

The vertical axis of a band diagram is electron energy. We measure it in electron-volts (eV). One electron-volt is the energy an electron gains when it moves through a potential difference of 1 volt: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J. This unit is tiny, which is exactly why it suits single electrons. Higher on the diagram means more energy.

At a glance, a band diagram tells us three things: where electrons are allowed to be, where they are forbidden, and how much energy it takes to make a bound electron free.

To understand where the diagram comes from, we will start with a single atom and build up, step by step, to a full crystal.

Key points

  • A band diagram shows electron energy on the vertical axis. Higher means more energy.
  • 1 eV is the energy an electron gains moving through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J.
  • It shows where electrons are allowed, where they are forbidden, and how much energy frees them.

Energy levels in a single atom

In an isolated atom an electron cannot have just any energy. It can only sit on certain fixed energy levels, the shells and subshells named 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p and so on. We say the energy is quantised. Energies between the allowed levels are simply not allowed for the electron.

Silicon has 14 electrons, arranged as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². The 10 inner electrons (1s, 2s, 2p) sit on deep levels and are held very tightly by the nucleus. The 4 outer electrons in 3s and 3p are the valence electrons. They are the ones that form bonds, and later the ones that can become free.

The levels are drawn as a ladder. An electron can climb to a higher, empty level only if it absorbs exactly the right amount of energy, for example from a photon whose energy matches the difference between the two levels. A photon with any other energy cannot be absorbed and passes by. When the excited electron falls back down, it releases the same energy, often as a new photon.

Try it below. Tap any electron on the atom to find it on the ladder. Then send photons of different energies at a 3p electron. Only the energies that match a step on the ladder are absorbed.

Key points

  • In an atom, electron energy is quantised: only fixed levels are allowed.
  • Si: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². 10 tightly bound inner electrons, 4 valence electrons (3s² 3p²).
  • An electron jumps up only by absorbing exactly the energy difference, and gives it back when it falls.

From energy levels to energy bands

A real crystal is not one atom. A single cm³ of silicon holds about 5 × 1022 atoms. What happens to the energy levels when so many atoms sit side by side?

The key rule is the Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in the same system can have exactly the same quantum state.

When atoms are far apart, each atom is its own system and keeps its own identical set of levels. When they come close, their outer electron clouds overlap and the atoms become one system. Now the identical levels are not allowed to stay identical, so each level splits into slightly different levels. Two atoms turn each level into 2. N atoms turn each level into N levels. For N ≈ 1022 these levels are so close together that they form a practically continuous band of allowed energies.

Inner levels (1s, 2s, 2p) hardly split, because inner electron clouds are small and do not overlap. The outer levels (3s, 3p) split the most.

In silicon something special happens. As the atoms approach, the 3s band (2N states) and the 3p band (6N states) widen until they merge into one band of 8N states. At the actual spacing in the crystal, this band splits again into two bands of 4N states each, with a range of energies between them where no state exists. Silicon has exactly 4N valence electrons, so at 0 K they fill the lower band completely and leave the upper band empty.

The lower, full band is the valence band. The upper, empty band is the conduction band. The empty range between them is the forbidden gap, or band gap.

Key points

  • Pauli exclusion: overlapping atoms form one system, so identical levels must split.
  • N atoms → each level splits into N levels; for a crystal (≈ 5 × 1022 cm−3) they form a continuous band.
  • Inner levels barely split; outer 3s and 3p split the most.
  • In Si the 3s and 3p bands merge, then split into two bands of 4N states. The 4N valence electrons fill the lower one at 0 K.
  • Lower full band = valence band, upper empty band = conduction band, between = band gap.

Reading a band diagram

The full picture has many bands, but for electronics only two matter, so the simplified band diagram keeps just those two.

  • Valence band (VB): the highest band that is full at 0 K. Its electrons are the ones locked in covalent bonds. Its top edge is called Ev.
  • Conduction band (CB): the lowest band that is empty at 0 K. An electron that reaches it is free to move through the crystal and carry current. Its bottom edge is called Ec.
  • Band gap: Eg = Ec − Ev. In a perfect crystal no electron can have an energy inside it. It is the minimum energy needed to free a bonded electron.

When an electron jumps from the VB to the CB, it leaves an empty state behind in the VB. This empty state is called a hole. A neighbouring bonded electron can move into it, so the hole moves too, and it behaves like a positive charge carrier. One jump always makes one electron–hole pair.

An electric field pushes the two carriers in opposite directions: electrons move against the field and holes move with it. Both movements give current in the same direction, the direction of the field, because the charges are opposite.

The horizontal axis of this simplified diagram is position in the crystal. Flat, straight lines mean the material is the same everywhere. Later, in PN junctions, you will see these lines bend.

Key points

  • VB: highest band full at 0 K, top edge Ev. CB: lowest band empty at 0 K, bottom edge Ec.
  • Eg = Ec − Ev: no states inside; minimum energy to free a bonded electron.
  • Each jump makes an electron in the CB and a hole in the VB. Both carry current.
  • In a field, electrons and holes move opposite ways but their currents add.
  • Horizontal axis = position. Flat lines = uniform material.

Insulator, semiconductor and metal on a band diagram

Now we can explain the three kinds of material from topic 1.1 with one picture. The only thing that changes is the band gap.

Insulator. The gap is very wide, more than about 4–5 eV (SiO2 ≈ 9 eV, diamond ≈ 5.5 eV). The thermal energy available at room temperature is only about kT ≈ 0.026 eV. That is hundreds of times too small, so practically no electron crosses and the CB stays empty.

Semiconductor. The gap is small: Ge 0.66 eV, Si 1.12 eV and GaAs 1.42 eV (values at 300 K). Heat lifts a small number of electrons into the CB, each leaving a hole in the VB. The hotter it gets, the more electron–hole pairs there are, and the better it conducts.

Metal. The bands overlap, or the highest band is only partly filled. There is no gap, so there are plenty of free electrons at any temperature, even at 0 K.

Key points

  • Insulator: Eg > ~4–5 eV (SiO2 ≈ 9, diamond ≈ 5.5). kT ≈ 0.026 eV is far too small; CB stays empty.
  • Semiconductor: small Eg (Ge 0.66, Si 1.12, GaAs 1.42 eV at 300 K). Heat makes a few electron–hole pairs, more when hotter.
  • Metal: bands overlap or are partly filled. No gap, many free electrons always.

How electrons cross the gap

An electron needs at least Eg of energy to cross from the VB to the CB. There are two main ways to get it.

Thermal excitation. The atoms of a crystal are always vibrating, and these lattice vibrations pass energy to electrons. The chance that an electron gets enough energy to cross falls very steeply as the gap gets bigger, roughly like e−Eg/2kT. At 300 K, 2kT ≈ 0.052 eV, so making Eg bigger by just 0.1 eV divides the rate by about e2 ≈ 7. That is why Ge, Si and GaAs behave so differently even though their gaps look similar.

Optical excitation. A photon carries energy E = hc/λ. In handy units, λ(nm) = 1240 / E(eV). If E ≥ Eg, the photon can be absorbed and lift an electron across, making an electron–hole pair. If E < Eg, the photon passes straight through. Silicon (Eg = 1.12 eV) absorbs light with wavelength shorter than about 1107 nm, which includes all visible light. Any extra energy above Eg is quickly lost as heat while the electron settles to Ec.

Recombination. A CB electron can fall back into a VB hole. Both carriers disappear and about Eg of energy is released. In silicon and germanium this energy mainly becomes heat. In direct-gap materials like GaAs it often comes out as light, which is how LEDs work (topic 3.2).

Note: Eg itself shrinks slightly as temperature rises. For silicon it is about 1.17 eV at 0 K and 1.12 eV at 300 K. The animation includes this change.

Key points

  • Thermal: rate ∝ e−Eg/2kT, so a slightly bigger gap means far fewer carriers.
  • Optical: absorbed only if E = hc/λ ≥ Eg. λ(nm) = 1240/E(eV). Si absorbs λ < ~1107 nm.
  • Recombination releases ≈ Eg: mostly heat in Si/Ge, light in direct-gap GaAs (LEDs).
  • Eg falls slightly with temperature: Si ≈ 1.17 eV at 0 K, 1.12 eV at 300 K.
