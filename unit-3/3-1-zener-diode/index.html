TOPIC 3.1

Zener Diode: Construction and V-I Characteristics

A diode built to live in breakdown: how doping sets VZ, and what the curve tells you

What a Zener diode is, and how it is built

An ordinary rectifier diode is never meant to reach reverse breakdown. A ZENER DIODE is a silicon pn junction designed to work there. In reverse breakdown its voltage stays almost constant over a wide range of current. That makes it a voltage reference, and it is the basis of the simple voltage regulator in topic 3.2.

Breakdown itself does not damage a junction. A diode fails when the power V·I heats it too much, or when breakdown concentrates at one weak spot. A Zener diode avoids both. Its junction is uniform, so breakdown spreads evenly over the whole area. The external circuit limits the current so that VZ·IZ stays below the rated power.

In use, a Zener is reverse biased: the cathode (K) is held more positive than the anode (A). Its symbol is a diode with the cathode bar bent at the ends, like a letter Z. In forward bias it behaves like any silicon diode, with a drop of about 0.7 V.

The breakdown voltage VZ is set during manufacture by the DOPING. Zener diodes are made with an abrupt junction and heavy doping. Take a one-sided p⁺n junction, where the lightly doped side has doping N. The depletion width and the peak electric field at a reverse voltage VR are:

W = √[ 2εs(Vbi + VR) / (qN) ]   and   Emax = √[ 2qN(Vbi + VR) / εs ]

Heavier doping makes the depletion region NARROWER, so the same voltage produces a STRONGER field. Breakdown starts when the field reaches a critical value, so heavier doping means a LOWER VZ. For an abrupt silicon junction, a widely used empirical fit is:

VZ ≈ 60 V × (N / 1016 cm−3)−3/4

For example, N = 1017 cm−3 gives VZ ≈ 10.7 V, and N = 1018 cm−3 gives about 1.9 V. Commercial Zener diodes cover roughly 2.4 V to 200 V, with power ratings from about 250 mW to 50 W.

Key points

  • A Zener diode is a heavily doped, abrupt silicon junction designed to work in reverse breakdown.
  • Breakdown is not destructive if the current, and so the power, is limited by the circuit.
  • Heavier doping gives a narrower W and a stronger field, so a lower VZ: VZ ≈ 60 V·(N/1016)−3/4.
  • In use the cathode is positive (reverse bias). In forward bias it acts as an ordinary diode.

Zener breakdown: tunnelling through a thin barrier

When both sides are very heavily doped (around 1018 cm−3 or more), the depletion region is only a few tens of nanometres wide. A reverse bias of a volt or two then produces a field near 106 V/cm.

Look at the band diagram. Reverse bias lowers the n side, and soon the filled valence band on the p side lines up with the empty conduction band on the n side. Only the forbidden gap separates them, and the gap is now very thin. Its horizontal width is roughly:

d ≈ Eg / (qE)

Electrons can then cross from the p-side valence band to the n-side conduction band by quantum-mechanical TUNNELLING, without having to climb over the gap. This is ZENER BREAKDOWN. It is sometimes called field emission, because the strong field pulls electrons straight out of their covalent bonds. No carrier multiplication is involved.

The tunnelling probability depends on the field roughly as exp(−constant/E). As the field increases, the current rises steeply but not abruptly, so Zener breakdown gives a fairly SOFT KNEE. It dominates in diodes with VZ below about 4Eg/q ≈ 4.5 V.

Key points

  • Very heavy doping on both sides gives a W of tens of nm and a field near 106 V/cm.
  • The filled p-side valence band faces the empty n-side conduction band across a thin gap, d ≈ Eg/qE.
  • Electrons tunnel straight through the gap. There is no multiplication.
  • The knee is soft. This mechanism dominates for VZ below about 4.5 V.

Avalanche breakdown: carriers that multiply

With lighter doping, the depletion region is wider, around a micrometre. The field never becomes strong enough for tunnelling. Instead, a carrier crossing the depletion region is accelerated by the field and gains kinetic energy between collisions with the lattice.

Near breakdown, a carrier can gain more than about Eg of energy before a collision. It can then break a covalent bond and create a new electron-hole pair. This is called IMPACT IONISATION. The new electron and hole are accelerated in turn and can ionise again, so one carrier sets off a chain reaction: an AVALANCHE.

The growth is described by the multiplication factor M, the number of carriers that leave the depletion region for each one that enters. An empirical form is:

M = 1 / [ 1 − (VR / VBR)n ]   with n ≈ 3 to 6 for silicon

M stays close to 1 at low voltage and then shoots towards infinity as VR approaches VBR. The current is then limited only by the external resistor. The knee is therefore SHARP. Avalanche dominates for VZ above about 6Eg/q ≈ 6.7 V. Between about 4.5 V and 6.7 V, both mechanisms contribute.

Despite the name, most "Zener diodes" above about 6 V actually break down by avalanche. The name is kept for the whole family.

Key points

  • Lighter doping gives a wide W, about 1 µm. Carriers gain energy as the field accelerates them.
  • Impact ionisation creates new electron-hole pairs, which ionise in turn: an avalanche.
  • M = 1/[1 − (VR/VBR)n] blows up at VBR, so the knee is sharp.
  • Avalanche dominates above about 6.7 V. Between 4.5 V and 6.7 V both mechanisms act.

The V-I characteristic

In the forward direction the Zener diode behaves like an ordinary silicon diode, conducting from about 0.6 to 0.7 V. In the reverse direction only a tiny leakage current IR flows, typically under a microampere, until the voltage reaches the knee. Beyond the knee the current rises sharply while the voltage barely changes. This near-vertical part is the Zener's working region.

A Zener datasheet describes this region with a few numbers:

  • VZ: the Zener voltage, measured at a stated test current IZT.
  • IZK: the knee current, the minimum current that keeps the diode in breakdown. Below it, regulation is lost.
  • IZM: the maximum current, set by the power rating: IZM = PZM / VZ.
  • rZ: the dynamic (Zener) resistance, the inverse slope of the curve in breakdown. It is a few ohms to a few tens of ohms at IZT, and much larger near the knee.

rZ = ΔVZ / ΔIZ   and   VZ(IZ) ≈ VZ0 + rZ·IZ

Example: a 5.1 V, 500 mW Zener has IZM = 0.5 / 5.1 ≈ 98 mA. If rZ = 10 Ω, a change in current from 10 mA to 30 mA changes VZ by only 10 × 0.02 = 0.2 V.

Notice in the plot how the knee changes with VZ. The 3.3 V diode (Zener effect) has a soft, rounded knee. The 12 V diode (avalanche) has a sharp one. In the plot, the forward and reverse halves are drawn to different scales so that both can be seen.

Key points

  • Forward: like an ordinary diode, about 0.7 V. Reverse: tiny leakage until the knee.
  • In breakdown the voltage stays near VZ while the current changes a lot.
  • Stay between IZK (keeps regulating) and IZM = PZM/VZ (keeps cool).
  • rZ = ΔVZ/ΔIZ: small at IZT, large near the knee.

Temperature coefficient

VZ drifts with temperature. The drift is given by the temperature coefficient, quoted either in mV/°C or as a percentage per °C:

TC = ΔVZ / ΔT   or   TC(%) = ΔVZ / (VZ·ΔT) × 100

The two breakdown mechanisms drift in opposite directions:

  • Zener (tunnelling), low VZ: NEGATIVE TC. A higher temperature slightly reduces the band gap and gives the valence electrons more energy, so tunnelling starts at a lower voltage and VZ falls.
  • Avalanche, high VZ: POSITIVE TC. A higher temperature makes the lattice vibrate more, so carriers collide more often and travel a shorter distance between collisions. They need a stronger field, and so a higher voltage, to gain the ionisation energy. VZ rises.

Near 5 to 6 V the two effects cancel and the TC is close to zero. Precision references use this. They also add a forward-biased diode, which has a TC of about −2 mV/°C, in series with a slightly higher-voltage Zener to cancel its positive drift.

Example: a 12 V Zener with TC ≈ +0.085 %/°C drifts by about 12 × 0.00085 ≈ +10 mV/°C. Heating it from 25 °C to 100 °C raises VZ by about 0.77 V. The values in the plot are typical. Always check the datasheet of the actual part.

Key points

  • Zener effect (below about 4.5 V): negative TC. VZ falls when hot.
  • Avalanche (above about 6.7 V): positive TC. VZ rises when hot.
  • Around 5 to 6 V the TC is near zero. A series forward diode (−2 mV/°C) can cancel the rest.
  • ΔVZ = TC × ΔT. A 12 V part gains about 0.77 V from 25 °C to 100 °C.

Summary

A Zener diode is a heavily doped, abrupt pn junction made to work in reverse breakdown. The doping sets VZ: heavier doping gives a narrower depletion region, a stronger field and a lower VZ. Two mechanisms cause the breakdown:

Zener breakdownAvalanche breakdown
DopingVery heavy (≳ 1018 cm−3)Moderate
Depletion widthTens of nmAround 1 µm
ProcessTunnelling through the gap (field emission)Impact ionisation and multiplication
Typical VZBelow about 4.5 VAbove about 6.7 V
KneeSoft, roundedSharp
Temperature coefficientNegativePositive

On the V-I curve, the diode is used between IZK and IZM = PZM/VZ, where the voltage stays near VZ and changes only by rZ·ΔIZ. Topic 3.2 puts it to work as a voltage regulator.

Key points

  • A Zener is a heavily doped junction used in reverse breakdown, with VZ set by the doping.
  • Below about 4.5 V: Zener effect (tunnelling), soft knee, negative TC.
  • Above about 6.7 V: avalanche (multiplication), sharp knee, positive TC.
  • Work between IZK and IZM. The voltage changes by only rZ·ΔIZ.
