Recap

Key points

  • A tunnel (Esaki) diode is a pn junction doped extremely heavily (≈ 1 in 103, against ≈ 1 in 108 for an ordinary diode), so the depletion region is only about 10 nm thick.
  • Electrons can tunnel through such a thin barrier (probability ≈ e−2κW) instead of climbing over it. Tunnelling is extremely fast, so the device works in picoseconds and at microwave frequencies.
  • Heavy doping puts the Fermi level inside the bands. Forward bias first aligns filled n-side states with empty p-side states, so current rises to the peak (Vp, Ip); further bias misaligns them, so current falls to the valley (Vv, Iv); beyond that, normal diode current takes over.
  • Between peak and valley dI/dV < 0: negative resistance. The peak-to-valley ratio Ip/Iv is a key figure of merit. In reverse it conducts heavily by tunnelling.
  • Negative resistance cancels the losses of an LC tank, so it makes oscillators (up to ~100 GHz) and amplifiers; a load line with three crossings gives a bistable fast switch.
  • Limitations: very small voltage swing, only two terminals (no input/output isolation), hard to integrate. Symbol: a diode with a hooked cathode bar.
