TOPIC 3.3

Light-Emitting Diode (LED)

When an electron falls across the band gap and gives its energy away as light

How a forward-biased junction makes light

A LIGHT-EMITTING DIODE is a pn junction that turns electrical energy directly into light. Its basic behaviour is the same as any forward-biased diode (topic 2.3). The applied voltage lowers the barrier, electrons from the n side are injected into the p side, and holes from the p side are injected into the n side.

Near the junction, the injected minority carriers recombine with majority carriers. Each recombination drops an electron from the conduction band to the valence band. The energy it loses is about the band gap Eg. In an ordinary silicon diode this energy ends up as heat. In an LED material it is released as a PHOTON. This is called radiative recombination, and light produced this way is called electroluminescence.

The photon energy, and therefore the colour, is fixed by the band gap:

Ephoton = hf = hc/λ ≈ Eg   ⇒   λ (nm) ≈ 1240 / Eg (eV)

Example: GaAs has Eg = 1.42 eV, giving λ ≈ 1240/1.42 ≈ 873 nm, in the infrared. A red LED at 630 nm needs Eg ≈ 1240/630 ≈ 1.97 eV. A blue LED at 465 nm needs about 2.67 eV.

A wider band gap gives a shorter wavelength, which means bluer light. Choosing the colour of an LED therefore means choosing (and alloying) the semiconductor to get the right band gap.

Key points

  • Forward bias injects electrons and holes across the junction, where they recombine.
  • In an LED, recombination releases a photon of energy ≈ Eg (electroluminescence).
  • λ (nm) ≈ 1240 / Eg (eV). A wider gap gives a bluer colour.
  • Red needs about 2.0 eV, blue about 2.7 eV, and GaAs (1.42 eV) emits infrared.

Why silicon does not glow: direct and indirect band gaps

An electron in a crystal has both an energy E and a crystal momentum, written as the wave vector k. A transition must conserve both. A photon carries plenty of energy but almost no momentum, so an electron can emit a photon only if it falls straight down on the E-k diagram, without changing k.

  • In a DIRECT band gap semiconductor, such as GaAs, InGaN or AlGaInP, the bottom of the conduction band sits directly above the top of the valence band, at the same k. Electrons can drop straight down and emit light efficiently.
  • In an INDIRECT band gap semiconductor, such as silicon and germanium, the conduction band minimum is at a different k. To recombine, the electron must also hand over momentum to a lattice vibration (a phonon). That three-particle process is unlikely, so most electrons instead recombine through defect levels in the gap and give their energy to the lattice as heat.

This is why ordinary silicon and germanium diodes give no useful light, and why LEDs are made from compound semiconductors of groups III and V. Older green and yellow LEDs used GaP, which is indirect. Nitrogen doping adds traps that let it emit a little light, but with low efficiency.

Key points

  • A photon has energy but almost no momentum, so light needs a vertical transition on the E-k diagram.
  • Direct gap (GaAs, InGaN, AlGaInP): CB minimum above the VB maximum, so efficient light.
  • Indirect gap (Si, Ge): a phonon is needed, so recombination mostly makes heat.
  • That is why LEDs use III-V compounds, not silicon.

Materials, colours and the emission spectrum

Alloying two compounds lets the band gap be tuned smoothly between their values. Common LED materials are:

  • GaAs (1.42 eV): infrared, about 870 to 940 nm. Used in remote controls, optocouplers and sensors.
  • GaAsP, AlGaAs: red, the classic early LEDs.
  • AlGaInP: bright red, orange, amber and yellow.
  • InGaN: green, blue and violet. More indium gives a smaller gap.
  • White LEDs: a blue InGaN chip coated with a yellow phosphor. The phosphor absorbs part of the blue and re-emits a broad yellow band, and blue plus yellow looks white.

An LED is not perfectly monochromatic. Electrons and holes are spread over an energy range of a few kT in their bands, so the emitted photons are spread too. The width of the spectrum is roughly:

ΔE ≈ 1.8 kT ≈ 47 meV at 300 K   ⇒   Δλ ≈ λ²·ΔE / 1240

For a red LED that is about 15 to 20 nm. That is far narrower than an incandescent lamp, but much broader than a laser. The dashed curve in the plot is the human eye's daytime sensitivity, which peaks at 555 nm. For the same optical power, green looks much brighter than deep red or blue, and infrared is invisible.

Key points

  • GaAs: infrared. AlGaInP: red to yellow. InGaN: green to blue.
  • White LED: a blue chip plus a yellow phosphor.
  • Spectral width ΔE ≈ 1.8kT, about 15 to 30 nm: nearly one colour, but not a laser.
  • The eye is most sensitive at 555 nm (green). IR is invisible.

The V-I characteristic and forward voltage

Electrically, an LED is a diode with the familiar exponential forward curve. Because its band gap is wider than silicon's, it needs a higher forward voltage before useful current flows. As a rule of thumb, VF is at least about Eg/q:

  • Infrared (GaAs): about 1.2 to 1.5 V
  • Red, orange and yellow: about 1.8 to 2.2 V
  • Green, blue and white (InGaN): about 2.9 to 3.4 V, a little above Eg/q because of extra resistance and barriers in these structures

Typical indicator LEDs are rated for about 20 mA continuous, with an absolute maximum of around 30 mA. The light output is roughly proportional to the forward current, which is why LEDs are controlled by current, not by voltage.

LEDs are delicate in reverse: their reverse breakdown voltage is low, typically only 3 to 5 V. In circuits where an LED may see a reverse voltage, a protection diode is placed in anti-parallel with it.

Key points

  • Same exponential curve as any diode, but VF ≳ Eg/q.
  • IR ≈ 1.2 V, red ≈ 2 V, green and blue ≈ 3 V or more. Silicon ≈ 0.7 V.
  • Rated about 20 mA. Light output is roughly proportional to current.
  • Low reverse breakdown, about 3 to 5 V: protect against reverse voltage.

Driving an LED: the series resistor

Because the curve is so steep, connecting an LED straight across a voltage source is a mistake. A few hundred millivolts too much would let the current run away and destroy it. The simplest fix is a series resistor that sets the current:

R = (VS − VF) / IF

Example: a red LED (VF ≈ 2.0 V) from a 5 V supply at IF = 15 mA needs R = (5 − 2.0)/0.015 = 200 Ω. Choose the next standard value up, 220 Ω, which gives IF ≈ (5 − 2.0)/220 ≈ 13.6 mA. The resistor dissipates I2R ≈ 41 mW, so a ¼ W part is fine, and the LED dissipates VF·IF ≈ 27 mW.

The same resistor gives a blue LED (VF ≈ 3.1 V) only about 9 mA, because less voltage is left across the resistor. With a 3.3 V supply a blue LED barely lights at all. A supply must exceed VF with some margin for a resistor to control the current well. For high-power lighting LEDs, a dedicated constant-current driver replaces the resistor.

Key points

  • Never drive an LED straight from a voltage source: the current would run away.
  • R = (VS − VF)/IF. Round up to a standard value.
  • Check the resistor power IF2R and the LED power VF·IF.
  • The supply must be well above VF, which matters most for blue and white LEDs.

Summary and applications

An LED is a forward-biased pn junction in a direct band gap semiconductor, where injected carriers recombine and emit photons of energy ≈ Eg, so λ ≈ 1240/Eg nm. The material sets the colour and the forward voltage (≈ 1.2 V for IR up to ≈ 3.3 V for blue). The current, set by a series resistor, sets the brightness.

Advantages: low voltage and power, small size, very long life (tens of thousands of hours), fast switching (nanoseconds), rugged, available in many colours, and highly efficient for lighting.

Limitations: they need current limiting, can be destroyed by small reverse voltages (about 5 V), their output falls as temperature rises, and VF varies from part to part.

Applications: indicator lamps, seven-segment and dot-matrix displays, backlights and general lighting (white LEDs), traffic signals, infrared remote controls, and optocouplers (an IR LED paired with a photodiode, topic 3.4). A seven-segment display contains seven LEDs (a to g) with either all anodes or all cathodes joined: a common-anode or common-cathode display.

Key points

  • Forward bias leads to recombination in a direct-gap material, which emits a photon with hf ≈ Eg.
  • The material sets the colour and VF. The current (via R) sets the brightness.
  • Efficient, fast and long-lived, but needs current limiting and protection from reverse voltage.
  • Uses: indicators, seven-segment displays, lighting, IR remotes, optocouplers.
