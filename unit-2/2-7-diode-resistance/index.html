TOPIC 2.7

Diode Resistance (Static and Dynamic)

Why a diode has two resistances, and why the one that matters for signals is just VT/I

Static (DC) resistance

A resistor has one resistance because its V-I graph is a straight line through the origin. The diode's graph is a steep exponential curve (topic 2.5), so the ratio V/I is different at every point. The operating point, set by the DC bias, is called the Q POINT (quiescent point). The STATIC or DC RESISTANCE at the Q point is simply

RD = VD / ID

Graphically, 1/RD is the slope of the CHORD drawn from the origin to the Q point. For the example silicon diode of the previous topics:

  • 1 µA at 0.52 V: RD ≈ 520 kΩ
  • 1 mA at 0.70 V: RD ≈ 700 Ω
  • 10 mA at 0.76 V: RD ≈ 76 Ω
  • 20 mA at 0.78 V: RD ≈ 39 Ω

The voltage barely changes while the current changes by thousands of times, so RD falls almost in proportion to 1/I. In reverse bias the same idea gives enormous values: about 11 pA at 5 V means RD ≈ 4.6 × 1011 Ω (460 GΩ). This large ratio of reverse to forward resistance is what makes the diode a good one-way valve.

Key points

  • RD = VD/ID at the Q point: the inverse slope of the chord from the origin.
  • It depends strongly on the Q point: about 700 Ω at 1 mA, 76 Ω at 10 mA.
  • Reverse bias: hundreds of GΩ. Forward/reverse ratio is what makes a diode a valve.

Dynamic (AC) resistance

Often the bias is fixed and a small change is added to it: a signal, ripple or noise. What matters then is how much the current changes for a small change in voltage. That is the DYNAMIC, AC or INCREMENTAL RESISTANCE, the inverse slope of the TANGENT at the Q point:

rd = dV/dI = ΔV/ΔI (small changes)

Differentiating I = IS[exp(V/ηVT) − 1] gives dI/dV = (I + IS)/ηVT, and since I ≫ IS in forward bias,

rd = ηVT / ID ≈ 26 mV / ID (η = 1, room temperature)

Remarkably, rd does not depend on the material, the area or the doping: only on the current. For the example diode (η = 1): 25.9 Ω at 1 mA, 2.6 Ω at 10 mA, 26 kΩ at 1 µA. Older books take η = 2 for silicon, which doubles these (52 Ω at 1 mA). Because VT = kT/q, rd also rises with temperature: about 32 Ω at 1 mA and 100 °C (topic 2.6).

The tangent is much steeper than the chord, so rd is much smaller than RD. Their ratio is RD/rd = VD/ηVT: about 27 at 1 mA.

Key points

  • rd = dV/dI: the inverse slope of the tangent at the Q point.
  • rd = ηVT/ID ≈ 26 mV/ID: 26 Ω at 1 mA, 2.6 Ω at 10 mA.
  • Independent of material and size; proportional to T.
  • rd ≪ RD; the ratio is VD/ηVT (about 27 at 1 mA).

Small signals ride on the bias

Add a small sine wave vd = Vp·sin ωt to the DC bias. The operating point slides up and down the curve, and the current swings around ID. If the swing is small, the curve is almost straight over that range, so the diode behaves like a plain resistor of value rd:

id = vd / rd

This is the SMALL-SIGNAL MODEL: for the signal, the diode is the resistance rd. At 1 mA (rd = 25.9 Ω), a 5 mV peak gives a predicted 0.19 mA; the exact peaks are +0.21 mA and −0.18 mA, about 10% off. At 10 mV they are +0.47 and −0.32 mA against a predicted 0.39 mA, and at 20 mV the wave is badly DISTORTED: +1.17 and −0.54 mA.

The condition is Vp ≪ ηVT: in practice, a peak of a few millivolts (Sedra and Smith use 10 mV as the limit). Larger signals see the curvature, and the positive half grows more than the negative half shrinks.

Key points

  • For a small signal on top of a bias, the diode acts as a resistor rd: id = vd/rd.
  • Valid while Vp ≪ ηVT (a few mV; 5 mV gives about 10% error).
  • Large signals are distorted: the positive half swells, the negative half shrinks.

Static and dynamic, side by side

On log-log axes the two resistances become two nearly parallel lines. rd = VT/I is an exact straight line falling one decade per decade of current. RD = V/I falls almost as fast, because V only creeps up by 60 mV per decade. The gap between them, RD/rd = V/VT, grows slowly: about 20 at 1 µA, 27 at 1 mA, 29 at 10 mA.

Which one to use depends on the question:

  • RD answers "what DC voltage drop at this DC current?" It is used for bias and power calculations (but usually it is easier to work with VD directly).
  • rd answers "how much does the current change for a small change of voltage?" It is used for signals, ripple and stability (the small-signal model).

Key points

  • Both resistances fall roughly as 1/I; rd exactly so.
  • RD/rd = V/VT: 20 to 30 over the useful range.
  • RD for DC (bias, power); rd for signals and small changes.

Bulk resistance and the piecewise-linear model

A real diode also has the ordinary resistance of its neutral p and n regions and contacts: the BULK RESISTANCE rB (the series resistance RS of topic 2.5), typically a fraction of an ohm to a few ohms. It simply adds:

r = ηVT/ID + rB

At low currents rd dominates; at high currents rB takes over. With rB = 2 Ω: 25.9 + 2 ≈ 28 Ω at 1 mA, 2.6 + 2 = 4.6 Ω at 10 mA, and 0.26 + 2 ≈ 2.3 Ω at 100 mA.

For hand calculation with large signals, the curve is replaced by a straight line through two points on it. Its inverse slope is the AVERAGE AC RESISTANCE rav = ΔV/ΔI, and where it meets the voltage axis is the CUT-IN VOLTAGE Vγ. The diode becomes an ideal switch in series with a battery Vγ and a resistor rav: the PIECEWISE-LINEAR MODEL used in later topics. For the example diode between 1 mA and 10 mA: Vγ ≈ 0.70 V and rav ≈ 6.6 Ω, or 8.6 Ω with rB = 2 Ω.

Key points

  • Bulk resistance adds: r = ηVT/I + rB; it dominates at high current.
  • Piecewise-linear model: ideal switch + Vγ + rav.
  • Example (1–10 mA): Vγ ≈ 0.70 V, rav ≈ 6.6 Ω (8.6 Ω with rB = 2 Ω).

Summary

A diode has no single resistance. The static resistance RD = VD/ID is the inverse slope of the chord from the origin to the Q point; it describes the DC drop. The dynamic resistance rd = ηVT/ID ≈ 26 mV/ID is the inverse slope of the tangent; it describes small changes and is the diode's small-signal model. Both fall roughly as 1/I, with RD about 20–30 times larger. A real diode adds its bulk resistance rB, and for large signals the curve is approximated by Vγ in series with rav.

Key points

  • Static: RD = VD/ID (chord). Dynamic: rd = ηVT/ID (tangent).
  • rd ≈ 26 Ω at 1 mA (η = 1, 300 K), independent of the diode's size.
  • Small-signal model: the diode is the resistor rd for signals of a few mV.
  • Real diode: add rB; piecewise-linear model Vγ + rav.
