TOPIC 2.2

Barrier Potential and the Energy Band Diagram of a PN Junction

The built-in potential, and how it bends the energy bands across the junction

The built-in potential barrier

In topic 2.1 we saw that the fixed ions in the depletion region create an electric field. Because of this field, there is a POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE between the neutral n region and the neutral p region. The n side is at a higher potential than the p side. This potential difference is called the BUILT-IN POTENTIAL Vbi, also called the barrier potential, contact potential or junction potential. Some textbooks write it as V0.

It acts as a BARRIER: an electron in the n region must climb this potential step to enter the p region, and a hole in the p region must climb it to enter the n region. Only the few carriers with enough thermal energy can cross by diffusion, and this small flow is exactly balanced by drift in equilibrium.

The built-in potential cannot be measured by connecting a voltmeter across the diode. The metal-semiconductor contacts at the two ends have their own contact potentials, and around the complete loop these exactly cancel Vbi. If they did not, an unbiased diode would be a battery delivering power for nothing.

Key points

  • The depletion-region field makes the n side more positive than the p side by Vbi.
  • Vbi is a barrier: only energetic electrons (n → p) and holes (p → n) can cross.
  • A voltmeter reads 0 V: the metal contact potentials cancel Vbi around the loop.

How big is the barrier?

The built-in potential depends on the doping on both sides:

Vbi = (kT/q) · ln( NA·ND / ni2 )

where kT/q ≈ 0.0259 V at 300 K (the thermal voltage VT).

For the example silicon junction (NA = 1017, ND = 1016 cm−3, ni = 1.5 × 1010 cm−3):

Vbi = 0.0259 × ln(1033 / 2.25 × 1020) ≈ 0.0259 × 29.1 ≈ 0.754 V

(With ni = 1010 cm−3, as used in some books and in Unit 1, the result is about 0.775 V.)

Important features:

  • Vbi depends on doping only through a LOGARITHM, so even large changes in doping change it only a little. For typical silicon junctions it is about 0.6 to 0.9 V.
  • A material with a smaller band gap has a much larger ni, and so a smaller Vbi. A germanium junction with the same doping (ni = 2.4 × 1013 cm−3, Eg = 0.66 eV) has Vbi ≈ 0.37 V. Some books use ni ≈ 2.5 × 1013 cm−3 for germanium, which changes Vbi by only about 2 mV.
  • As temperature rises, ni increases rapidly (ni ∝ T3/2·exp(−Eg/2kT)), so Vbi DECREASES. The thermal voltage kT/q also grows, but the rise of ni wins.

Key points

  • Vbi = (kT/q)·ln(NAND/ni2) ≈ 0.754 V for the example.
  • Logarithmic in doping: typically 0.6–0.9 V for silicon.
  • Smaller band gap → larger ni → smaller Vbi (Ge ≈ 0.37 V).
  • Higher temperature → larger ni → smaller Vbi.

Band diagrams before contact

Recall from Unit 1 that on an energy band diagram, the Fermi level EF shows how filled the bands are.

  • In p-type material EF lies BELOW the intrinsic level Ei, closer to the valence band: Ei − EF = kT·ln(NA/ni).
  • In n-type material EF lies ABOVE Ei, closer to the conduction band: EF − Ei = kT·ln(ND/ni).

Here kT ≈ 0.0259 eV at 300 K, so the results come out directly in electron-volts. For the example: on the p side, Ei − EF ≈ 0.0259 × ln(1017/1.5 × 1010) ≈ 0.407 eV; on the n side, EF − Ei ≈ 0.0259 × ln(1016/1.5 × 1010) ≈ 0.347 eV.

If we imagine the p and n pieces separately, with their band edges at the same level, their Fermi levels are at different heights: the n-side EF is higher by 0.407 + 0.347 ≈ 0.754 eV.

Key points

  • p-type: EF is kT·ln(NA/ni) below Ei (0.407 eV here).
  • n-type: EF is kT·ln(ND/ni) above Ei (0.347 eV here).
  • Separately, the two Fermi levels differ by 0.754 eV, which is qVbi.

Band diagram in equilibrium

In THERMAL EQUILIBRIUM, the Fermi level must be the SAME (flat) throughout the whole junction. If it were not, electrons would flow from the higher Fermi level to the lower one, and that is exactly what happens during the diffusion of topic 2.1. The flow stops when the Fermi levels have lined up.

With EF flat, the bands must BEND across the depletion region. Far into the p region the bands are high; far into the n region they are low. The total bending equals the original difference between the Fermi levels:

qVbi = (EF on n side − EF on p side, before contact) = kT · ln( NA·ND / ni2 )

This gives the same formula for Vbi as before, seen from the energy point of view.

The band bending happens only inside the depletion region, where the electric field exists. In the neutral regions, the bands are flat. The shape of the bend follows the potential: two parabolic pieces.

Key points

  • Equilibrium ⇔ flat Fermi level through the whole junction.
  • The bands bend down from p to n by qVbi.
  • Bending only in the depletion region (two parabolas); flat bands in the neutral regions.

Reading the barrier on the band diagram

On a band diagram, electron energy increases upward, and hole energy increases downward.

  • An ELECTRON in the n-side conduction band sees the conduction band edge rise toward the p side: to cross it must climb a hill of height qVbi.
  • A HOLE in the p-side valence band sees the valence band edge fall toward the n side, which for a hole is also an uphill climb of height qVbi.

Electrons in the conduction band are spread over energies above Ec according to the Boltzmann tail. Only the small fraction with energy above the top of the barrier can cross. That fraction is about exp(−qVbi/kT). This is why the minority electron concentration on the p side is so much smaller than the majority electron concentration on the n side:

np0 = nn0 · exp(−qVbi/kT)

For the example this gives np0 = 1016 × exp(−29.1) ≈ 2.25 × 103 cm−3, the same as ni2/NA.

The electric field in the depletion region slides electrons downhill (toward the n side) and lets holes float up the diagram (which is downhill for a hole) toward the p side. That is the drift that balances the diffusion.

Key points

  • Electrons climb up the Ec hill; holes climb "down" the Ev hill: both barriers are qVbi.
  • Only a fraction ≈ exp(−qVbi/kT) of carriers can cross.
  • np0 = nn0·exp(−qVbi/kT) ≈ 2.25 × 103 cm−3 here.
  • Drift down the slope balances diffusion over the top.

What happens when a voltage is applied (preview)

If an external voltage is applied, the junction is no longer in equilibrium, and the Fermi level is no longer flat.

  • FORWARD BIAS (p side made positive by V): the barrier is LOWERED to q(Vbi − V). Many more carriers can cross, so current flows easily (topic 2.3).
  • REVERSE BIAS (n side made positive by VR): the barrier is RAISED to q(Vbi + VR). Almost no carriers can cross, so only a tiny current flows (topic 2.4).

On the band diagram, the applied voltage separates the Fermi level on the n side from the one on the p side by qV. Near and inside the depletion region, two separate "quasi-Fermi levels" are used to describe electrons and holes. These details are covered in the next two topics.

Key points

  • Bias breaks equilibrium: the Fermi levels on the two sides split by qV.
  • Forward: barrier q(Vbi − V), narrower region, many more carriers cross.
  • Reverse: barrier q(Vbi + VR), wider region, almost none cross.

Summary

The fixed charges in the depletion region create a built-in potential Vbi = (kT/q)·ln(NA·ND/ni2), about 0.754 V for the example silicon junction and smaller for germanium. It cannot be measured with a voltmeter. In equilibrium the Fermi level is flat, so the energy bands bend by qVbi across the depletion region, forming a barrier that electrons and holes must climb. Forward bias lowers the barrier to q(Vbi − V); reverse bias raises it to q(Vbi + VR).

Key points

  • Vbi = (kT/q)·ln(NAND/ni2): 0.754 V (Si example), ≈ 0.37 V (Ge).
  • Not measurable with a voltmeter.
  • Flat EF in equilibrium; bands bend by qVbi.
  • Forward bias lowers the barrier; reverse bias raises it.
