TOPIC 2.6

Temperature Effects on the Diode

Why leakage doubles every few degrees and the forward drop falls by 2 mV per degree

Two quantities that change with temperature

Everything in the diode equation I = IS[exp(V/ηVT) − 1] depends on temperature through just two quantities:

  • The THERMAL VOLTAGE VT = kT/q is proportional to the absolute temperature T: 18.8 mV at −55 °C, 25.9 mV at 27 °C (300 K), 32.2 mV at 100 °C and 36.5 mV at 150 °C. This is a gentle, linear change.
  • The INTRINSIC CONCENTRATION ni follows ni2 = B·T3·exp(−Eg/kT). The exponential makes it change EXPLOSIVELY: about 8% per °C near room temperature. For silicon, ni is about 1.6 × 106 cm−3 at −55 °C, 1.5 × 1010 at 27 °C, 2.0 × 1012 at 100 °C and 2.3 × 1013 at 150 °C.

The physical picture: a hotter lattice vibrates more strongly, so more covalent bonds break and more electron-hole pairs are generated (topic 1.3). Germanium, with its smaller band gap (0.66 eV against 1.12 eV), starts with a much larger ni (2.4 × 1013 cm−3 at 27 °C) and reaches 6.5 × 1014 at 100 °C. The band gap itself also shrinks a little as temperature rises; the model below includes this.

Key points

  • VT = kT/q rises linearly: 25.9 mV at 27 °C, 32.2 mV at 100 °C.
  • ni2 ∝ T3·exp(−Eg/kT): ni rises about 8% per °C.
  • Silicon ni: 1.5 × 1010 cm−3 at 27 °C, 2.0 × 1012 at 100 °C.
  • Germanium (smaller Eg) has a far larger ni at every temperature.

Reverse current: doubling every few degrees

The ideal reverse saturation current is proportional to ni2 (topic 2.4), so it inherits the double dose of the exponential. For silicon near room temperature, IS rises about 16% per °C and DOUBLES ABOUT EVERY 4–5 °C. For the example diode it grows from 1.7 fA at 27 °C to about 31 pA at 100 °C and 3.9 nA at 150 °C. (D and L also change a little with temperature; they are held constant here.)

The generation current that actually dominates the reverse current of a silicon diode is proportional to ni only, so it rises about 8% per °C and doubles roughly every 8–9 °C. This is the origin of the familiar rule of thumb that the reverse current doubles for every 10 °C rise (Millman quotes about 7% per °C). For the example diode at VR = 5 V: about 11 pA at 27 °C, 1.5 nA at 100 °C and 20 nA at 150 °C.

Because IS grows twice as fast (in log terms) as Igen, it catches up at high temperatures. In germanium, IS dominates at all temperatures: 4.4 nA at 27 °C becomes about 3.2 µA at 100 °C for the same small junction.

Key points

  • IS ∝ ni2: about +16% per °C in silicon, doubling every 4–5 °C.
  • Igen ∝ ni: about +8% per °C, doubling every 8–9 °C.
  • Rule of thumb: reverse current doubles every 10 °C (about 7% per °C).
  • Example Si at 5 V: 11 pA (27 °C) → 1.5 nA (100 °C). Ge IS: 4.4 nA → 3.2 µA.

Forward voltage: about −2 mV per °C

Hold the forward current constant and heat the diode. The voltage needed is V = VT·ln(I/IS). VT rises, but IS rises much faster, so the log term shrinks and V FALLS. Differentiating with ni2 ∝ T3·exp(−Vg0/VT) gives

dV/dT = −(Vg0 + 3VT − V) / T

where Vg0 ≈ 1.21 V is the silicon band gap extrapolated to 0 K. At 1 mA (V = 0.70 V) this is −(1.21 + 0.078 − 0.70)/300 ≈ −1.9 mV/°C. At smaller currents V is lower, so the change is larger: about −2.5 mV/°C at 1 µA and −1.7 mV/°C at 10 mA. The usual rule of thumb is −2 mV/°C; books quote values between −2 and −2.5 mV/°C.

Over the military temperature range, the forward voltage of the example diode at 1 mA falls from about 0.85 V at −55 °C to 0.45 V at 150 °C. Germanium behaves the same way, about −1.8 mV/°C at 1 mA.

Key points

  • At constant current, V falls as T rises: dV/dT = −(Vg0 + 3VT − V)/T.
  • About −1.9 mV/°C at 1 mA; rule of thumb −2 mV/°C (−2 to −2.5 in books).
  • Lower current: larger change (−2.5 mV/°C at 1 µA).
  • Example at 1 mA: 0.85 V (−55 °C), 0.70 V (27 °C), 0.45 V (150 °C).

At a fixed voltage, the current climbs

The same fact seen the other way: if the forward VOLTAGE is held fixed, the current I ≈ IS·exp(V/VT) rises with temperature, because the growth of IS beats the growth of VT. On a linear plot the whole characteristic slides to the LEFT by about 2 mV for each degree.

At a fixed 0.70 V, the example diode carries 1.0 mA at 27 °C, about 4.9 mA at 50 °C and 23 mA at 75 °C: roughly +7.5% per °C, doubling about every 9 °C.

This is dangerous. More current means more power, more heating and so even more current: THERMAL RUNAWAY. That is why diodes and transistors are driven from a current source or through a resistor rather than from a fixed voltage, and why bias circuits are designed for stability (Unit 6).

Key points

  • At a fixed voltage, forward current rises about 7.5% per °C (doubles every 9 °C at 0.70 V).
  • The curve slides left by about 2 mV per °C.
  • Fixed-voltage drive risks thermal runaway; drive with a current or through a resistor.

Consequences

  • A DIODE IS A THERMOMETER. Fed from a constant current, its forward voltage falls almost linearly with temperature. For the example diode at 100 µA, V goes from about 0.81 V at −55 °C to 0.37 V at 150 °C, about −2.1 mV/°C. Integrated-circuit temperature sensors and band-gap voltage references are built on this.
  • GERMANIUM RUNS OUT OF ROOM FIRST. Its leakage starts higher and grows fast: about 3.2 µA at 100 °C for the small example junction, against 1.5 nA for silicon. Typical maximum junction temperatures are about 75–100 °C for germanium and 150–200 °C for silicon. This is one of the main reasons silicon replaced germanium.

Key points

  • Constant-current diode: V falls about 2 mV/°C, nearly linearly: a temperature sensor.
  • Germanium leakage is far higher; maximum junction temperature about 75–100 °C (Si: 150–200 °C).

Summary

Temperature enters the diode through VT = kT/q, which rises gently, and ni, which rises about 8% per °C. The reverse current follows ni2 (IS, doubling every 4–5 °C) or ni (generation current, doubling every 8–9 °C), giving the rule that reverse current doubles every 10 °C. At constant current, the forward voltage falls by about 2 mV/°C; at constant voltage, the forward current climbs about 7.5% per °C, which can cause thermal runaway. The same effect makes a diode a good thermometer, and germanium's high leakage limits it to lower temperatures than silicon.

Key points

  • VT = kT/q rises linearly; ni rises about 8% per °C.
  • Reverse current: doubles every 4–5 °C (IS) to 8–9 °C (Igen); rule of thumb every 10 °C.
  • Forward voltage at constant current: about −2 mV/°C.
  • Fixed voltage: current rises about 7.5% per °C, risk of thermal runaway.
