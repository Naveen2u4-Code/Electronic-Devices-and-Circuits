TOPIC 2.3

Forward Bias

Lowering the barrier: minority carrier injection and the diode equation

Connecting a forward bias

A pn junction is FORWARD BIASED when the p side is connected to the POSITIVE terminal of a battery and the n side to the NEGATIVE terminal. The applied voltage V is then positive.

The neutral p and n regions conduct well, so almost all of the applied voltage appears across the DEPLETION REGION, where there are no free carriers and the resistance is high. The applied voltage opposes the built-in potential, so the barrier height is reduced from Vbi to:

barrier = Vbi − V

A lower barrier needs less uncovered charge, so the depletion region becomes NARROWER:

W = √[ (2εs·(Vbi − V) / q) · (NA + ND)/(NA·ND) ]

For the example junction, W shrinks from about 0.33 µm at V = 0 to about 0.16 µm at V = 0.58 V.

Key points

  • Forward bias: p to +, n to −, V > 0.
  • The applied voltage appears across the depletion region and opposes Vbi.
  • Barrier falls to Vbi − V; W narrows (0.33 µm → 0.16 µm at 0.58 V).

Injection of minority carriers

Lowering the barrier upsets the balance between diffusion and drift. The diffusion of majority carriers across the junction now greatly exceeds the drift:

  • many HOLES from the p side cross into the n side;
  • many ELECTRONS from the n side cross into the p side.

Once across, they become MINORITY carriers in a region full of majority carriers of the opposite type. This process is called MINORITY CARRIER INJECTION.

The concentration of injected minority carriers at the edges of the depletion region rises exponentially with the applied voltage. This is often called the LAW OF THE JUNCTION:

pn(xn) = pn0 · exp(V/VT)   and   np(−xp) = np0 · exp(V/VT)

where pn0 = ni2/ND and np0 = ni2/NA are the equilibrium minority concentrations.

Example: on the n side, pn0 = (1.5 × 1010)2 / 1016 ≈ 2.25 × 104 cm−3. At V = 0.6 V, exp(0.6/0.0259) ≈ 1.15 × 1010, so pn(xn) ≈ 2.6 × 1014 cm−3. That is enormously larger than pn0, but still much less than ND = 1016 cm−3. This condition, where minority carriers stay far fewer than majority carriers, is called LOW-LEVEL INJECTION, and it is assumed in the basic diode theory.

Key points

  • A lower barrier lets diffusion win: holes go p → n, electrons go n → p.
  • Across the junction they are injected minority carriers.
  • Law of the junction: edge concentration = equilibrium value × exp(V/VT), ×10 per 60 mV.
  • Low-level injection: injected carriers stay far below the majority level.

Diffusion and recombination in the neutral regions

The injected minority carriers are most concentrated at the edge of the depletion region. They diffuse away from the junction into the neutral region and, as they go, they recombine with the majority carriers there.

The EXCESS minority concentration (above the equilibrium value) therefore falls off with distance. For a long diode (neutral regions much longer than the diffusion length), it decays exponentially:

δpn(x) = δpn(xn) · exp( −(x − xn) / Lp )

Lp = √(Dp·τp) is the hole DIFFUSION LENGTH: the average distance a hole travels in the n region before recombining. Dp is the hole diffusion coefficient and τp the hole lifetime. With Dp = 10 cm²/s and τp = 1 µs, Lp ≈ 32 µm. Similarly, injected electrons in the p region decay over the electron diffusion length Ln (≈ 16 µm here, with Dn = 25 cm²/s and τn = 0.1 µs).

At a distance of a few diffusion lengths, the minority concentration has returned to its equilibrium value. (If a neutral region is much shorter than the diffusion length, a "short diode", few carriers recombine on the way and the profile becomes nearly a straight line instead.)

Key points

  • Injected carriers diffuse away from the junction and recombine.
  • Long diode: the excess decays as exp(−distance/L).
  • Lp = √(Dpτp) ≈ 32 µm, Ln ≈ 16 µm for the example.
  • A few diffusion lengths away, the concentration is back to equilibrium.

Where the current flows

The total current must be the same at every cross-section of the diode. Yet the CARRIERS that carry it change along the way:

  • Near the junction on the n side, the current is mainly carried by DIFFUSION of injected holes.
  • As these holes recombine, they are replaced by ELECTRONS flowing in from the n contact, which arrive by drift as majority carriers to recombine with them.
  • Far from the junction, the current is carried entirely by majority carriers drifting in a small electric field.

The same happens in mirror image on the p side.

So the forward current is set by how fast minority carriers diffuse away from the edges of the depletion region. The total current at the junction is the sum of the hole diffusion current at xn and the electron diffusion current at −xp. Conventional current flows from the p side to the n side (from anode to cathode).

In our example, the n side is ten times more lightly doped, so ten times more holes are injected into it than electrons into the p side: pn(xn) is ten times np(−xp). The hole current dominates, but only by about two to one, because the injected electrons have a larger diffusion coefficient and a shorter diffusion length:

Ip / In = (Dp·Ln·NA) / (Dn·Lp·ND) ≈ 2

So holes carry about 67% of the current at the junction and electrons about 33%.

Key points

  • Total current is constant along the diode; its carriers change.
  • Near the junction: minority diffusion. Far away: majority drift.
  • I at the junction = hole diffusion at xn + electron diffusion at −xp; conventional current flows p → n.
  • The lighter-doped side receives more injection: here holes carry ≈ 67%.

How the current depends on voltage

The diffusion current is proportional to the slope of the minority-carrier profile, which is proportional to the excess concentration at the depletion edge, and that grows as exp(V/VT) − 1. So the total current is:

I = IS · [ exp(V/VT) − 1 ]

where IS is the SATURATION CURRENT:

IS = A · q · ni2 · [ Dp/(Lp·ND) + Dn/(Ln·NA) ]

IS is tiny for silicon because ni2 is small, typically in the range of femtoamperes (10−15 A) for small junctions. For the example (A = 10−4 cm²), IS ≈ 1.7 × 10−15 A, so the current is about 1 mA at 0.70 V and about 20 mA at 0.78 V.

Consequences:

  • The current rises EXPONENTIALLY: for every increase of about 60 mV (VT·ln10), the current increases ten times.
  • Below about 0.5 V, the current in a silicon diode is too small to notice. Between roughly 0.6 and 0.8 V it becomes large. This is why the diode is said to have a CUT-IN (threshold) voltage of about 0.5 to 0.6 V and a typical forward drop of about 0.7 V for silicon. For germanium the cut-in voltage is only about 0.2 V.
  • Real diodes follow this equation closely over many decades of current. At low currents, recombination in the depletion region adds current that grows as exp(V/2VT), and at high currents high-level injection and the resistance of the neutral regions and contacts limit the current. Both are often described by an ideality factor n between 1 and 2 in I = IS·[exp(V/nVT) − 1] (topic 2.5). Some books write it as η and quote η ≈ 1 for germanium and η ≈ 2 for silicon at small currents.

Key points

  • I = IS[exp(V/VT) − 1], with IS = Aqni2[Dp/(LpND) + Dn/(LnNA)] ≈ 1.7 fA here.
  • ×10 current per ≈ 60 mV: a straight line on a log axis.
  • Silicon: cut-in ≈ 0.5–0.6 V, forward drop ≈ 0.7 V; germanium cut-in ≈ 0.2 V.
  • Real diodes: ideality factor n between 1 and 2 (topic 2.5).

The band diagram under forward bias

Under forward bias, the Fermi level is no longer flat. Far in the n region it is raised by qV relative to the p region. The band bending across the depletion region is reduced from qVbi to q(Vbi − V), so the barrier for electrons and holes is lower.

Inside and near the depletion region, electrons and holes are no longer in equilibrium with each other. Their concentrations are described by two separate QUASI-FERMI LEVELS: EFn for electrons and EFp for holes. Across the depletion region they are separated by qV. In the neutral regions, the minority quasi-Fermi level slopes back toward the majority one as the injected carriers recombine. It falls by only about kT for every diffusion length, so although the excess carriers are mostly gone within a few diffusion lengths, the two levels meet only where the excess has died out completely (at the latest, at the ohmic contact).

Key points

  • Band bending drops to q(Vbi − V): a lower barrier.
  • Two quasi-Fermi levels, EFn and EFp, split by qV across the depletion region.
  • In the neutral regions the minority level slopes back (≈ kT per diffusion length) until the two meet again.

Summary

In forward bias, the p side is positive. The applied voltage reduces the barrier to Vbi − V and narrows the depletion region. Majority carriers cross the junction and become injected minority carriers, with concentrations at the depletion edges of pn0·exp(V/VT) and np0·exp(V/VT). They diffuse into the neutral regions and recombine over a diffusion length, and majority carriers from the contacts take over the current. The result is the diode equation I = IS[exp(V/VT) − 1], a current that grows ten times for every 60 mV, with a practical forward drop of about 0.7 V for silicon.

Key points

  • p side positive: barrier Vbi − V, narrower depletion region.
  • Edge minority concentrations rise as exp(V/VT).
  • Diffusion + recombination over Lp, Ln; majority carriers carry the current far away.
  • I = IS[exp(V/VT) − 1]: ×10 per 60 mV, ≈ 0.7 V drop for silicon.
