TOPIC 2.8

Transition and Diffusion Capacitance

Two kinds of stored charge, two capacitances: one rules reverse bias, the other forward bias

Two kinds of stored charge

A capacitor is anything whose stored charge changes when its voltage changes: C = dQ/dV. A pn junction stores charge in two different places, so it has two capacitances.

  • DEPLETION CHARGE. The fixed ions in the depletion region: negative acceptors on the p side, positive donors on the n side (topic 2.1). When the voltage changes, the depletion region widens or narrows and ions are uncovered or covered at its edges. This gives the TRANSITION (depletion, junction or space-charge) CAPACITANCE CT.
  • INJECTED MINORITY CARRIERS. In forward bias, holes injected into the n side and electrons injected into the p side form decaying "tails" beyond the depletion region (topic 2.3). More voltage, more stored carriers. This gives the DIFFUSION (storage) CAPACITANCE CD.

Move the bias below. In reverse bias the depletion region is wide and there are no tails: only CT matters. In forward bias the depletion region narrows and the tails grow explosively: CD takes over.

Key points

  • C = dQ/dV: any charge that changes with voltage acts as a capacitance.
  • Depletion-region ions → transition capacitance CT.
  • Injected minority carriers → diffusion capacitance CD.

Transition capacitance CT

The depletion region is an insulating layer between two conducting neutral regions: a PARALLEL-PLATE CAPACITOR whose plate spacing is the depletion width W. A small increase in reverse voltage uncovers a thin slab of ions at each edge, +ΔQ on the n side and −ΔQ on the p side, just like charging a capacitor. The result is exactly the parallel-plate formula:

CT = εsA / W

The difference from an ordinary capacitor is that W itself depends on the voltage. For an abrupt junction W ∝ (Vbi + VR)1/2 (topic 2.4), so

CT = CT0 / (1 + VR/Vbi)m, m = 1/2 (abrupt), 1/3 (linearly graded)

For the example junction (A = 10−4 cm2, Vbi = 0.754 V): CT0 = 3.16 pF at zero bias (W = 0.33 µm), 2.07 pF at 1 V, 1.15 pF at 5 V (W = 0.90 µm) and 0.84 pF at 10 V. Typical values are a few picofarads, so CT matters at high frequencies and in fast switching. In forward bias the formula would blow up near Vbi; a common approximation is CT ≈ 2CT0.

Key points

  • CT = εsA/W: a parallel-plate capacitor with voltage-dependent spacing.
  • CT = CT0/(1 + VR/Vbi)m; m = 1/2 abrupt, 1/3 graded.
  • Example: 3.16 pF at 0 V, 1.15 pF at 5 V. Dominant in reverse bias.

Using CT: the varactor diode

A capacitance that can be set by a DC voltage is very useful. A VARACTOR (varicap) diode is a diode designed to be used this way: always reverse biased, so almost no current flows, with its CT forming part of a tuned LC circuit. The resonant frequency is

f = 1 / (2π√(L·CT))

so turning a voltage knob tunes the circuit, with no moving parts. This is how radio and TV tuners, voltage-controlled oscillators and phase-locked loops are tuned. With the example junction and L = 10 µH, raising VR from 1 V to 20 V lowers CT from 2.07 pF to 0.60 pF and raises f from about 35 MHz to 65 MHz. Real varactors use a larger area and a HYPERABRUPT doping profile (m larger than 1/2) to get a wider tuning range.

Key points

  • Varactor: a reverse-biased diode used as a voltage-controlled capacitor.
  • f = 1/(2π√(LCT)): higher VR → smaller CT → higher f.
  • Used in tuners, VCOs and PLLs; hyperabrupt profiles widen the range.

Diffusion capacitance CD

In forward bias, the injected minority carriers spread out from the junction with exponentially decaying profiles (topic 2.3). Each injected carrier lives on average one lifetime τ before it recombines, so in steady state the stored minority charge is simply the current times the lifetime:

Q = τT · I

Here τT is the mean transit time, the lifetimes weighted by how the current is shared. In the example, two thirds of the current is holes (τp = 1 µs) and one third electrons (τn = 0.1 µs), so τT = 0.70 µs. Since I ∝ exp(V/ηVT), a small change in V changes Q in proportion:

CD = dQ/dV = τT·I / ηVT = τT / rd

CD is proportional to the current, and it is LARGE: for the example diode, 27 pF at 1 µA, 2.7 nF at 100 µA, 27 nF at 1 mA (Q = 0.70 nC) and 270 nF at 10 mA. Some books include a factor of ½ for a long diode; the simple form above is the one used by Millman and by Sedra and Smith.

This stored charge also explains why a diode does not switch off instantly. When the bias is reversed, the stored carriers must first be swept out or recombine, so a large reverse current flows for a time of the order of τ: the REVERSE RECOVERY TIME. Fast-switching diodes use short lifetimes; Schottky diodes avoid minority storage altogether.

Key points

  • Stored minority charge Q = τTI.
  • CD = τTI/ηVT = τT/rd: proportional to current.
  • Example (τT = 0.70 µs): 27 nF at 1 mA, far larger than CT.
  • Stored charge causes reverse recovery time when switching off.

Which one dominates?

Both capacitances are always present, in parallel, but they change with voltage in completely different ways. CT changes slowly (as a square root), while CD follows the current and rises ten times every 60 mV.

  • REVERSE BIAS: CD is essentially zero; the diode is a small CT of a few picofarads.
  • FORWARD BIAS: CD overtakes CT at about 0.48 V for the example diode (both about 5 pF there; the current is only about 0.2 µA). At normal operating currents CD is thousands of times larger.

In short: CT for reverse bias, CD for forward bias.

Key points

  • CT and CD are in parallel; the total is their sum.
  • Reverse bias: CT only (picofarads).
  • Forward bias: CD dominates above about 0.48 V (example), reaching nanofarads at milliamperes.

Summary

A pn junction stores charge in two places. The ions of the depletion region give the transition capacitance CT = εsA/W = CT0/(1 + VR/Vbi)m, a few picofarads that fall as the reverse voltage rises; the varactor diode uses it as a voltage-tuned capacitor. The injected minority carriers give the diffusion capacitance CD = τTI/ηVT, proportional to the forward current and usually far larger. CT dominates in reverse bias and CD in forward bias; the stored charge behind CD also sets the reverse recovery time.

Key points

  • CT (depletion ions): εsA/W, a few pF, falls with VR. Dominant in reverse bias.
  • Varactor: reverse-biased diode as a voltage-controlled capacitor.
  • CD (stored minority carriers): τTI/ηVT, ∝ I. Dominant in forward bias.
  • Stored charge → reverse recovery time when switching.
