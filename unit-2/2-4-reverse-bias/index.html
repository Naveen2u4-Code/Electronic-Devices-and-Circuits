TOPIC 2.4

Reverse Bias and Reverse Saturation Current

Raising the barrier: why only a tiny, nearly constant current flows

Connecting a reverse bias

A pn junction is REVERSE BIASED when the p side is connected to the NEGATIVE terminal of a battery and the n side to the POSITIVE terminal. The reverse voltage is written VR (a positive number), so the applied voltage is V = −VR.

The applied voltage adds to the built-in potential, so the barrier becomes HIGHER:

barrier = Vbi + VR

(Some books write the built-in potential as V0 or φi instead of Vbi.)

The battery pulls holes in the p region toward the negative terminal and electrons in the n region toward the positive terminal, away from the junction. More fixed ions are uncovered, and the depletion region becomes WIDER:

W = √[ (2εs·(Vbi + VR) / q) · (NA + ND)/(NA·ND) ]

For the example junction, W grows from about 0.33 µm at VR = 0 to about 0.90 µm at VR = 5 V. The maximum electric field at the junction also increases, from about 4.6 × 104 V/cm to about 1.3 × 105 V/cm. Because the field falls linearly to zero at both edges, its peak is simply |Emax| = 2(Vbi + VR)/W.

This movement of majority carriers happens only for a brief moment when the voltage is applied. It is not a steady current.

Key points

  • Reverse bias: p to −, n to +; V = −VR.
  • The barrier rises to Vbi + VR.
  • W widens as √(Vbi + VR): 0.33 µm → 0.90 µm at 5 V; |Emax| grows too.
  • Majority carriers move away only briefly; there is no steady current from this.

Why almost no current flows

With the higher barrier, majority carriers can no longer cross the junction by diffusion. Almost no holes from the p side reach the n side, and almost no electrons from the n side reach the p side.

But the strong field in the depletion region HELPS minority carriers. Any electron (a minority carrier in the p region) that wanders to the edge of the depletion region is swept across into the n region, and any hole in the n region that reaches the edge is swept into the p region. Near the depletion edges, the minority concentration drops almost to zero:

pn(xn) = pn0 · exp(−VR/VT) ≈ 0   for VR above a few tenths of a volt

Minority carriers within about one diffusion length of the junction diffuse toward it and are collected. This flow forms a small, steady current called the REVERSE SATURATION CURRENT, IS (some books call it I0). It is the same IS that appears in the diode equation: with V = −VR,

I = IS·[exp(−VR/VT) − 1] ≈ −IS

It is called “saturation” current because, once VR is more than about 0.1 V (a few VT), the current stops depending on VR: the supply of minority carriers, not the voltage, sets it. At VR = 0.1 V the current is already within 2% of IS.

Key points

  • Majority carriers are blocked by the higher barrier.
  • The field sweeps minority carriers across; edge concentrations fall to almost zero.
  • Minority carriers within about a diffusion length are collected: this is IS.
  • Beyond about 0.1 V the current is flat at −IS: “saturation”.

How large is IS?

From the diode theory:

IS = A · q · ni2 · [ Dp/(Lp·ND) + Dn/(Ln·NA) ]

The key factor is ni2. For silicon at room temperature, ni2 is very small, so IS is tiny: for the example diode (Lp = √(Dpτp) ≈ 32 µm, Ln ≈ 16 µm), it is about 1.7 × 10−15 A (1.7 fA). For germanium, ni is about 1,600 times larger, so ni2 is about 2.6 million times larger. With the same other values, IS rises to about 4.4 nA for our small diode; practical germanium diodes, with larger junctions, have IS in the microampere range.

IS is also proportional to the junction AREA A: a bigger diode has a bigger IS.

Because ni rises very rapidly with temperature, IS is extremely temperature-sensitive (topic 2.6).

Key points

  • IS ∝ ni2: about 1.7 fA for the silicon example.
  • Germanium: ni ×1,600, so IS × 2.6 million (≈ 4.4 nA here; µA in practical diodes).
  • IS ∝ junction area A.
  • ni rises fast with temperature, so IS does too.

Generation current: the real reverse current in silicon

In real silicon diodes, the measured reverse current is usually much larger than the ideal IS, typically in the picoampere to nanoampere range. For our small example diode it is about 4 pA at VR = 0 and about 11 pA at VR = 5 V, some 2,000 to 6,000 times its ideal IS. The main reason is GENERATION inside the depletion region.

The depletion region has no free carriers, so the normal balance between generation and recombination is broken. Thermal generation of electron-hole pairs continues there, and each pair is immediately separated by the field: the electron is swept to the n side and the hole to the p side. This produces a GENERATION CURRENT:

Igen = q · ni · W · A / (2τ0)

where τ0 is a generation lifetime. Two important differences from IS:

  • Igen is proportional to ni, not ni2. In silicon at room temperature, ni (1.5 × 1010 cm−3) is about 700,000 times larger than ni2/ND (2.25 × 104 cm−3). That far outweighs the other factors, so Igen dominates.
  • Igen is proportional to the depletion width W, which grows with VR. So the real reverse current is not perfectly flat: it rises slowly, roughly as √(Vbi + VR).

Some treatments use a generation width slightly smaller than W, but the difference is small at large VR. Surface leakage at the edges of the junction can add further current. In germanium, ni is 1,600 times larger, so IS (∝ ni2) catches up with Igen (∝ ni); in practical germanium diodes the ideal IS is usually the larger part.

Key points

  • Pairs generated inside the depletion region are split by the field: Igen = q·ni·W·A/(2τ0).
  • Igen ∝ ni, IS ∝ ni2: in silicon Igen wins (≈ 4–11 pA vs 1.7 fA here).
  • Igen ∝ W, so the real reverse current rises slowly, about as √(Vbi + VR).
  • Surface leakage adds more; in germanium the ideal IS usually dominates.

The band diagram under reverse bias

Under reverse bias, the band bending increases from qVbi to q(Vbi + VR). The n-side Fermi level is now LOWER than the p-side Fermi level by qVR. The barrier for majority carriers is much higher, so almost none can cross.

Minority carriers that reach the depletion region simply “fall” down the steep slope: electrons from the p side roll down into the n side's conduction band, and holes from the n side “float up” into the p side's valence band. This is the reverse current.

Key points

  • Band bending grows to q(Vbi + VR).
  • The n-side Fermi level sits qVR below the p side.
  • Majority carriers face a much higher hill; minority carriers roll down it: that is the reverse current.

What reverse bias is used for

The reverse-biased junction is not just an “off” state; it is useful in its own right:

  • As an almost perfect ONE-WAY VALVE for rectifiers (Unit 4): a reverse-biased diode blocks current.
  • The depletion region acts like the dielectric of a capacitor whose width depends on VR. This gives a VOLTAGE-CONTROLLED CAPACITOR (the varactor diode, topic 3.4; capacitance in topic 2.8).
  • Light that creates electron-hole pairs in or near the depletion region adds to the reverse current. This is the PHOTODIODE (topic 3.3).
  • If VR becomes large enough, the junction BREAKS DOWN and a large reverse current flows (topic 2.9). The Zener diode uses this deliberately.

Key points

  • Rectifiers: a reverse-biased diode blocks current.
  • Varactor: W, and so the capacitance, is set by VR.
  • Photodiode: light-generated pairs in the depletion region add reverse current.
  • Breakdown: at a large enough VR the current shoots up; Zener diodes use this.

Summary

In reverse bias, the n side is positive. The barrier rises to Vbi + VR, the depletion region widens and the field grows. Majority carriers cannot cross, but minority carriers near the junction are swept across, giving a tiny reverse saturation current IS (about 1.7 fA for the silicon example), independent of VR beyond about 0.1 V and proportional to ni2. In real silicon diodes, thermal generation in the depletion region gives a larger current (about 4–11 pA here), proportional to ni and to W, that rises slowly with VR. Reverse bias is used for blocking, variable capacitance and photodetection, until breakdown.

Key points

  • n side positive: barrier Vbi + VR, wider W, stronger field.
  • Ideal reverse current: −IS, flat beyond ≈ 0.1 V, ∝ ni2.
  • Real silicon: generation current ∝ ni·W dominates and rises slowly with VR.
  • Uses: blocking, varactors, photodiodes; limit: breakdown.
