TOPIC 2.5

V-I Characteristics and the Diode Equation

One exponential law for both directions, and what it really says

The V-I characteristic

The V-I CHARACTERISTIC of a diode is the graph of the current I through it against the voltage V across it. The usual sign convention: V is positive when the p side (the ANODE) is positive, and I is positive when it flows from p to n, anode to cathode.

Topics 2.3 and 2.4 gave the two halves. Put together, the curve has three regions:

  • FORWARD (V > 0): almost nothing flows until a few tenths of a volt, then the current rises very steeply. For the example silicon diode used throughout this unit, I is about 1 µA at 0.52 V and 1 mA at 0.70 V.
  • REVERSE (V < 0): only a tiny current flows, a few picoamperes here, rising slowly as the depletion region widens (topic 2.4).
  • BREAKDOWN: at a large enough reverse voltage, about 60 V for this doping, the reverse current shoots up (topic 2.9).

The forward current is measured in milliamperes and the reverse current in picoamperes, so textbook plots use DIFFERENT SCALES in the four directions. The plot below does the same. Read the axis labels: a picoampere is a thousand million times smaller than a milliampere.

Key points

  • V positive when the anode (p) is positive; I positive from anode to cathode.
  • Forward: tiny current, then a steep rise (1 mA at about 0.70 V for the silicon example).
  • Reverse: picoamperes, nearly flat; breakdown near 60 V here.
  • Forward and reverse are drawn on very different scales.

The diode equation

The ideal pn junction theory gives one formula for both directions, the DIODE EQUATION (also called the Shockley equation):

I = IS · [ exp(V / ηVT) − 1 ]

  • IS is the reverse saturation current from topic 2.4, about 1.7 fA for the example silicon diode.
  • VT = kT/q is the THERMAL VOLTAGE: 25.9 mV at 300 K, often rounded to 26 mV or 25 mV.
  • η (some books write n) is the IDEALITY FACTOR, between 1 and 2. For the ideal diode η = 1; section 4 explains where 2 comes from.

Each term has a physical meaning. Lowering the barrier by V multiplies the number of majority carriers that can climb over it by the Boltzmann factor exp(V/VT): that is the IS·exp(V/VT) term, the injected carriers of topic 2.3. Minority carriers that reach the junction are swept back across whatever the barrier height: that is the −IS term, independent of V. At V = 0 the two flows are equal and I = 0.

Two useful limits follow (with η = 1):

V > 4VT (≈ 0.1 V):   I ≈ IS · exp(V/VT)   |   V < −4VT:   I ≈ −IS

At ±0.1 V each approximation is already within about 2%. Turning the equation round gives the voltage needed for a given current:

V = ηVT · ln(1 + I/IS)

Key points

  • I = IS[exp(V/ηVT) − 1], with VT = kT/q = 25.9 mV at 300 K.
  • exp(V/VT): carriers injected over the lowered barrier. −1: minority carriers swept back.
  • V > 0.1 V: I ≈ IS·exp(V/VT). V < −0.1 V: I ≈ −IS.
  • Inverse form: V = ηVT·ln(1 + I/IS).

A straight line on a log scale

In forward bias, taking the logarithm of I ≈ IS·exp(V/ηVT) gives

ln I = ln IS + V / ηVT

So if the current is plotted on a LOG SCALE, the forward characteristic is a STRAIGHT LINE. Extended back to V = 0, it meets the current axis at IS. The slope gives the rule every circuit designer uses:

ΔV = ηVT · ln(I2/I1) = 2.3·ηVT · log10(I2/I1)

For η = 1, each ×10 in current needs only 59.6 mV more (often rounded to 60 mV), and doubling the current needs 18 mV. For the example silicon diode: 1 µA at 0.523 V, 1 mA at 0.702 V, 10 mA at 0.761 V. Six decades of current over only 0.18 V.

Germanium has the same slope, but its IS is about 2.6 million times larger (topic 2.4). Its line is shifted left by VT·ln(2.6 × 106) ≈ 0.38 V, so the same 1 mA flows at about 0.32 V.

Key points

  • On a log current scale the forward characteristic is a straight line that meets V = 0 at IS.
  • ΔV = ηVT·ln(I2/I1): 59.6 mV per decade and 18 mV per doubling for η = 1.
  • Silicon example: 1 µA at 0.52 V, 1 mA at 0.70 V, 10 mA at 0.76 V.
  • Germanium: same slope, about 0.38 V to the left (1 mA at about 0.32 V).

The ideality factor and series resistance

A real silicon diode follows the ideal line only over its middle range. Two effects bend it:

  • RECOMBINATION IN THE DEPLETION REGION. Some injected electrons and holes recombine while crossing the depletion region. This adds a current Irec = IR0·[exp(V/2VT) − 1] with η = 2. It is the forward-bias partner of the generation current of topic 2.4, and IR0 is the same few picoamperes (about 4 pA here). IR0 is much larger than IS but grows only half as fast, so it dominates at low current: below about 0.4 V (20 nA) for the example diode, where the line has a slope of 119 mV per decade.
  • SERIES RESISTANCE. The neutral regions and contacts have a resistance RS, typically a few ohms. The junction sees only Vj = V − I·RS. At high current, the I·RS drop takes over: with RS = 5 Ω, 20 mA needs an extra 0.1 V and the log plot bends over.

At very high currents, high-level injection (minority carriers as many as the majority doping) also pushes the slope toward η = 2.

In practice, η is a number fitted to the measured curve. The local value is η = (1/VT)·dV/d(ln I). Books differ: Millman takes η = 1 for germanium and η ≈ 2 for silicon at small currents; Sedra and Smith write n and use values between 1 and 2.

Key points

  • Recombination in the depletion region: η = 2, dominates at low current (below about 0.4 V here).
  • Diffusion (ideal): η = 1, dominates in the middle range.
  • Series resistance RS: V = Vj + I·RS, bends the curve at high current.
  • η is fitted to measurements; books use 1 to 2 (Millman: 1 for Ge, ≈ 2 for Si at small currents).

The cut-in voltage

On a linear scale, the exponential looks like zero followed by a sharp “knee”. The voltage at which the current starts to become noticeable is called the CUT-IN VOLTAGE Vγ (also threshold, offset or turn-on voltage). A common definition is the voltage at which the current reaches about 1% of the rated (full-scale) current. Textbooks quote about 0.5 to 0.7 V for silicon and 0.1 to 0.3 V for germanium.

But the knee is NOT a property of the diode alone. The exponential has no corner: it only seems to have one because of the scale used. Zoom out by ×100 and the knee moves right by 2 × 59.6 ≈ 119 mV. For the example diodes, with the 1% definition:

  • full scale 10 µA: Si about 0.46 V, Ge about 0.08 V
  • full scale 1 mA: Si about 0.58 V, Ge about 0.20 V
  • full scale 100 mA: Si about 0.70 V, Ge about 0.32 V

Germanium turns on about 0.38 V earlier at every scale because of its larger IS. In circuit analysis (Unit 4), a conducting diode is often replaced by a constant drop of 0.7 V for silicon and 0.3 V for germanium, which matches the milliampere-to-100 mA range where most diodes work.

Key points

  • Cut-in voltage Vγ: where the current reaches about 1% of rated current.
  • Typical: 0.5–0.7 V for Si, 0.1–0.3 V for Ge.
  • The knee depends on the current scale: ×100 in scale moves it by about 0.12 V.
  • Constant-drop model for circuits: 0.7 V (Si), 0.3 V (Ge).

Summary

The diode's V-I characteristic follows I = IS[exp(V/ηVT) − 1]. In forward bias the current grows exponentially, so it looks like a knee on a linear plot and a straight line on a log plot, with 59.6 mV per decade for η = 1 (1 mA at about 0.70 V for the silicon example). In reverse bias it saturates at −IS (plus generation current) until breakdown. Real silicon diodes show η ≈ 2 at low currents from recombination and bend over at high currents from series resistance. The cut-in voltage, about 0.7 V for Si and 0.3 V for Ge at normal currents, depends on the current scale chosen.

Key points

  • I = IS[exp(V/ηVT) − 1]; VT = 25.9 mV at 300 K.
  • Forward: exponential, 59.6 mV per decade (η = 1). Reverse: −IS until breakdown.
  • Real Si: η ≈ 2 at low current, series resistance at high current.
  • Cut-in: about 0.7 V (Si), 0.3 V (Ge), and it depends on the scale.
