TOPIC 2.1

Formation of a PN Junction and the Depletion Region

How joining p-type and n-type silicon creates a region of fixed charge and a built-in field

What is a PN junction?

A PN JUNCTION is formed when one part of a single semiconductor crystal is doped p-type and the neighbouring part is doped n-type. The plane where the doping changes from p-type to n-type is called the METALLURGICAL JUNCTION.

It is not made by pressing a p-type piece against an n-type piece. A junction needs the crystal to be continuous on an atomic scale, and two separately made surfaces pressed together can never match that closely. Instead, the crystal stays continuous, and the doping is changed inside it, for example by diffusing or implanting acceptor atoms into part of an n-type wafer, or by growing an n-type layer on a p-type wafer (epitaxy).

The pn junction is the most basic building block of semiconductor devices. It is the diode itself, and it appears in BJTs, JFETs, MOSFETs, solar cells and LEDs.

In this topic we assume a STEP (abrupt) JUNCTION: the doping changes suddenly from a uniform NA on the p side to a uniform ND on the n side. Real diffused junctions change more gradually, but the step model shows all the essential physics.

Key points

  • One single crystal: p-type on one side, n-type on the other.
  • The plane where the doping changes is the metallurgical junction.
  • Made by changing the doping inside the crystal, never by pressing two pieces together.
  • Step junction: uniform NA switches abruptly to uniform ND.

Diffusion across the junction

In the first instant after the junction forms, the p side contains many holes and very few electrons, while the n side contains many electrons and very few holes. These large concentration differences cause DIFFUSION:

  • holes diffuse from the p side into the n side;
  • electrons diffuse from the n side into the p side.

When holes enter the n side, they meet many electrons and RECOMBINE. Similarly, electrons entering the p side recombine with holes. So near the junction, free carriers disappear.

Each hole that leaves the p side leaves behind a negatively charged acceptor ion that cannot move. Each electron that leaves the n side leaves behind a positively charged donor ion. Recombination has the same effect: when an arriving hole removes one of the n side's electrons, another donor ion is left uncovered, and arriving electrons uncover acceptor ions on the p side in the same way. These ions are fixed in the crystal lattice.

Key points

  • Concentration differences drive holes p → n and electrons n → p.
  • Carriers that cross recombine with the majority carriers on the other side.
  • Fixed ions are left uncovered: − acceptors on the p side, + donors on the n side.

The depletion (space-charge) region

After diffusion and recombination, a thin region on both sides of the junction is left with almost NO free carriers. It contains only the fixed ions: negative acceptor ions on the p side and positive donor ions on the n side. This region is called the DEPLETION REGION (it is depleted of mobile carriers) or the SPACE-CHARGE REGION (it contains a net fixed charge). Outside it, the material is still neutral: these are the NEUTRAL p and n REGIONS.

The positive ions on the n side and the negative ions on the p side create an ELECTRIC FIELD inside the depletion region, pointing from the n side to the p side (from + charge to − charge). With x measured from the p side toward the n side, this field points in the −x direction, so E(x) is negative.

To describe the region simply, the DEPLETION APPROXIMATION is used: inside the depletion region, the free carrier concentration is taken as zero, and outside it the material is exactly neutral. The edges are at x = −xp on the p side and x = +xn on the n side.

Real edges are not perfectly sharp: the carrier concentration falls from its full value to nearly zero over a few Debye lengths (about 0.04 µm for 1016 cm−3). This is small compared with the width of the region, so the approximation works well.

Key points

  • Depletion region = space-charge region: fixed ions, almost no free carriers.
  • Its field points from n to p and is strongest at the junction.
  • Depletion approximation: no carriers inside, exact neutrality outside, sharp edges at −xp and +xn.
  • Real edges are smeared over only a few Debye lengths.

Equilibrium: drift balances diffusion

The electric field in the depletion region pushes holes toward the p side and electrons toward the n side. Motion caused by a field is DRIFT, and here it is in the opposite direction to diffusion. The drift current is carried mainly by minority carriers: holes generated by heat in the n side and electrons generated in the p side wander to the edge of the depletion region and are swept across by the field.

As more ions are uncovered, the field grows and the potential barrier across the region rises. Fewer and fewer majority carriers have enough energy to diffuse over this barrier, so the diffusion current falls. Eventually the diffusion of each carrier type exactly balances its drift. The junction is then in THERMAL EQUILIBRIUM:

  • the hole diffusion current equals the hole drift current, in opposite directions;
  • the electron diffusion current equals the electron drift current, in opposite directions;
  • the NET current through the junction is zero.

Carriers still cross the junction all the time in both directions; the flows simply cancel. The balance holds because the depletion region and field have grown to exactly the size needed. If diffusion were larger, more ions would be uncovered and the barrier would rise; if drift were larger, the region would shrink. Either way the junction returns to balance.

Key points

  • Drift (field-driven, mostly minority carriers) opposes diffusion (concentration-driven, majority carriers).
  • A growing barrier cuts diffusion until it equals drift.
  • In equilibrium, hole and electron currents each balance: net current = 0.
  • Carriers keep crossing both ways; only the net flow is zero.

How wide is the depletion region?

The whole junction must remain electrically neutral, so the total negative charge on the p side equals the total positive charge on the n side:

NA · xp = ND · xn

This means the depletion region extends FARTHER into the LIGHTLY doped side. In our example, the n side (ND = 1016 cm−3) is ten times more lightly doped than the p side (NA = 1017 cm−3), so xn is ten times larger than xp.

The total width at zero bias is:

W = xn + xp = √[ (2εs·Vbi / q) · (NA + ND) / (NA·ND) ]

where Vbi is the built-in potential (topic 2.2; about 0.754 V here, from Vbi = (kT/q)·ln(NAND/ni2) with ni = 1.5 × 1010 cm−3) and εs = 11.7 × 8.854 × 10−14 F/cm is the permittivity of silicon. Some textbooks write Vbi as V0, and some use ni = 1.45 × 1010 or 1 × 1010 cm−3; with 1 × 1010 the built-in potential comes out about 0.775 V instead.

Each side's share follows from charge balance: xn = W·NA/(NA + ND) and xp = W·ND/(NA + ND).

For the example junction: W ≈ 0.33 µm, with xn ≈ 0.30 µm and xp ≈ 0.03 µm.

When one side is much more heavily doped than the other (a ONE-SIDED or p+n / n+p junction), almost the entire depletion region lies in the lightly doped side, and W depends mainly on the lighter doping.

Key points

  • Charge balance: NA·xp = ND·xn.
  • The region extends farther into the lightly doped side.
  • W = √[(2εsVbi/q)(NA + ND)/(NAND)] ≈ 0.33 µm for the example.
  • One-sided (p+n) junction: W is set almost entirely by the lighter doping.

Charge, field and potential profiles

Using the depletion approximation, the profiles across the junction are simple:

CHARGE DENSITY: −q·NA from −xp to 0, +q·ND from 0 to +xn, and zero elsewhere. Two rectangles of opposite sign with equal areas.

ELECTRIC FIELD: from Poisson's equation (Gauss's law in one dimension), the field magnitude rises linearly through the p-side depletion region, reaches its MAXIMUM at the metallurgical junction, and falls linearly back to zero at the n-side edge. It is a triangle. Because the field points from n to p, E(x) is negative and the triangle is drawn below the axis. The maximum magnitude is:

|Emax| = q·ND·xn / εs = q·NA·xp / εs

For the example, |Emax| ≈ 4.6 × 104 V/cm.

POTENTIAL: taking V = 0 at the p-side edge, integrating the field gives a potential made of two parabolic pieces. It rises smoothly from the p side to the n side, and the total rise across the depletion region equals the built-in potential Vbi. The area under the field triangle equals Vbi:

Vbi = ½ · |Emax| · W

Key points

  • ρ(x): two rectangles, −qNA and +qND, of equal area.
  • E(x): a triangle with |Emax| = qNDxn/εs at the junction (≈ 4.6 × 104 V/cm here).
  • V(x): two parabolas rising by Vbi from the p side to the n side.
  • Area under the field triangle = Vbi = ½|Emax|W.

Summary

A pn junction is a single crystal doped p-type on one side and n-type on the other. Holes and electrons diffuse across and recombine, leaving a depletion region of fixed ions: negative on the p side, positive on the n side. These ions create an electric field from n to p, which drives a drift current that balances diffusion, so the net current is zero in equilibrium. Charge balance gives NA·xp = ND·xn, so the depletion region extends mostly into the lighter-doped side. The field is triangular with its maximum at the junction, and the potential rises by Vbi across the region.

Key points

  • Diffusion + recombination → depletion region of fixed ions.
  • Field from n to p; drift balances diffusion; net current = 0.
  • NAxp = NDxn: the region lies mostly in the lighter-doped side.
  • Triangular field, parabolic potential, total rise Vbi.
