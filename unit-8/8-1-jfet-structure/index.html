JFET Structure and Working

A transistor that squeezes its own channel with a voltage

What is a field-effect transistor?

A FIELD-EFFECT TRANSISTOR (FET) is a transistor in which the current is controlled by an ELECTRIC FIELD created by a voltage, not by an input current. Its three terminals are:

  • SOURCE (S): where the charge carriers enter the device;
  • DRAIN (D): where the carriers leave;
  • GATE (G): the control terminal.

The current flows from source to drain through a path of semiconductor called the CHANNEL. The gate voltage changes how wide or how conductive the channel is, and so controls the drain current ID.

Two differences from the BJT:

  • The FET is VOLTAGE-CONTROLLED: the gate draws almost no current. The BJT is current-controlled: it needs a base current.
  • The FET is UNIPOLAR: the current is carried by only ONE type of carrier (electrons in an n-channel FET, holes in a p-channel FET). The BJT is bipolar.

There are two main families: the JUNCTION FET (JFET), covered first, and the MOSFET (from topic 8.4).

Key points

  • Terminals: source (carriers enter), drain (carriers leave), gate (control).
  • The gate voltage sets the width or conductivity of the channel, and so ID.
  • Voltage-controlled (gate current ≈ 0) and unipolar (one carrier type).
  • Two families: JFET (first) and MOSFET (from 8.4).

Structure of an n-channel JFET

An n-channel JFET is built from a bar of lightly doped N-type silicon. This bar is the CHANNEL. Metal contacts at its two ends form the SOURCE and the DRAIN.

On both sides of the channel (or on top and in the substrate below), heavily doped P+ regions are formed. These P+ regions are connected together and form the GATE. Each P+ gate region makes a PN junction with the N channel.

Because the gate is P+ (heavily doped) and the channel is N (lightly doped), any depletion region at these junctions extends mostly into the CHANNEL, not into the gate.

A p-channel JFET is the opposite: a P-type channel with N+ gate regions.

Circuit symbols: a vertical line for the channel with source and drain at its ends and the gate drawn as a line with an arrow. The arrow points from P to N: INTO the channel for an n-channel JFET, OUT OF the channel for a p-channel JFET.

Key points

  • Lightly doped N bar = channel; metal contacts at its ends = source and drain.
  • Heavily doped P+ regions on both sides, joined together = gate.
  • Depletion regions extend mostly into the lightly doped channel.
  • Symbol arrow points P → N: into the channel (n-channel), out of it (p-channel).

Operation with VGS = 0 and small VDS

Connect the source to ground, tie the gate to the source (VGS = 0), and apply a small positive voltage VDS to the drain.

Electrons, the majority carriers of the N channel, flow from the source through the channel to the drain. Conventional current ID flows from drain to source. Thin depletion regions exist at the gate junctions (from their built-in voltage), but most of the channel is open.

For small VDS the channel behaves like an ordinary RESISTOR: doubling VDS doubles ID.

Key points

  • Source grounded, gate tied to source (VGS = 0), small positive VDS.
  • Electrons flow source → drain; conventional ID flows drain → source.
  • Thin built-in depletion regions; most of the channel is open.
  • Small VDS: the channel acts as a resistor.

Controlling the channel with the gate

Now make the gate NEGATIVE with respect to the source (VGS < 0). This REVERSE BIASES the gate-channel junctions. A larger reverse bias makes the depletion regions wider, and since they grow mainly into the channel, the conducting part of the channel becomes NARROWER. A narrower channel has a higher resistance, so for the same VDS the drain current ID becomes smaller.

If VGS is made negative enough, the two depletion regions meet across the whole channel. The channel is completely closed and ID falls to practically zero. The gate-source voltage at which this happens is called the PINCH-OFF VOLTAGE VP (also written VGS(off)). For our example JFET, VP = −4 V.

In normal use the gate junction is always reverse biased, so the gate current is only a tiny leakage current (nanoamperes or less). This gives the JFET a very high input resistance.

Never forward bias the gate of a JFET: the junction would conduct like a diode and the high input resistance would be lost.

Key points

  • VGS < 0 reverse biases the gate junctions; depletion widens into the channel.
  • Narrower channel → higher resistance → smaller ID.
  • At VGS = VP (−4 V here) the channel closes and ID ≈ 0.
  • Gate current is only nA leakage; never forward bias the gate.

The effect of VDS: pinch-off at the drain end

The drain voltage also affects the channel shape. Because current flows along the channel, the voltage inside the channel rises gradually from 0 V at the source to VDS at the drain. The reverse bias between gate and channel is therefore SMALLEST near the source and LARGEST near the drain. As a result, the depletion regions are WEDGE-SHAPED: narrow at the source end and wide at the drain end.

As VDS increases, the channel near the drain becomes narrower and narrower. When the gate-to-drain reverse voltage reaches |VP|, the channel just closes at the drain end. This happens at:

VDS = VGS − VP   (for VGS = 0 and VP = −4 V, at VDS = 4 V)

This condition is also called PINCH-OFF. Beyond it, increasing VDS hardly increases ID any more: the current SATURATES. With VGS = 0, the saturated current is the maximum drain current of the JFET, called IDSS (10 mA for our example).

The current does not stop at pinch-off. Electrons still reach the narrow pinched region and are swept across it by the strong electric field there, much as electrons are swept across the collector junction of a BJT.

Key points

  • Channel voltage rises from 0 at S to VDS at D: wedge-shaped depletion.
  • The channel closes at the drain end when VDS = VGS − VP (4 V for VGS = 0).
  • Beyond pinch-off ID saturates; at VGS = 0 it is IDSS = 10 mA.
  • Current still flows: electrons are swept across the pinched region.

Summary

A JFET is a voltage-controlled, unipolar transistor. In an n-channel JFET, electrons flow from source to drain through an N channel squeezed by reverse-biased P+ gate junctions. Making VGS more negative narrows the channel and reduces ID; at VGS = VP the channel is closed. Increasing VDS pinches the channel at the drain end at VDS = VGS − VP, after which ID saturates. The gate draws almost no current. A p-channel JFET works the same way with all polarities reversed.

Key facts

  • VGS ≤ 0 for n-channel
  • ID = 0 at VGS = VP
  • Pinch-off at VDS = VGS − VP
  • IDSS = ID at VGS = 0 in saturation
  • IG ≈ 0

Key points

  • Voltage-controlled, unipolar: electrons through an N channel (n-channel JFET).
  • More negative VGS narrows the channel; closed at VGS = VP.
  • Pinch-off at VDS = VGS − VP, then ID saturates.
  • IG ≈ 0; p-channel is the same with polarities reversed.
