TOPIC 8.4

MOSFET Basics: The MOS Capacitor

Gate, oxide and silicon: how a gate voltage gathers or repels charge without any current

The MOS structure

The MOSFET, the transistor inside almost every modern chip, is built on a simple sandwich called the MOS STRUCTURE:

  • METAL: the top electrode, called the GATE. In modern chips it is often heavily doped polysilicon or a metal.
  • OXIDE: a very thin insulating layer of silicon dioxide (SiO2), only a few nanometres thick in modern devices.
  • SEMICONDUCTOR: the silicon underneath, called the SUBSTRATE or BODY. Here we use p-type silicon.

Because the oxide is an insulator, NO DC current flows through the gate. The structure is a capacitor: the gate is one plate, the silicon is the other, and the oxide is the dielectric. Its capacitance per unit area is:

Cox = εox / tox

where εox = 3.9 × 8.85 × 10−12 F/m is the permittivity of SiO2. For tox = 10 nm, Cox ≈ 3.45 × 10−3 F/m², or about 3.45 fF per square micrometre.

The key idea of this topic: the gate voltage can pull charges toward or push them away from the silicon surface just under the oxide, without any current flowing through the oxide.

Key points

  • MOS = metal (gate), oxide (SiO2) and semiconductor (substrate or body).
  • The oxide is an insulator, so no DC current flows into the gate.
  • It is a capacitor: Cox = εox/tox ≈ 3.45 fF/µm² for 10 nm.
  • The gate voltage moves charge at the silicon surface without any gate current.

Accumulation (negative gate voltage)

Apply a NEGATIVE voltage to the gate (more precisely, below the flat-band voltage VFB). The negative charge on the gate attracts the positive majority carriers of the p-type substrate, the HOLES, toward the surface. A thin layer with extra holes forms just under the oxide. This is called ACCUMULATION.

The silicon surface is now strongly p-type. The capacitor behaves like a simple parallel-plate capacitor with capacitance Cox: negative charge on the gate, equal positive charge (holes) right at the surface.

Key points

  • Accumulation happens for VG below the flat-band voltage VFB.
  • The negative gate attracts holes, the majority carriers, to the surface.
  • The surface becomes strongly p-type.
  • It acts as a parallel-plate capacitor: C = Cox.

Depletion (small positive gate voltage)

Now make the gate voltage POSITIVE (above VFB but below the threshold voltage VT). The positive gate repels the holes away from the surface. Left behind near the surface are the fixed, negatively charged ACCEPTOR IONS, which cannot move. A region with no free carriers, the DEPLETION REGION, forms under the oxide.

The positive charge on the gate is balanced by the negative charge of these fixed ions. As the gate voltage increases, more holes are pushed away and the depletion region grows deeper.

The total capacitance is now Cox in SERIES with the depletion capacitance, so it is SMALLER than Cox, and it keeps decreasing as the depletion region gets wider.

Key points

  • Depletion: VFB < VG < VT.
  • Holes are pushed away, leaving fixed negative acceptor ions.
  • The depletion region grows deeper as VG rises.
  • Cox in series with the depletion capacitance: C is smaller than Cox.

Inversion (gate voltage above threshold)

If the gate voltage is increased further, the positive gate begins to attract the MINORITY carriers of the p-type substrate, the ELECTRONS, which are always present in small numbers (generated thermally). They collect in a very thin layer right at the surface.

When the gate voltage reaches the THRESHOLD VOLTAGE VT, the electron concentration at the surface becomes as large as the hole concentration deep in the substrate. The surface has turned from p-type into n-type: it has been INVERTED. This thin electron layer is called the INVERSION LAYER.

The threshold condition is that the surface potential reaches twice the Fermi potential, 2φF, where:

φF = (kT/q) · ln(NA / ni)

For NA = 1017 cm−3 and ni = 1010 cm−3 at 300 K, φF ≈ 0.42 V, so the surface band bending at threshold is about 0.84 V.

Above VT, the depletion region stops growing (it has reached its maximum width). Any extra gate charge is balanced by MORE ELECTRONS in the inversion layer:

Qinv ≈ Cox · (VG − VT) (charge per unit area)

This inversion layer is exactly the conducting CHANNEL of a MOSFET. In the next topic, adding n+ source and drain regions on either side will let current flow through it.

Key points

  • Above VT the gate gathers electrons, the minority carriers, at the surface.
  • Threshold: surface band bending = 2φF ≈ 0.84 V for NA = 1017 cm−3.
  • The depletion region stops at its maximum width; extra charge is Qinv ≈ Cox(VG − VT).
  • The inversion layer is the channel of the MOSFET.

The band diagram view

The same three regimes can be seen on the energy band diagram of the silicon near the surface. The Fermi level EF in the substrate is flat; the bands (Ec, Ei, Ev) BEND near the surface as the gate voltage changes.

  • Accumulation: the bands bend UP at the surface. Ev moves closer to EF, so there are more holes.
  • Depletion: the bands bend DOWN slightly. Ev moves away from EF, so holes are pushed away.
  • Inversion: the bands bend down so far that Ei at the surface drops BELOW EF. Where Ei is below EF, the material behaves as n-type. At threshold, EF is as far above Ei at the surface as it is below Ei in the bulk (a total bending of 2φF).

Key points

  • EF stays flat; Ec, Ei and Ev bend near the surface.
  • Accumulation: bands bend up, Ev nears EF.
  • Depletion: bands bend down slightly.
  • Inversion: Ei drops below EF at the surface; threshold at a bending of 2φF.

The C-V curve

Measuring the capacitance of a MOS capacitor while sweeping the gate voltage gives the C-V CURVE, a standard way to check the quality of an oxide in chip fabrication.

  • In accumulation, C = Cox (maximum).
  • In depletion, C falls as the depletion region widens.
  • In inversion, the result depends on the measurement frequency. At LOW frequency the inversion electrons can follow the signal, so C rises back to Cox. At HIGH frequency they cannot, and C stays at its minimum value.

Key points

  • The C-V curve is measured by sweeping VG; it checks oxide quality.
  • Accumulation: C = Cox, the maximum.
  • Depletion: C falls as the depletion region widens.
  • Inversion: low frequency returns to Cox; high frequency stays at the minimum.

Summary

The MOS capacitor is a gate, a thin oxide and a semiconductor body, with capacitance Cox = εox/tox and no DC gate current. On p-type silicon: negative gate voltage gives accumulation of holes; small positive voltage gives depletion; a voltage above the threshold VT gives an inversion layer of electrons at the surface. That inversion layer is the channel of the n-channel MOSFET, and its charge is about Cox·(VG − VT).

Key formulas

  • Cox = εox/tox
  • εox = 3.9ε0
  • φF = (kT/q)ln(NA/ni)
  • Threshold when surface bending = 2φF
  • Qinv ≈ Cox(VG − VT)

Key points

  • Gate, thin oxide, body: Cox = εox/tox, no DC gate current.
  • p-type body: accumulation (VG < VFB), depletion, then inversion (VG > VT).
  • Threshold when the surface bending reaches 2φF.
  • The inversion layer is the MOSFET channel, Qinv ≈ Cox(VG − VT).
