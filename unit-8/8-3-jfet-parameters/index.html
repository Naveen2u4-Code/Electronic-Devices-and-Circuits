TOPIC 8.3

JFET Parameters: gm, rd and μ

Three slopes at the Q-point that describe how a JFET amplifies

Why we need small-signal parameters

In an amplifier, the JFET is biased at a steady operating point (the Q-POINT) in the saturation region, and a small AC signal makes VGS and VDS vary slightly around it. To calculate how much the drain current changes, we do not need the whole curved characteristic. We only need how STEEP the characteristics are at the Q-point.

Three small-signal parameters describe this:

  • the TRANSCONDUCTANCE gm: how much ID changes when VGS changes;
  • the DRAIN RESISTANCE rd: how much ID changes when VDS changes;
  • the AMPLIFICATION FACTOR μ: how they combine.

Key points

  • An amplifying JFET sits at a Q-point in the saturation region.
  • A small signal moves VGS and VDS only slightly around Q.
  • Over such a small swing the curve is nearly straight, so only its slope matters.
  • The slopes are described by gm, rd and μ.

Transconductance gm

The TRANSCONDUCTANCE gm (also written gfs or yfs on datasheets) is the change in drain current caused by a small change in gate-source voltage, with VDS held constant:

gm = ΔID / ΔVGS (VDS constant)

It is measured in siemens (S), usually millisiemens (mS). On the transfer characteristic, gm is the SLOPE of the curve at the Q-point. It tells how strongly the gate controls the current, so it is the most important parameter for amplification.

Differentiating Shockley's equation gives:

gm = gm0 · (1 − VGS/VP), where gm0 = 2·IDSS / |VP|

An equivalent form is gm = gm0 · sqrt(ID / IDSS).

gm0 is the largest transconductance, at VGS = 0. For our JFET, gm0 = 2 × 10 mA / 4 V = 5 mS. At VGS = −2 V, gm = 5 × (1 − 0.5) = 2.5 mS. At VGS = VP, gm = 0.

Key points

  • gm = ΔID/ΔVGS at constant VDS: the slope of the transfer curve.
  • gm = gm0(1 − VGS/VP), with gm0 = 2IDSS/|VP| = 5 mS here.
  • gm falls in a straight line from gm0 at VGS = 0 to zero at VP.
  • At VGS = −2 V, gm = 2.5 mS.

Drain resistance rd

The DRAIN RESISTANCE rd (also called the output resistance; its inverse 1/rd is listed on datasheets as gos or yos) describes how much the drain current changes when VDS changes, with VGS held constant:

rd = ΔVDS / ΔID (VGS constant)

On the drain characteristics it is the inverse of the SLOPE of a curve. In the saturation region the curves are nearly flat, so rd is large, typically tens of kilohms to megohms. The slight slope comes from channel-length modulation, and rd can be estimated as:

rd ≈ 1 / (λ·ID)

For λ = 0.01 V−1 and ID = 2.5 mA, rd ≈ 40 kΩ.

In the ohmic region the curves are steep, so rd is small: there the JFET acts as a low-value resistor.

Key points

  • rd = ΔVDS/ΔID at constant VGS: the inverse slope of a drain curve.
  • In saturation the curves are nearly flat, so rd is large: rd ≈ 1/(λ·ID).
  • At ID = 2.5 mA and λ = 0.01 V−1, rd ≈ 40 kΩ.
  • In the ohmic region the curves are steep, so rd is small.

Amplification factor μ

The AMPLIFICATION FACTOR μ compares how effective the gate voltage and the drain voltage are at controlling the current. It is the change in VDS needed to cancel the effect of a change in VGS, keeping ID constant:

μ = − ΔVDS / ΔVGS (ID constant)

The three parameters are related simply:

μ = gm · rd

For our example at VGS = −2 V: μ = 2.5 mS × 40 kΩ = 100.

μ is the largest voltage gain a single JFET can give, reached only when the drain load resistance is much larger than rd. With a practical drain resistor RD, the gain is gm·(RD ∥ rd), which is smaller than μ.

Key points

  • μ = −ΔVDS/ΔVGS at constant ID: how much more the gate controls ID than the drain.
  • μ = gm·rd; at VGS = −2 V, μ = 2.5 mS × 40 kΩ = 100.
  • μ is the largest voltage gain one JFET can give.
  • With a drain resistor the gain is gm·(RD ∥ rd), smaller than μ.

How the parameters change with the Q-point

The parameters are not constants; they depend on where the JFET is biased:

  • gm is largest near VGS = 0 (large ID) and falls to zero at VGS = VP.
  • rd ≈ 1/(λ·ID) is LARGER at small currents.
  • μ = gm·rd INCREASES at lower currents, because rd rises faster than gm falls (for this model μ is proportional to 1/sqrt(ID)).

Designers choose the Q-point as a compromise: a larger current gives more gm (more gain), but uses more power, and the swing available for the signal must be kept in the saturation region.

Datasheets list typical gm (often measured at VGS = 0, i.e. gm0) and output admittance yos.

Key points

  • gm is largest at VGS = 0 and zero at VP.
  • rd ≈ 1/(λ·ID) grows as the current falls.
  • μ = gm·rd grows at low current (∝ 1/sqrt(ID) in this model).
  • The Q-point is a compromise between gain, power and signal swing.

Summary

gm = ΔID/ΔVGS is the slope of the transfer curve: gm = gm0(1 − VGS/VP) with gm0 = 2IDSS/|VP|. rd = ΔVDS/ΔID is the inverse slope of a drain curve, large in saturation (rd ≈ 1/(λID)). μ = gm·rd is the maximum voltage gain. All three depend on the Q-point.

Key formulas

  • gm = ΔID/ΔVGS
  • gm = gm0(1 − VGS/VP)
  • gm0 = 2IDSS/|VP|
  • gm = gm0·sqrt(ID/IDSS)
  • rd = ΔVDS/ΔID ≈ 1/(λID)
  • μ = gm·rd

Key points

  • gm: slope of the transfer curve, gm0(1 − VGS/VP).
  • rd: inverse slope of a drain curve, large in saturation, ≈ 1/(λID).
  • μ = gm·rd is the maximum voltage gain.
  • All three depend on the Q-point.
