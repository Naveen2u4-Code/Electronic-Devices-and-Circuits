TOPIC 8.5

Enhancement MOSFET

A normally-off transistor whose channel is created by the gate voltage

Structure of an n-channel enhancement MOSFET

Take the MOS capacitor of topic 8.4 (gate, thin oxide, p-type substrate) and add two heavily doped n+ regions in the substrate, one on each side of the gate. These are the SOURCE and the DRAIN. The result is an n-channel MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor).

The device has four terminals: gate (G), source (S), drain (D) and body or substrate (B). The body is usually connected to the source.

The region of substrate under the gate, between source and drain, is where the channel will form. Its length is the CHANNEL LENGTH L (the distance from source to drain) and its width is the CHANNEL WIDTH W. In modern chips L can be just a few tens of nanometres.

With zero gate voltage there is NO channel. Between the n+ source and n+ drain lies p-type silicon, which forms two back-to-back PN junctions. Whatever the polarity of VDS, one of them is reverse biased, so no current flows. Because the device is OFF with no gate voltage, and a channel must be created ("enhanced") by the gate voltage, it is called an ENHANCEMENT MOSFET or "normally-off" MOSFET.

Key points

  • MOS capacitor + two n+ regions = n-channel MOSFET (source and drain).
  • Four terminals: G, S, D and B; the body is usually tied to the source.
  • Channel length L (source to drain) and width W; L can be tens of nanometres.
  • At VGS = 0 two back-to-back junctions block current: normally off.

Creating the channel: the threshold voltage

Apply a positive voltage VGS between gate and source (keep VDS small). As in the MOS capacitor, holes are pushed away from the surface under the gate, creating a depletion region. When VGS reaches the THRESHOLD VOLTAGE VT, an inversion layer of electrons forms at the surface. This thin n-type layer CONNECTS the n+ source to the n+ drain: a conducting channel now exists. Because it is created by the gate, it is called an INDUCED channel.

The larger VGS is above VT, the more electrons are in the channel (charge ≈ Cox·(VGS − VT) per unit area), so the channel conducts better. The quantity VGS − VT is called the OVERDRIVE voltage (or effective voltage).

Typical VT values are a few tenths of a volt in modern chips and about 1 to 4 V in discrete power MOSFETs. We use VT = 1 V.

The threshold voltage also rises if the source is at a higher voltage than the body (the BODY EFFECT), which is why the body is usually tied to the source in discrete circuits.

Key points

  • At VGS = VT an inversion layer links source and drain: an induced channel.
  • Channel charge ≈ Cox(VGS − VT) per unit area.
  • VGS − VT is the overdrive voltage; here VT = 1 V.
  • A source above the body raises VT (body effect).

Small VDS: the MOSFET as a resistor

With a channel present and a small VDS, the channel has nearly the same thickness everywhere, and it behaves like a resistor. The drain current is proportional to VDS:

ID ≈ 2·Kn·(VGS − VT)·VDS

The channel resistance is rDS = 1 / [2·Kn·(VGS − VT)], which DECREASES as VGS increases. This is the TRIODE (or linear, or ohmic) region. The MOSFET can be used as a voltage-controlled resistor or, when VGS is large, as a closed switch with a low on-resistance.

Example: with Kn = 0.5 mA/V² and VGS = 5 V, rDS = 1 / (2 × 0.5 mA/V² × 4 V) = 250 Ω.

Key points

  • At small VDS the channel is uniform and acts as a resistor.
  • ID ≈ 2Kn(VGS − VT)VDS: straight lines through the origin.
  • rDS = 1/[2Kn(VGS − VT)] falls as VGS rises; 250 Ω at VGS = 5 V.
  • Used as a voltage-controlled resistor or a closed switch.

Larger VDS: channel tapering and pinch-off

As VDS increases, the voltage inside the channel rises from 0 at the source to VDS at the drain. The gate-to-channel voltage is therefore VGS at the source end but only VGS − VDS at the drain end. Fewer electrons are induced near the drain, so the channel TAPERS, becoming thinner toward the drain. The resistance increases and the ID-VDS curve bends.

The full triode equation is:

ID = Kn·[2(VGS − VT)·VDS − VDS²]

When the gate-to-drain voltage falls to VT, the channel just disappears at the drain end. This is PINCH-OFF, and it happens at:

VDS(sat) = VGS − VT

For VGS = 3 V and VT = 1 V, pinch-off occurs at VDS = 2 V.

As with the JFET, current still flows: electrons reaching the pinch-off point are swept across the short depleted region to the drain by the strong field there.

Key points

  • The channel voltage rises from 0 at the source to VDS at the drain, so the channel tapers.
  • Triode: ID = Kn[2(VGS − VT)VDS − VDS²]; the curve bends.
  • Pinch-off at VDS(sat) = VGS − VT (2 V for VGS = 3 V).
  • Electrons are swept across the pinched region, so current keeps flowing.

Saturation: the square law

Beyond pinch-off, increasing VDS hardly changes the current, because the voltage across the channel stays at about VGS − VT; the extra drain voltage is dropped across the small pinched region. The drain current SATURATES at:

ID = Kn · (VGS − VT)²

where Kn = ½·μn·Cox·(W/L). This is the SQUARE LAW of the MOSFET. A wider (larger W) or shorter (smaller L) transistor gives more current.

Example (Kn = 0.5 mA/V², VT = 1 V): VGS = 3 V gives ID = 0.5 × 2² = 2 mA; VGS = 5 V gives 8 mA.

In reality the pinch-off point moves slightly toward the source as VDS increases, making the effective channel shorter, so ID still rises a little. This CHANNEL-LENGTH MODULATION is modelled by:

ID = Kn·(VGS − VT)²·(1 + λ·VDS)

The saturation region is used for AMPLIFIERS. (Note: MOSFET "saturation" corresponds to the BJT "active" region, not to BJT saturation.)

Key points

  • Beyond pinch-off: ID = Kn(VGS − VT)², the square law.
  • Kn = ½μnCox(W/L): wider or shorter means more current.
  • Channel-length modulation: a slight rise, (1 + λVDS).
  • MOSFET saturation is used for amplifiers (like the BJT active region).

Symbols and the p-channel MOSFET

The enhancement MOSFET symbol has a gate line separated from the channel (showing the insulating oxide), and the channel line is drawn BROKEN into three segments, showing that no channel exists until it is induced. The body terminal has an arrow: pointing IN for n-channel (from p body to n channel), pointing OUT for p-channel. Simplified symbols used in digital circuits often omit the body and instead show an arrow on the source, or a small circle on the gate for the p-channel device.

A P-CHANNEL enhancement MOSFET (PMOS) is the complement: n-type substrate, p+ source and drain, and a channel of HOLES induced by a NEGATIVE gate voltage. Its threshold voltage is negative (for example VT = −1 V), and it conducts when VGS < VT. All voltages and currents are reversed. Holes have lower mobility than electrons (about two to three times lower in silicon), so a PMOS must be made wider than an NMOS to carry the same current. Using NMOS and PMOS together gives CMOS (topic 8.12).

Key points

  • Broken channel line = enhancement (no channel until induced); the gate line is separate.
  • Body arrow points in for NMOS, out for PMOS.
  • PMOS: n-substrate, p+ source and drain, hole channel, VT negative.
  • Holes are slower, so a PMOS is made about 2 to 3 times wider; together they form CMOS.

Summary

An n-channel enhancement MOSFET has no channel at VGS = 0. When VGS exceeds VT, an inversion layer links source and drain. For small VDS it acts as a resistor controlled by VGS (triode). At VDS = VGS − VT the channel pinches off at the drain, and beyond that ID saturates at Kn(VGS − VT)². The broken channel line in the symbol marks it as an enhancement device. PMOS works the same way with all polarities reversed.

Key formulas

  • Cutoff, VGS < VT: ID = 0
  • Triode: ID = Kn[2(VGS − VT)VDS − VDS²]
  • Saturation: ID = Kn(VGS − VT)²(1 + λVDS)
  • Kn = ½μnCox(W/L)
  • VDS(sat) = VGS − VT
  • rDS ≈ 1/[2Kn(VGS − VT)]

Key points

  • No channel at VGS = 0; above VT an inversion layer links S and D.
  • Small VDS: triode, a resistor controlled by VGS.
  • VDS ≥ VGS − VT: pinch-off, ID saturates at Kn(VGS − VT)².
  • Broken channel line = enhancement; PMOS has all polarities reversed.
