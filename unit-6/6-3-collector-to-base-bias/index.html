TOPIC 6.3

Collector-to-Base Bias (Collector Feedback Bias)

Move one wire, and the transistor starts to correct its own Q-point

Your device asks for reduced motion, so every animation starts paused. Press Play on any animation to run it. Sliders and buttons still update the picture.

The circuit: one wire moved

Collector-to-base bias looks almost the same as fixed bias, with one important change: the base resistor RB is connected from the COLLECTOR to the base, instead of from VCC to the base.

The collector resistor RC still connects VCC to the collector, and the emitter is still grounded. But now the voltage that drives current through RB is the collector voltage VC, not the fixed supply.

Since VC depends on how much current the transistor is drawing, the output of the transistor now affects its own input. This is called FEEDBACK, and that is why the circuit is also known as COLLECTOR FEEDBACK BIAS.

Key points

  • Same parts as fixed bias, but RB runs from the collector to the base.
  • RC still goes from VCC to the collector; the emitter is grounded.
  • The current through RB is driven by VC, not by VCC.
  • The output affects the input: feedback, hence "collector feedback bias".

How the feedback stabilizes IC

Suppose IC tries to increase, because the temperature rises or because the transistor has a higher β. Follow the chain:

  1. IC increases.
  2. More current flows through RC, so the voltage drop across RC increases.
  3. The collector voltage VC = VCC − (IC + IB)·RC therefore FALLS.
  4. The voltage across RB (VC − VBE) falls, so the base current IB DECREASES.
  5. A smaller IB pulls IC back down.

The circuit automatically opposes any change in IC. This is NEGATIVE FEEDBACK, and it makes the Q-point much more stable than in fixed bias.

A useful extra property: since VCB = VC − VB = IB·RB is always positive, the collector can never fall below the base voltage. So the transistor can NEVER go into deep saturation with this circuit.

Key points

  • IC ↑ → drop across RC ↑ → VC ↓ → IB ↓ → IC ↓.
  • The circuit opposes any change in IC: negative feedback.
  • The Q-point is much more stable than in fixed bias.
  • VCB = IB·RB > 0, so the transistor never goes into deep saturation.

Finding the Q-point

The current through RC is IC + IB (both the collector current and the base current flow through it).

LOOP through RC, RB and the base-emitter junction:

VCC = (IC + IB)·RC + IB·RB + VBE

Using IC = β·IB, so IC + IB = (β + 1)·IB:

VCC − VBE = IB·[RB + (β + 1)·RC]

IB = (VCC − VBE) / [RB + (β + 1)·RC]

Then IC = β·IB and VCE = VCC − (IC + IB)·RC.

Worked example (VCC = 12 V, RB = 180 kΩ, RC = 2 kΩ, β = 100):

  • IB = 11.3 / (180 kΩ + 101 × 2 kΩ) = 11.3 / 382 kΩ ≈ 29.6 µA
  • IC = 100 × 29.6 µA ≈ 2.96 mA
  • VCE = 12 − (2.96 + 0.03) mA × 2 kΩ ≈ 6.0 V

Notice the term (β + 1)·RC in the denominator. It means a larger β automatically reduces IB, which is the feedback written as a formula.

Key points

  • RC carries IC + IB = (β + 1)·IB.
  • Loop: VCC = (IC + IB)·RC + IB·RB + VBE, giving IB = (VCC − VBE)/[RB + (β + 1)RC].
  • Example: 29.6 µA, 2.96 mA, 6.0 V.
  • The (β + 1)·RC term makes a larger β reduce IB.

How much better than fixed bias?

Compare the two circuits when β changes from 100 to 200 (both designed for about 3 mA and 6 V at β = 100):

FIXED BIAS: IC goes from 2.90 mA to 5.80 mA (+100%). VCE falls to about 0.4 V, at the edge of saturation.

COLLECTOR-TO-BASE BIAS: IB falls from 29.6 µA to 11.3 / (180 kΩ + 201 × 2 kΩ) ≈ 19.4 µA, so IC goes from 2.96 mA to only about 3.88 mA (+31%). VCE falls to about 4.2 V, still safely in the active region.

The stability factor for this circuit is:

S = (1 + β) / [1 + β·RC / (RC + RB)]

For the example, S ≈ 101 / (1 + 100 × 2/182) ≈ 48, about half the fixed-bias value of 101. The larger RC is compared with RB, the stronger the feedback and the better the stability.

Key points

  • β 100 → 200, fixed bias: IC 2.90 → 5.80 mA (+100%), VCE ≈ 0.4 V.
  • β 100 → 200, collector-to-base: IC 2.96 → 3.88 mA (+31%), VCE ≈ 4.2 V.
  • S = (1 + β)/[1 + β·RC/(RC + RB)] ≈ 48 for the example, against 101 for fixed bias.
  • Larger RC compared with RB means stronger feedback and better stability.

The drawback: feedback also reduces signal gain

The feedback through RB does not know the difference between a slow DC drift and a fast AC signal. When the circuit is used as an amplifier, the output signal at the collector is also fed back to the base through RB, opposite in phase to the input. This reduces the voltage gain of the amplifier and lowers its input resistance.

A common fix is to split RB into two resistors in series and connect a capacitor from their midpoint to ground. The capacitor shorts the AC signal to ground so it cannot be fed back, while the DC feedback still works.

Also, the stability of this circuit, while better than fixed bias, is still limited. For stability that is nearly independent of β, the voltage divider bias of topic 6.4 is used.

Key points

  • RB feeds back the AC output too, opposite in phase to the input.
  • This lowers the voltage gain and the input resistance.
  • Fix: split RB and bypass the midpoint to ground with a capacitor; DC feedback stays.
  • Stability is still limited; voltage divider bias (6.4) is nearly independent of β.

Summary

Collector-to-base bias connects RB from the collector to the base. Negative feedback through RB reduces IB whenever IC rises, so the Q-point is much more stable than with fixed bias, and the transistor cannot saturate deeply. IB = (VCC − VBE)/[RB + (β + 1)RC], and S = (1 + β)/[1 + β·RC/(RC + RB)]. The feedback also reduces AC gain unless RB is split and bypassed.

Key formulas

  • IB = (VCC − VBE)/[RB + (β + 1)RC]
  • IC = β·IB
  • VCE = VCC − (IC + IB)·RC
  • S = (1 + β)/[1 + β·RC/(RC + RB)]

Key points

  • RB from collector to base gives negative feedback on IC.
  • Much more stable Q-point than fixed bias; no deep saturation.
  • S is about half the fixed-bias value for the example (48 vs 101).
  • AC gain is reduced unless RB is split and bypassed.
