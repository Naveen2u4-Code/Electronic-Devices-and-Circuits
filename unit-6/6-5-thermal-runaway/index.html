TOPIC 6.5

Thermal Runaway

Heat raises current, current raises heat: when the loop wins, the transistor dies

Heat in a transistor

Whenever a transistor conducts, it turns electrical power into heat. The power dissipated is mostly at the collector junction:

PD = VCE × IC

This heat raises the temperature of the junction inside the transistor, called the JUNCTION TEMPERATURE Tj. The heat must flow out through the package to the surroundings (the AMBIENT, at temperature TA). How easily it flows is described by the THERMAL RESISTANCE θJA, in °C per watt:

Tj = TA + θJA × PD

Example: a small TO-92 transistor may have θJA of about 200 °C/W. Dissipating 0.5 W at TA = 25 °C gives Tj ≈ 25 + 200 × 0.5 = 125 °C. Silicon transistors typically have a maximum junction temperature of about 150 °C.

Key points

  • A conducting transistor dissipates PD = VCE × IC, mostly at the collector junction.
  • The heat raises the junction temperature: Tj = TA + θJA × PD.
  • θJA (°C/W) describes how easily heat leaves the package.
  • TO-92, 0.5 W, 25 °C: Tj ≈ 125 °C; the silicon limit is about 150 °C.

The vicious circle

From topic 6.1 we know that the collector current INCREASES with temperature: the leakage ICBO rises (doubling about every 10 °C), VBE falls (about 2.5 mV/°C), and β increases.

Now combine this with the heating effect:

  1. The junction gets warmer.
  2. IC increases.
  3. The power PD = VCE × IC increases.
  4. More power means more heat, so the junction gets even warmer.
  5. Back to step 2.

This is POSITIVE FEEDBACK. If each round of heating causes a larger increase in heat than the package can remove, the temperature and current keep climbing until the transistor is destroyed. This self-destruction is called THERMAL RUNAWAY.

If instead each round causes a smaller increase, the loop settles at a slightly higher but steady temperature. The circuit is then THERMALLY STABLE.

Key points

  • IC rises with temperature (ICBO ↑, VBE ↓, β ↑).
  • Tj ↑ → IC ↑ → PD ↑ → Tj ↑: positive feedback.
  • If each round adds more heat than can be removed: thermal runaway.
  • If each round adds less: the loop settles; the circuit is thermally stable.

The condition for thermal stability

The junction stays stable only if the heat removed rises faster with temperature than the heat produced. The heat the package can remove increases by 1/θJA watts for every 1 °C rise in Tj. So the condition is:

∂PD/∂Tj < 1/θJA

The left side is how much extra power is generated per degree of temperature rise; the right side is how much extra power the package can remove per degree. Two things help: making ∂PD/∂Tj small (a stable bias circuit, so IC hardly changes with temperature) and making θJA small (a better heat path, such as a heat sink).

Key points

  • Removed heat rises by 1/θJA watts per °C.
  • Stable only if ∂PD/∂Tj < 1/θJA.
  • Make ∂PD/∂Tj small: a stable bias circuit.
  • Make θJA small: a better heat path, such as a heat sink.

The half-supply rule

In a circuit with a resistance R in the collector and emitter path (R = RC + RE), the transistor voltage is VCE = VCC − IC·R, and the transistor power is:

PD = VCE·IC = VCC·IC − IC2·R

This power is largest when VCE = VCC/2. What happens when IC increases depends on which side of that point the Q-point is:

  • If VCE < VCC/2, an increase in IC makes VCE fall so much that PD DECREASES. The power drop opposes the temperature rise, so runaway cannot start.
  • If VCE > VCC/2, an increase in IC makes PD INCREASE, which can feed the runaway loop.

So a Q-point with VCE below half the supply is inherently safe from thermal runaway. A circuit with no collector or emitter resistance (R = 0) has no such protection, which is why power transistors are most at risk.

Key points

  • PD = VCC·IC − IC2·R peaks at VCE = VCC/2.
  • VCE < VCC/2: more IC means less PD, so runaway cannot start.
  • VCE > VCC/2: more IC means more PD, which feeds the loop.
  • R = 0 gives no protection: power transistors are most at risk.

Preventing thermal runaway

Thermal runaway is prevented by attacking both sides of the loop:

  1. USE A STABLE BIAS CIRCUIT: voltage divider bias with an emitter resistor keeps IC almost constant as temperature changes (small stability factor S). Fixed bias, with S = 1 + β, is the most vulnerable.
  2. ADD EMITTER RESISTANCE: even a small RE gives negative feedback against current increases.
  3. IMPROVE COOLING: a HEAT SINK lowers the thermal resistance from the case to the air. The total is θJA = θJC + θCS + θSA (junction-to-case + case-to-sink + sink-to-air). A larger heat sink, thermal paste and airflow all reduce θJA.
  4. USE COMPENSATION: temperature-sensitive parts such as diodes and thermistors can cancel the drift (topic 6.7).
  5. DESIGN THE Q-POINT with VCE below VCC/2 where possible, and keep PD well below the maximum rating at the highest expected ambient temperature (derating).

Key points

  • Stable bias (voltage divider + RE, small S); fixed bias (S = 1 + β) is the most vulnerable.
  • Better cooling: θJA = θJC + θCS + θSA, reduced by a heat sink, paste and airflow.
  • Compensation with diodes or thermistors (topic 6.7).
  • VCE below VCC/2 where possible, and derate PD at high ambient temperature.

Where it happens in practice

Thermal runaway is most dangerous in POWER transistors, such as audio power amplifier output stages, motor drivers and linear regulators, because they carry large currents, dissipate a lot of power and often have little resistance in the collector or emitter path. Small-signal transistors in properly biased amplifiers rarely suffer from it.

In push-pull audio output stages, the bias diodes or bias transistor are mounted on the same heat sink as the output transistors, so their voltage drops track the output transistors' temperature (a form of compensation). Datasheets give the maximum power dissipation at 25 °C and a DERATING factor (for example in mW/°C) that tells how much the allowed power must be reduced at higher temperatures.

Key points

  • Most dangerous in power transistors: output stages, motor drivers, linear regulators.
  • Properly biased small-signal stages rarely suffer from it.
  • Push-pull stages mount the bias diodes or transistor on the output heat sink.
  • Datasheets give PD(max) at 25 °C and a derating factor in mW/°C.

Summary

Collector current rises with temperature, power dissipation PD = VCE·IC raises the temperature, and the loop can run away and destroy the transistor. The junction is stable when ∂PD/∂Tj < 1/θJA. Runaway is prevented by stable biasing (voltage divider with RE), emitter resistance, heat sinks (low θJA), compensation, keeping VCE below VCC/2, and derating at high temperature.

Key formulas

  • PD = VCE·IC
  • Tj = TA + θJA·PD
  • θJA = θJC + θCS + θSA
  • Stability condition: ∂PD/∂Tj < 1/θJA
  • No runaway if VCE < VCC/2

Key points

  • Tj ↑ → IC ↑ → PD ↑ → Tj ↑ can run away and destroy the transistor.
  • Stable when ∂PD/∂Tj < 1/θJA.
  • Brakes: stable bias, RE, heat sink, compensation, VCE < VCC/2.
  • Derate the power rating at high temperature.
