TOPIC 6.7

Bias Compensation Using Diodes and Thermistors

Fight temperature with temperature: parts that drift the opposite way

Stabilization vs compensation

There are two different ways to keep the Q-point steady as temperature changes:

STABILIZATION uses negative feedback with ordinary resistors. When IC drifts, the circuit senses it (for example through the voltage across RE) and pushes it back. Topics 6.3 and 6.4 are stabilization techniques. They reduce the drift but cannot remove it completely.

COMPENSATION uses components whose own properties change with temperature in a way that CANCELS the transistor's changes. Diodes and thermistors are the usual compensating components. Compensation can remove a particular drift (such as the fall in VBE) much more completely.

In practice, good circuits use both: stabilization for overall robustness, compensation to cancel the main temperature effect.

Key points

  • Stabilization: negative feedback with resistors (topics 6.3, 6.4); reduces drift but cannot remove it.
  • Compensation: parts whose temperature changes cancel the transistor's changes.
  • Diodes and thermistors are the usual compensating components.
  • Good circuits use both.

Diode compensation for VBE

In voltage divider bias, the emitter current is IE ≈ (VB − VBE) / RE. When temperature rises, VBE falls (about 2.5 mV/°C) while VB stays fixed, so VE = VB − VBE rises and IE increases. This is the drift measured by S′ in topic 6.6.

The fix is to put a forward-biased DIODE D in series with R2 in the lower leg of the divider. The diode is made of the same material as the transistor, so its forward voltage VD falls with temperature at the same rate as VBE.

Now the base voltage includes the diode voltage: VB = VR2 + VD. When the temperature rises, VD falls by the same amount as VBE. So VB falls by exactly as much as VBE, and the emitter voltage VE = VB − VBE does NOT change. The emitter current stays constant.

For best tracking, the diode should be thermally coupled to the transistor (mounted close to it or on the same heat sink), so both are at the same temperature.

Key points

  • Without compensation, VBE falls, VE = VB − VBE rises and IE grows.
  • A forward-biased diode in series with R2: VB = VR2 + VD.
  • VD falls with VBE, so VE and IE stay (nearly) constant.
  • The diode must be thermally coupled to the transistor.

Diode compensation for ICO (leakage)

In germanium transistors and in high-leakage circuits, the main problem is the leakage ICO. A REVERSE-BIASED diode can compensate it.

Circuit: a resistor R1 feeds a current I from VCC to the base node, and a diode D is connected from the base to ground, reverse biased (cathode at the base). The current I is nearly constant: I ≈ (VCC − VBE) / R1. The diode takes its reverse saturation current I0 from the base node, so the base current is IB = I − I0.

The collector current becomes:

IC = β·(I − I0) + (β + 1)·ICO ≈ β·I + β·(ICO − I0)

If the diode is made of the same material as the transistor and has a matching reverse saturation current, then I0 ≈ ICO, and both rise together with temperature. Their effects cancel, leaving IC ≈ β·I, which is independent of temperature-dependent leakage.

Key points

  • For high leakage (germanium, power devices) use a reverse-biased diode from base to ground.
  • I ≈ (VCC − VBE)/R1; the diode removes I0, so IB = I − I0.
  • IC = β(I − I0) + (β + 1)ICO ≈ β·I when I0 ≈ ICO.
  • Matched material makes I0 and ICO rise together and cancel.

Thermistor compensation

A THERMISTOR is a resistor whose resistance changes strongly with temperature.

  • An NTC thermistor (negative temperature coefficient) has a resistance that DECREASES as temperature rises.
  • A PTC thermistor or SENSISTOR (positive temperature coefficient) has a resistance that INCREASES as temperature rises.

They can be placed in the bias network so that their change cancels the rise in IC:

  1. NTC in the lower leg of the divider (in place of R2, or in parallel with it): as temperature rises, the lower leg's resistance falls, so VB FALLS. This lowers VE and IE, cancelling the natural increase in IC.
  2. PTC (sensistor) as the emitter resistor: as temperature rises, RE increases, so IE = VE/RE DECREASES, again cancelling the increase.

The position matters: an NTC in the UPPER leg (in place of R1) would make VB rise with temperature and make the drift worse. The thermistor's value and temperature coefficient must be chosen so its effect matches the transistor's drift over the expected temperature range; a fixed resistor is often added in series or parallel to shape its response.

Key points

  • NTC: resistance falls with temperature; PTC (sensistor): resistance rises.
  • NTC in the lower leg makes VB fall; PTC as RE makes IE = VE/RE fall.
  • NTC in the upper leg makes VB rise: drift gets worse.
  • Value and coefficient must match the drift; fixed resistors shape the response.

Compensation in real circuits

The most common practical use of diode compensation is in PUSH-PULL (class AB) audio power amplifier output stages. Two diodes (or a transistor "VBE multiplier") set a small bias voltage across the bases of the two output transistors, just enough to keep them slightly on and avoid crossover distortion. The diodes are mounted on the same heat sink as the output transistors. When the output transistors heat up and their VBE falls, the diodes' voltage falls by the same amount, so the bias current stays steady instead of running away.

Thermistors are used in a similar way in power supplies, battery chargers and motor drives to track and correct temperature effects.

Key points

  • Class AB push-pull: two diodes (or a VBE multiplier) bias the output bases.
  • A small bias keeps both transistors slightly on: no crossover distortion.
  • Diodes on the same heat sink track VBE, so the idle current stays steady.
  • Thermistors do similar jobs in supplies, chargers and motor drives.

Stabilization and compensation compared

Stabilization (feedback with resistors):

  • works against all causes of drift (β, VBE, ICO) together;
  • cannot remove drift completely; it reduces it by the stability factor;
  • may reduce AC gain unless capacitors are used.

Compensation (diodes, thermistors):

  • cancels a specific temperature effect very precisely when well matched;
  • needs matched components and good thermal coupling;
  • does not correct for β differences between devices.

Best practice: use voltage divider bias with RE for stabilization, and add diode or thermistor compensation where temperature drift must be very small, as in power amplifiers.

Key points

  • Stabilization fights all causes together but only reduces drift.
  • Compensation cancels one effect precisely but needs matching and thermal coupling.
  • Compensation does not fix β spread; stabilization does.
  • Best: voltage divider + RE, plus compensation where drift must be tiny.

Summary

Compensation cancels temperature drift using components that change with temperature in the opposite direction. A forward-biased diode in the lower leg of the divider cancels the fall of VBE. A reverse-biased diode cancels the leakage ICO. An NTC thermistor in the lower leg, or a PTC as RE, cancels the rise in IC. Compensation works best with matched, thermally coupled components, and is combined with stabilization in good designs.

Key formulas

  • VE = VB − VBE (diode in lower leg keeps VE constant since ΔVD = ΔVBE)
  • Reverse diode: IC = β(I − I0) + (β + 1)ICO ≈ βI when I0 ≈ ICO
  • NTC: RT = R25·exp(B(1/T − 1/298 K))
  • ΔVBE ≈ −2.5 mV/°C

Key points

  • Forward diode in the lower leg cancels the VBE fall.
  • Reverse-biased diode cancels the leakage ICO.
  • NTC in the lower leg or PTC as RE cancels the rise in IC.
  • Needs matched, thermally coupled parts; combine with stabilization.
