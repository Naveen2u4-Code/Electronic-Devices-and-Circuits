TOPIC 6.6

Stability Factors (S, S′, S″)

Three numbers that tell how far the collector current drifts, and why

What does "stability" mean?

The Q-point of a transistor drifts mainly because three transistor quantities change with temperature or from one device to another:

  1. the reverse leakage current ICO (that is, ICBO), which roughly doubles every 10 °C;
  2. the base-emitter voltage VBE, which falls about 2.5 mV for every 1 °C rise;
  3. the current gain β, which varies widely between devices and increases with temperature.

To compare bias circuits fairly, we need numbers that tell how sensitive the collector current IC is to each of these. These numbers are the STABILITY FACTORS. A smaller stability factor means a more stable circuit.

Key points

  • Three causes of Q-point drift: ICO (doubles every 10 °C), VBE (−2.5 mV/°C) and β.
  • β also varies widely from one device to another.
  • Stability factors measure how sensitive IC is to each cause.
  • Smaller stability factor = more stable circuit.

Stability factor S (for ICO)

The stability factor S measures how much IC changes for a change in leakage current ICO, with VBE and β held constant:

S = ∂IC / ∂ICO ≈ ΔIC / ΔICO

The general form for any bias circuit is:

S = (1 + β) / (1 − β·∂IB/∂IC)

The term ∂IB/∂IC describes the feedback: in a good bias circuit, an increase in IC automatically reduces IB, so ∂IB/∂IC is negative and S becomes smaller.

  • With no feedback (fixed bias), ∂IB/∂IC = 0 and S = 1 + β, the largest possible value.
  • The ideal value is S = 1, which would mean IC changes only by the leakage itself.

Formulas for the three circuits:

  • Fixed bias: S = 1 + β
  • Collector-to-base bias: S = (1 + β) / [1 + β·RC/(RC + RB)]
  • Voltage divider bias: S = (1 + β)·(RTH + RE) / [RTH + (1 + β)·RE]

For the example circuits with β = 100: fixed bias S = 101, collector-to-base S ≈ 48, voltage divider S ≈ 7.3.

Key points

  • S = ∂IC/∂ICO = (1 + β)/(1 − β·∂IB/∂IC).
  • Feedback makes ∂IB/∂IC negative and S smaller; ideal S = 1, worst S = 1 + β.
  • Examples at β = 100: 101 (fixed), ≈ 48 (collector-to-base), ≈ 7.3 (voltage divider).
  • Voltage divider S approaches 1 + RTH/RE for large β.

Stability factor S′ (for VBE)

The stability factor S′ measures how much IC changes for a change in VBE, with ICO and β held constant:

S′ = ∂IC / ∂VBE ≈ ΔIC / ΔVBE

It is negative, because a lower VBE allows more current. Since VBE falls with temperature, ΔVBE is negative and the product S′·ΔVBE is a positive increase in IC.

Formulas:

  • Fixed bias: S′ = −β / RB
  • Collector-to-base bias: S′ ≈ −β / [RB + (1 + β)·RC]
  • Voltage divider bias: S′ = −β / [RTH + (1 + β)·RE] ≈ −1/RE for large β

Example values: fixed bias ≈ −0.26 mA/V, collector-to-base ≈ −0.26 mA/V, voltage divider ≈ −0.93 mA/V.

Notice that voltage divider bias is MORE sensitive to VBE in absolute terms. The reason: in fixed bias, VBE (0.7 V) is tiny compared with the 11.3 V across RB, so a small change in VBE hardly matters. In voltage divider bias, VBE is compared with the much smaller voltage across RE (about 1.4 V). This is why designers make the voltage across RE reasonably large, and why diode compensation (topic 6.7) is used to cancel VBE drift.

Key points

  • S′ = ∂IC/∂VBE is negative; with VBE falling, S′·ΔVBE raises IC.
  • Examples: −0.26, −0.26 and −0.93 mA/V; voltage divider S′ ≈ −1/RE.
  • Voltage divider is most VBE-sensitive: VBE is compared with only ≈ 1.4 V across RE.
  • Remedies: a reasonably large voltage across RE, or diode compensation (6.7).

Stability factor S″ (for β)

The stability factor S″ measures how much IC changes for a change in β, with ICO and VBE held constant:

S″ = ∂IC / ∂β ≈ ΔIC / Δβ

Because β can change by a large amount (for example from 100 to 200 when a transistor is replaced), the change is usually calculated from β1 (old value, giving IC1) to β2 (new value):

  • Fixed bias: S″ = IC1 / β1 (= IB)
  • Collector-to-base bias: S″ = IC1·(RB + RC) / [β1·(RB + RC·(1 + β2))]
  • Voltage divider bias: S″ = IC1·(1 + RTH/RE) / [β1·(1 + β2 + RTH/RE)]

For β changing from 100 to 200 in the example circuits:

  • Fixed bias: S″ ≈ 29 µA per unit of β, so ΔIC ≈ 2.9 mA (IC doubles).
  • Collector-to-base: S″ ≈ 9.3 µA, so ΔIC ≈ 0.93 mA.
  • Voltage divider: S″ ≈ 0.48 µA, so ΔIC ≈ 0.048 mA (about 4%).

Key points

  • S″ = ∂IC/∂β, usually taken from β1 to β2 since β changes a lot.
  • Fixed bias S″ = IC1/β1 = IB: β 100 → 200 doubles IC.
  • Collector-to-base: ≈ 9.3 µA, ΔIC ≈ 0.93 mA.
  • Voltage divider: ≈ 0.48 µA, ΔIC ≈ 0.048 mA (about 4%).

Putting it together

When the temperature changes, all three quantities change at once. The total change in collector current is approximately:

ΔIC = S·ΔICO + S′·ΔVBE + S″·Δβ

Example: temperature rises from 25 °C to 75 °C. ICO rises from 10 nA to 320 nA (ΔICO ≈ 0.31 µA), VBE falls by 0.125 V, and β rises from 100 to 125.

  • Fixed bias: about 31 µA + 32 µA + 725 µA ≈ 0.79 mA, roughly +27% of its 2.9 mA.
  • Voltage divider bias: about 2.3 µA + 116 µA + 19 µA ≈ 0.14 mA, roughly +11% of its 1.29 mA.

For small-signal silicon transistors, leakage is tiny, so the β and VBE terms usually dominate. For germanium transistors and power transistors, leakage is much larger and S becomes more important.

Key points

  • ΔIC = S·ΔICO + S′·ΔVBE + S″·Δβ.
  • 25 → 75 °C: fixed bias ≈ 0.79 mA (+27%), voltage divider ≈ 0.14 mA (+11%).
  • Small-signal silicon: the β and VBE terms dominate.
  • Germanium and power transistors: large leakage makes S important.

Comparison of bias circuits

Comparing the three circuits with the same transistor:

  • FIXED BIAS: S = 1 + β (worst), strongly β-dependent. Simple; used mainly for switching.
  • COLLECTOR-TO-BASE BIAS: S about half of fixed bias in the example; better, and never saturates deeply. Feedback also reduces AC gain.
  • VOLTAGE DIVIDER BIAS: S close to 1 + RTH/RE (small), almost independent of β. The standard choice for amplifiers. Its VBE sensitivity is reduced by keeping a reasonable voltage across RE, or by compensation.

Design rule: for good stability, keep RTH/RE small (for example RTH ≤ 10·RE), while keeping the divider from drawing too much current or loading the signal.

Key points

  • Fixed bias: S = 1 + β, strongly β-dependent; mainly for switching.
  • Collector-to-base: S about half of fixed bias; no deep saturation; lower AC gain.
  • Voltage divider: S ≈ 1 + RTH/RE, almost independent of β; the amplifier standard.
  • Design rule: RTH ≤ 10·RE, without a power-hungry or loading divider.

Summary

Stability factors measure how sensitive the collector current is to the three causes of drift: S = ∂IC/∂ICO, S′ = ∂IC/∂VBE, S″ = ∂IC/∂β. Smaller magnitudes mean better stability; S ranges from 1 (ideal) to 1 + β (fixed bias). The total drift is ΔIC = S·ΔICO + S′·ΔVBE + S″·Δβ. Voltage divider bias gives the smallest S and S″, which is why it is the standard amplifier bias.

Key formulas

  • S = ∂IC/∂ICO; S′ = ∂IC/∂VBE; S″ = ∂IC/∂β
  • General: S = (1 + β)/(1 − β·∂IB/∂IC)
  • ΔIC = S·ΔICO + S′·ΔVBE + S″·Δβ
  • Fixed bias: S = 1 + β
  • Collector-to-base: S = (1 + β)/[1 + β·RC/(RC + RB)]
  • Voltage divider: S = (1 + β)(RTH + RE)/[RTH + (1 + β)RE]

Key points

  • S, S′, S″: sensitivity of IC to ICO, VBE and β.
  • S runs from 1 (ideal) to 1 + β (fixed bias).
  • Total drift: ΔIC = S·ΔICO + S′·ΔVBE + S″·Δβ.
  • Voltage divider bias: smallest S and S″, the standard amplifier bias.
