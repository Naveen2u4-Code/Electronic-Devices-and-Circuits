TOPIC 6.4

Voltage Divider Bias

Two resistors fix the base voltage, one resistor fixes the current

The circuit

VOLTAGE DIVIDER BIAS is the most widely used biasing method for transistor amplifiers. It is also called self-bias or emitter bias. It has four resistors:

  • R1 and R2 form a VOLTAGE DIVIDER across the supply, setting a fixed DC voltage at the base;
  • RC, the collector resistor, from VCC to the collector;
  • RE, an EMITTER RESISTOR, from the emitter to ground.

The key new part is RE. The divider sets the base voltage, and RE then sets the emitter current. As we will see, this makes the Q-point almost independent of β.

Key points

  • Four resistors: R1, R2 (the divider), RC and RE.
  • The divider fixes the DC voltage at the base.
  • RE from emitter to ground then sets the emitter current.
  • Result: a Q-point almost independent of β; the most widely used bias.

Quick analysis (approximate method)

If the current through the divider is much larger than the base current (a "stiff" divider), the base current hardly loads the divider, and the base voltage is simply:

VB ≈ VCC · R2 / (R1 + R2)

The emitter is one VBE below the base:

VE = VB − VBE

The emitter current is set by Ohm's law across RE:

IE = VE / RE, and IC ≈ IE.

Finally:

VCE ≈ VCC − IC·(RC + RE)

Example (VCC = 12 V, R1 = 39 kΩ, R2 = 8.2 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, RC = 3.3 kΩ):

  • VB ≈ 12 × 8.2 / 47.2 ≈ 2.08 V
  • VE ≈ 2.08 − 0.7 ≈ 1.38 V
  • IE ≈ 1.38 V / 1 kΩ ≈ 1.38 mA, so IC ≈ 1.38 mA
  • VCE ≈ 12 − 1.38 mA × 4.3 kΩ ≈ 6.1 V

Notice that β does not appear anywhere in this calculation. That is the main strength of the circuit.

A common design rule for a stiff divider is β·RE ≥ 10·R2. Here 100 × 1 kΩ = 100 kΩ, which is more than 10 × 8.2 kΩ = 82 kΩ, so the rule is met.

Key points

  • Stiff divider: VB ≈ VCC·R2/(R1 + R2).
  • VE = VB − 0.7 V, IE = VE/RE ≈ IC, VCE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE).
  • Example: 2.08 V, 1.38 V, 1.38 mA, about 6.1 V; β never appears.
  • Stiff-divider rule: β·RE ≥ 10·R2.

Exact analysis (Thevenin method)

For an exact result, replace the divider by its THEVENIN EQUIVALENT as seen from the base:

VTH = VCC · R2 / (R1 + R2)

RTH = R1 ∥ R2 = R1·R2 / (R1 + R2)

The base circuit is now a source VTH with series resistance RTH driving the base. The base loop gives:

VTH = IB·RTH + VBE + IE·RE, with IE = (β + 1)·IB

IB = (VTH − VBE) / [RTH + (β + 1)·RE]

Then IC = β·IB and VCE = VCC − IC·RC − IE·RE.

Example: VTH ≈ 2.085 V, RTH ≈ 6.78 kΩ.
IB = 1.385 / (6.78 kΩ + 101 kΩ) ≈ 12.9 µA, IC ≈ 1.29 mA, VCE ≈ 12 − 1.29 × 3.3 − 1.30 × 1 ≈ 6.5 V.

The exact IC (1.29 mA) is close to the approximate value (1.38 mA). The approximation is good when (β + 1)·RE is much larger than RTH.

Key points

  • Replace the divider by VTH = VCC·R2/(R1 + R2) and RTH = R1 ∥ R2.
  • Base loop: IB = (VTH − VBE)/[RTH + (β + 1)RE].
  • Example: IB ≈ 12.9 µA, IC ≈ 1.29 mA, VCE ≈ 6.5 V.
  • The approximation is good when (β + 1)·RE ≫ RTH.

Why it is so stable: emitter feedback

The emitter resistor provides negative feedback. Suppose IC tries to rise (higher temperature or higher β):

  1. IC, and therefore IE, increases.
  2. The voltage across RE, VE = IE·RE, increases.
  3. The base voltage VB is held almost fixed by the divider, so VBE = VB − VE DECREASES.
  4. A smaller VBE reduces the base current IB.
  5. A smaller IB pulls IC back down.

Any change in IC is opposed automatically. And because VB is fixed by resistors, not by the transistor, the final IC is set mainly by VB and RE, not by β.

Key points

  • IC ↑ → VE ↑ → VBE ↓ → IB ↓ → IC ↓.
  • RE gives negative feedback: any change in IC is opposed.
  • VB is held by the resistors, not by the transistor.
  • The final IC is set mainly by VB and RE, not by β.

Comparing the three bias circuits

When β changes from 100 to 200:

  • FIXED BIAS: IC 2.90 → 5.80 mA (+100%).
  • COLLECTOR-TO-BASE BIAS: IC 2.96 → 3.88 mA (+31%).
  • VOLTAGE DIVIDER BIAS: IC 1.29 → 1.33 mA (only about +4%).

The stability factor of voltage divider bias is:

S = (1 + β)·(RTH + RE) / [RTH + (1 + β)·RE]

For the example, S ≈ 101 × 7.78 / 107.8 ≈ 7.3, compared with 101 for fixed bias and about 48 for collector-to-base bias. For large β, S approaches 1 + RTH/RE, so the smaller RTH is compared with RE, the better the stability.

Key points

  • β 100 → 200: fixed +100%, collector-to-base +31%, voltage divider about +4%.
  • S = (1 + β)(RTH + RE)/[RTH + (1 + β)RE] ≈ 7.3 for the example.
  • Compare S = 101 (fixed) and about 48 (collector-to-base).
  • For large β, S → 1 + RTH/RE: keep RTH small compared with RE.

Design trade-offs and the bypass capacitor

Smaller R1 and R2 make RTH smaller and stability better, but they also draw more current from the supply (wasting power) and lower the input resistance of the amplifier, which loads the signal source. Designers balance these, typically choosing the divider current about 10 times the base current.

RE gives excellent DC stability, but it would also apply negative feedback to the signal and greatly reduce the voltage gain. The solution is a BYPASS CAPACITOR CE connected across RE. For DC, CE is an open circuit, so RE still stabilizes the Q-point. For the AC signal, CE is nearly a short circuit, so the emitter is at signal ground and the full gain is kept.

A typical design guideline is to put about one tenth of VCC across RE and about half of VCC across the transistor (VCE ≈ VCC/2).

Key points

  • Smaller R1, R2: better stability, but more supply current and lower input resistance.
  • Typical choice: divider current about 10 × IB.
  • CE across RE: open for DC (Q-point stays stable), short for AC (full gain).
  • Guideline: about VCC/10 across RE, VCE ≈ VCC/2.

Summary

Voltage divider bias uses R1 and R2 to fix the base voltage and RE to set the emitter current. Its Q-point is nearly independent of β, and negative feedback through RE keeps it stable against temperature. Approximate analysis: VB = VCC·R2/(R1 + R2), IE = (VB − VBE)/RE. Exact analysis uses the Thevenin equivalent. Its stability factor is the smallest of the three circuits, which is why it is the standard choice for amplifiers. A bypass capacitor across RE keeps the AC gain high.

Key formulas

  • VTH = VCC·R2/(R1 + R2)
  • RTH = R1·R2/(R1 + R2)
  • IB = (VTH − VBE)/[RTH + (β + 1)RE]
  • Approx. IE ≈ (VB − VBE)/RE
  • VCE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE)
  • S = (1 + β)(RTH + RE)/[RTH + (1 + β)RE]
  • Stiff divider: β·RE ≥ 10·R2

Key points

  • R1, R2 fix VB; RE sets IE; the Q-point is nearly independent of β.
  • Approximate method for quick work; the Thevenin method for exact values.
  • Smallest S of the three circuits (7.3 vs 48 vs 101 in the examples).
  • A bypass capacitor CE keeps the AC gain high.
