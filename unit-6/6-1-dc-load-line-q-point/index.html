TOPIC 6.1

Why Bias? The DC Load Line and Q-Point

Setting the resting point of a transistor, and why it has to stay there

Why does a transistor need biasing?

A transistor can amplify only when it works in the ACTIVE REGION: the base-emitter junction forward biased and the collector-base junction reverse biased. A signal, however, is AC: it swings both positive and negative. If we applied the signal alone, the transistor would be off for most of the cycle (a silicon transistor needs VBE of about 0.7 V before it conducts), and the output would be badly distorted.

BIASING means applying steady DC voltages and currents to the transistor, using resistors and the supply, so that with no signal present it already sits in the middle of the active region. The signal is then added on top of these DC values and moves the transistor a little above and below its resting point.

A good bias circuit must do two jobs:

  1. place the resting point correctly;
  2. keep it there, even when the transistor is replaced by another of the same type or the temperature changes.

The second job is called STABILIZATION, and it is the main theme of this unit.

Key points

  • Amplifying needs the active region: base-emitter forward biased, collector-base reverse biased.
  • A signal applied alone leaves a silicon transistor off below about 0.7 V, so the output is badly distorted.
  • Biasing sets steady DC values so the transistor rests mid-active; the signal rides on top.
  • A bias circuit must place the resting point and keep it there: that second job is stabilization.

The DC load line

In the common emitter circuit, the collector current IC flows from VCC through the collector resistor RC into the transistor. Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law to the collector loop gives:

VCC = IC·RC + VCE, so VCE = VCC − IC·RC.

This equation must be true no matter what the transistor does. Plotted on the output characteristics (IC against VCE), it is a straight line called the DC LOAD LINE. It is easy to draw from its two end points:

  • CUTOFF END (IC = 0): VCE = VCC. In the example, 12 V on the VCE axis.
  • SATURATION END (VCE = 0): IC = VCC / RC. In the example, 12 V / 2 kΩ = 6 mA on the IC axis.

The slope of the line is −1/RC. A larger RC makes the line flatter; a larger VCC moves the whole line outward.

Key points

  • KVL round the collector loop: VCE = VCC − IC·RC, whatever the transistor does.
  • Cutoff end (IC = 0) at VCE = VCC; saturation end (VCE = 0) at IC = VCC/RC.
  • Example: 12 V and 6 mA end points for VCC = 12 V, RC = 2 kΩ.
  • Slope −1/RC: larger RC flattens the line, larger VCC moves it outward.

The Q-point

The transistor's own behaviour is described by its output characteristics, one curve for each base current. The circuit's behaviour is described by the load line. The actual operating point must satisfy both, so it is where the load line CROSSES the output curve for the present base current.

This point is called the Q-POINT (quiescent point, meaning the resting point with no signal). It is described by two numbers: the collector current ICQ and the collector-emitter voltage VCEQ.

Example: with β = 100 and IB = 30 µA, IC = 100 × 30 µA = 3 mA, and VCE = 12 − 3 mA × 2 kΩ = 6 V. So the Q-point is (VCEQ = 6 V, ICQ = 3 mA), exactly in the middle of the load line.

As IB increases, the Q-point slides UP the load line toward saturation. As IB decreases, it slides DOWN toward cutoff.

Key points

  • The Q-point is where the load line crosses the output curve for the present IB.
  • It is described by two numbers: ICQ and VCEQ.
  • Example: β = 100, IB = 30 µA gives (6 V, 3 mA), the middle of the load line.
  • More IB slides Q up toward saturation; less IB slides it down toward cutoff.

Choosing where to put the Q-point

With a signal applied, the operating point swings up and down along the load line around the Q-point.

  • If the Q-point is too close to SATURATION, the upward swing in current hits the top of the line: the collector voltage cannot go below about 0.2 V, so the output waveform is clipped on that side.
  • If the Q-point is too close to CUTOFF, the downward swing hits IC = 0, and the output is clipped on the other side.
  • If the Q-point is near the MIDDLE of the load line (VCEQ ≈ VCC/2), the signal can swing equally in both directions, giving the largest possible undistorted output.

For small signals the Q-point can be anywhere well inside the active region. For large signals, the centre of the load line is the best choice. (With a load resistor connected through a coupling capacitor, the signal actually swings along a slightly steeper AC load line, covered in topic 5.9.)

Key points

  • Q too near saturation: vCE cannot go below about 0.2 V, so one side of the output clips.
  • Q too near cutoff: IC cannot go below 0, so the other side clips.
  • VCEQ ≈ VCC/2 allows equal swing both ways: the largest undistorted output.
  • Small signals: anywhere well inside the active region. Large signals: the centre of the load line.

Why the Q-point does not stay put

Setting the Q-point once is not enough. In real circuits it tends to move, for three main reasons:

  1. β VARIES: transistors of the same part number can have very different β values (for example anywhere from 100 to 300). Replacing the transistor can move the Q-point a long way.
  2. VBE FALLS WITH TEMPERATURE: for a fixed current, VBE decreases by about 2.5 mV for every 1 °C rise. With the same bias resistors, a lower VBE allows more base current to flow.
  3. LEAKAGE ICBO RISES WITH TEMPERATURE: it roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise, and in common emitter it appears multiplied as ICEO = (1 + β)·ICBO.

In addition, β itself increases with temperature. All of these effects push IC UP when the transistor gets hotter. If IC rises too far, the Q-point drifts toward saturation, the signal clips, and in extreme cases the transistor overheats (thermal runaway, topic 6.5).

The bias circuits in topics 6.2 to 6.4 differ mainly in how well they hold the Q-point steady against these changes.

Key points

  • β varies widely between transistors of the same type (for example 100 to 300).
  • VBE falls about 2.5 mV/°C; ICBO doubles every 10 °C and appears as ICEO = (1 + β)·ICBO; β also rises with temperature.
  • All of these push IC up, so the Q-point drifts toward saturation; at the extreme, thermal runaway.
  • Bias circuits (6.2 to 6.4) differ mainly in how well they hold Q steady.

Summary

Biasing sets the DC operating point so the transistor works in the active region with no signal. The DC load line VCE = VCC − IC·RC runs from (VCC, 0) to (0, VCC/RC). The Q-point (VCEQ, ICQ) is where the load line meets the transistor's output curve for the present base current. For maximum undistorted swing it is placed near the middle of the load line. Because β, VBE and ICBO change with device and temperature, a good bias circuit must also stabilize the Q-point.

Key formulas

  • VCE = VCC − IC·RC
  • IC(sat) ≈ VCC/RC
  • VCE(cutoff) = VCC
  • Best Q for maximum swing: VCEQ ≈ VCC/2
  • ΔVBE ≈ −2.5 mV/°C
  • ICBO doubles every 10 °C

Key points

  • Biasing puts the transistor in the active region with no signal present.
  • The DC load line runs from (VCC, 0) to (0, VCC/RC); Q is where it meets the present IB curve.
  • Place Q near the middle of the load line for the largest undistorted swing.
  • β, VBE and ICBO change with device and temperature, so the Q-point must be stabilized.
